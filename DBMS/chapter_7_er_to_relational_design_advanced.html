7.6 · Core conversion rules

1. Reduction of an E–R design to relational schemas

The E–R model describes the logical structure of an enterprise. A relational implementation represents that structure as relation schemas. In the textbook’s mapping approach, each entity set and relationship set initially yields a relation schema; primary-key and foreign-key constraints carry forward important E–R constraints.

1.1 Strong entity sets with simple attributes

Rule Create a relation schema with one attribute for each simple attribute of the strong entity set. The entity set’s primary key remains the relation’s primary key.

Example: student(ID, name, tot_cred), where ID is the primary key. One tuple represents one student entity.

The textbook’s example includes these schemas:

classroom(building, room_number, capacity)
department(dept_name, building, budget)
course(course_id, title, credits)
instructor(ID, name, salary)
student(ID, name, tot_cred)

Notice that instructor and student do not include dept_name at this stage: department membership is represented as a relationship in the conceptual design. See schema combination for when a relationship can later be folded into a table.

1.2 Strong entity sets with complex attributes

E–R attribute kindRelational mappingReason / example
CompositeReplace it with its component attributes; do not keep a separate column for the composite attribute.name becomes first_name, middle_name, last_name. A composite street can become street_number, street_name, apt_number.
MultivaluedCreate a separate relation containing the owner’s primary-key attributes plus the attribute’s value.instructor_phone(ID, phone_number).
DerivedNot explicitly represented as a stored relational attribute by this mapping.age can be computed from date_of_birth and the current date.

A relational schema for the complex instructor example, excluding the multivalued phone number, is:

instructor(
  ID, first_name, middle_name, last_name,
  street_number, street_name, apt_number,
  city, state, zip_code, date_of_birth
)
Multivalued attribute example If instructor 22222 has phone numbers 555-1234 and 555-4321, store two tuples in instructor_phone(ID, phone_number): (22222, 555-1234) and (22222, 555-4321). The primary key is the combination (ID, phone_number), and ID is a foreign key referencing instructor(ID).

For a multivalued attribute relation, the general primary key is the union of the owner’s primary-key attributes and the value attribute. The foreign key points back to the relation representing the owner.

Special time-slot case The textbook’s time_slot has key time_slot_id and one multivalued composite attribute (day, start_time, end_time). Its resulting relation is time_slot(time_slot_id, day, start_time, end_time). Because the source states that two meetings cannot share the same day and start time but end at different times, end_time is omitted from the key; the key is (time_slot_id, day, start_time). Since the entity relation would contain only time_slot_id, it can be dropped as an optimization. The textbook flags a consequence: a relation such as sec_time_slot can no longer reference a single row by time_slot_id, because that value can appear in multiple meeting tuples or have no tuple at all.

1.3 Weak entity sets

Rule A weak entity’s relation contains its own attributes plus the primary key of the strong entity on which it depends. The primary key is the strong entity’s key combined with the weak entity’s discriminator. A foreign key references the strong entity.

Example: a section depends on a course. The section discriminator is (sec_id, semester, year); course_id comes from the strong entity.

course(course_id, title, credits)
section(course_id, sec_id, semester, year)

PRIMARY KEY(section) =
  (course_id, sec_id, semester, year)
FOREIGN KEY(section.course_id) REFERENCES course(course_id)
ON DELETE CASCADE

The ON DELETE CASCADE rule means that deleting a course also deletes the sections that depend on it. See relationship mapping for the general foreign-key pattern.

1.4 Relationship sets

A relationship relation starts with the primary-key attributes of all participating entity sets. Any descriptive attributes of the relationship are added as well. Attribute names may need role prefixes when the same-named key comes from different entity sets; in recursive relationships, role names distinguish the roles.

Relationship cardinalityPrimary key of relationship relation
Binary many-to-manyUnion of the primary keys of both participating entity sets.
Binary one-to-onePrimary key of either entity set may be chosen.
Binary one-to-many / many-to-onePrimary key of the entity set on the many side.
N-ary, no arrow constraintsUnion of the primary keys of every participating entity set.
N-ary, with one arrowPrimary keys of the participating entity sets not on the arrow side, following the source’s convention of at most one arrow out of the relationship set.

For each participating entity set, add a foreign-key constraint from the relationship relation’s corresponding key attributes to the primary key of that entity’s relation.

University example schemas 
teaches(ID, course_id, sec_id, semester, year)
takes(ID, course_id, sec_id, semester, year, grade)
prereq(course_id, prereq_id)
advisor(s_ID, i_ID)
sec_course(course_id, sec_id, semester, year)
sec_time_slot(course_id, sec_id, semester, year, time_slot_id)
sec_class(course_id, sec_id, semester, year, building, room_number)
inst_dept(ID, dept_name)
stud_dept(ID, dept_name)
course_dept(course_id, dept_name)

The advisor example uses s_ID and i_ID to distinguish student ID from instructor ID. Under the source’s assumption that each student has at most one advisor, s_ID is the primary key; both attributes are foreign keys. For prereq, the role indicators course_id and prereq_id distinguish the course from its prerequisite.

1.5 Remove redundant schemas and combine schemas carefully

Redundant relationship schema

The relation for an identifying relationship between a weak entity and its strong owner is usually redundant when it has no descriptive attributes and is many-to-one. The weak entity relation already contains the owner key plus its discriminator.

Combine a many-to-one relationship

If every entity on the many side participates (total participation), the relationship relation can be merged into the many-side entity relation. Use the many-side primary key, and retain the foreign key to the other entity.

Examples from the chapter: inst_dept can be merged into instructor, stud_dept into student, course_dept into course, and sec_class and sec_time_slot into section when the stated constraints permit these combinations.

For a one-to-one relationship, the relationship schema may be merged with either participating entity schema. If participation is partial, merging is still possible but may require NULL values for entities with no associated counterpart.

Remember the foreign-key adjustment When a relationship schema is merged into one of its entity schemas, remove the foreign-key constraint that would reference that merged entity relation from the relationship relation (because that separate relation disappears) and preserve the foreign key to the other entity set. In the example, merging inst_dept into instructor leaves instructor.dept_name referencing department.
7.7 · Modeling decisions

2. E–R design issues: choosing the right representation

There is rarely only one way to describe a real-world enterprise. Decide whether something should be an attribute, entity set, or relationship set by considering its meaning, the constraints that apply, and the extra information the database must preserve.

2.1 Entity set versus attribute

ChoiceWhat it expressesExample / when appropriate
Phone number as a simple attributeOne value on each instructor.Appropriate only when one phone number per instructor is enough.
Phone number as a multivalued attributeZero or more phone numbers per instructor.Use when multiple values matter but phone-specific details do not.
phone as an entity set plus inst_phonePhones can have their own properties and relationships.Useful for location, type (mobile/IP/landline), or sharing a phone among people.

The textbook treats name as a natural attribute of an instructor rather than an independent entity. The distinction depends on the real-world semantics; there is no universal rule that every noun should become an entity.

Common modeling mistake Do not model a related entity’s primary key as an ordinary attribute when the association itself should be explicit. For example, do not put student ID inside instructor to represent advising; model the advisor relationship. Likewise, do not list the participating entities’ keys as descriptive attributes of the E–R relationship—their identity is already implicit in the relationship. Those key attributes may appear later in the generated relational schema.

2.2 Entity set versus relationship set

In the university model, takes compactly represents a student taking a section. An alternative is an entity set registration, with relationships linking each registration to one student and one section.

A useful starting guideline is to use a relationship set to describe an action or association occurring between entities. Promote the association to an entity when it needs its own details, identity, or further relationships.

2.3 Binary versus n-ary relationships

Some nonbinary relationships can be replaced with binary relationships without losing useful meaning. The parent example can use separate mother and father relationships so a child’s mother can be recorded even when the father is unknown, instead of leaving part of a ternary parent relationship incomplete.

More generally, a ternary relationship R(A,B,C) can be transformed into a new entity set E plus three binary relationships R_A, R_B, and R_C. Each original tuple becomes an E entity; relationship attributes move to E, and an identifying attribute is needed.

Why not always split? The new entity plus relationships increase complexity and storage. A ternary relationship makes it explicit that all participating entities form one association, and constraints on the original n-ary relationship may not be expressible by cardinality constraints on the binary replacements.

The chapter’s proj_guide(instructor, student, project) example is important: separate instructor–project and instructor–student links cannot show exactly which student is guided by which instructor on which project. Preserve the ternary relationship unless a replacement can retain the required combinations and constraints.

2.4 Where should relationship attributes go?

CardinalityPlacement ruleAdvisor-date example
One-to-manyA relationship attribute can be placed on the entity set on the many side.If one instructor advises many students and each student has at most one advisor, date could be stored with student.
One-to-oneThe attribute may be placed on either participating entity.The associated date can be stored on either side if the one-to-one assumptions hold.
Many-to-manyKeep it on the relationship when its value depends on a particular pair of participating entities.The date that a particular instructor became a particular student’s advisor belongs to advisor, not solely to either entity.

Even when moving a one-to-many relationship attribute is equivalent under the constraints, keeping it on the relationship may communicate its semantics more clearly—for example, the advising date is not the student’s university admission date.

7.8 · Extended E–R model

3. Specialization, generalization, and inheritance

Extended E–R (EER) features express distinctions that the basic entity–relationship constructs can represent less naturally. The central idea is a higher-level entity set with shared properties and lower-level entity sets with additional, distinctive properties.

3.1 Specialization and generalization

ConceptDesign directionTextbook example
SpecializationTop-down: refine a broad entity set into subgroups distinguished by attributes or roles.person → employee, student; employee → instructor, secretary.
GeneralizationBottom-up: combine entity sets that share attributes and relationships into a common higher-level entity set.instructor and secretary can share common ID/name/salary properties in employee.

The resulting structure is a superclass–subclass relationship, commonly called ISA (“is a”). An instructor ISA employee; employee ISA person. A person may be an employee, a student, both, or neither unless constraints state otherwise.

Attribute and relationship inheritance A lower-level entity inherits the attributes of its higher-level entity sets and inherits participation in their relationship sets. In the hierarchy above, an instructor inherits ID, name, and address from person, plus salary from employee, and adds rank.

A hierarchy gives each entity set at most one immediate superclass (single inheritance). If an entity set is a lower-level set in more than one ISA relationship, the structure has multiple inheritance and is called a lattice.

3.2 Membership and generalization constraints

Constraint dimensionOptionMeaningSource example
How membership is decidedCondition-definedMembership follows a predicate, often an attribute condition; when the same attribute determines membership, it is attribute-defined.student_type = 'graduate' places a student in graduate_student.
How membership is decidedUser-definedA user explicitly assigns an entity; no automatic membership condition controls it.Employees are assigned to work teams after three months.
Can an entity belong to multiple lower-level sets?DisjointAn entity belongs to at most one lower-level set within the generalization.A student is graduate or undergraduate, not both.
Can an entity belong to multiple lower-level sets?OverlappingAn entity may belong to more than one lower-level set.A person can be both student and employee; an employee may be on multiple teams.
Must every higher-level entity be classified?TotalEvery higher-level entity belongs to at least one lower-level set.Every student is either graduate or undergraduate.
Must every higher-level entity be classified?PartialSome higher-level entities may belong to no lower-level set. Partial is the default in the source.An employee may not yet belong to a work team.
Do not confuse the two dimensions Disjointness asks whether one entity may belong to multiple subclasses. Completeness asks whether every superclass entity must belong to some subclass. They are independent: a design may be total-disjoint, partial-disjoint, total-overlapping, or partial-overlapping.

These constraints have operational consequences. With total completeness, inserting a higher-level entity requires inserting it into at least one lower-level set. Condition-defined membership requires all entities satisfying the condition to be included. Deleting a higher-level entity also deletes its associated lower-level representations.

3.3 Mapping generalization into relations

The source describes two approaches. The first is the more general method and avoids the main representational problems of the alternative.

  1. Superclass plus subclass relations. Create a relation for the higher-level entity and one for each lower-level entity. The subclass relation contains its own attributes plus the superclass primary key, which is also its primary key and a foreign key to the superclass relation.
  2. Subclass-only relations. If the generalization is both disjoint and complete, omit the superclass relation and place the superclass attributes in each subclass relation. Each subclass uses the superclass key as its key.
-- Method 1: superclass plus subclasses
person(ID, name, street, city)
employee(ID, salary)       -- ID PK and FK → person(ID)
student(ID, tot_cred)      -- ID PK and FK → person(ID)

-- Method 2: disjoint + complete only
employee(ID, name, street, city, salary)
student(ID, name, street, city, tot_cred)

The subclass-only method has drawbacks: foreign keys referring to the superclass have no single superclass relation to target; overlapping subclasses duplicate shared attributes; and partial generalization leaves higher-level entities that fit no subclass unrepresented. Adding a separate superclass relation to address these issues leads back toward method 1.

7.8.5–7.8.6 · Relating relationships

4. Aggregation

Definition Aggregation is an abstraction in which a relationship set, together with its participating entity sets, is treated as a higher-level entity that can participate in another relationship.

Basic E–R constructs do not directly express a relationship between relationships. In the source example, proj_guide connects instructor, student, and project. Each particular guiding combination may have a monthly evaluation report, represented by evaluation.

A four-way relationship linking instructor, student, project, and evaluation could represent the combination, but it creates redundant information: every evaluation combination must already be a valid proj_guide combination. The relationships should not simply be collapsed, because some guidance combinations may not yet have an evaluation. A multivalued attribute is also unsuitable when an evaluation is an entity that may relate to other entities, such as a secretary processing it.

Mapping aggregation to relations

Map the relationship set that links the aggregation to another entity using the usual relationship-set rules. Its schema includes the primary-key attributes of the aggregated relationship’s participants, the primary key of the other entity, and any descriptive attributes of the linking relationship. The aggregation’s primary key is the primary key of its defining relationship; no extra relation is required solely for the aggregation itself. Continue mapping the entities and relationships inside the aggregation using the usual rules.

7.9 · Diagram languages

5. Alternative E–R notation and UML

Diagram notation supports communication between data-modeling specialists and domain experts. Different textbooks and tools may use different symbols, so interpret the legend rather than assuming every diagram follows the same convention.

Notation / constructTypical representationUseful distinction
Textbook E–R notation used in the sourceEntity-set rectangles with attributes inside; relationship diamonds; underlined key attributes; double lines for total participation; double diamonds for identifying relationships.Compact layout; relationship attributes are linked to the relationship with a dashed line.
Chen’s notationAttributes shown in ovals attached to entity rectangles or relationship diamonds; key attributes underlined.Recognizable original-style E–R notation.
Crow’s-foot notation / IDEF1X familyRelationships represented as lines; crow’s feet indicate “many”; bars and circles help express participation.In the described alternative, a crow’s foot on both ends indicates many-to-many; on one end indicates many-to-one in the corresponding direction. 5.1 UML in relation to E–R diagrams

The Unified Modeling Language (UML), developed under the Object Management Group (OMG), models several aspects of a software system, not only data. The source lists:

  • Class diagrams: structure of classes and their relationships.
  • Use-case diagrams: interactions between users and the system, such as withdrawing money or registering for a course.
  • Activity diagrams: flow of tasks among system components.
  • Implementation diagrams: software and hardware components and their interconnections.

UML objects resemble E–R entities but additionally expose methods. UML class diagrams can show attributes and methods, using + for public, - for private, and # for protected access. UML derived attributes correspond to methods requiring no parameters; UML does not directly support E–R composite or multivalued attributes in the same way.

A notation trap The placement of cardinality bounds on UML association edges is the reverse of the placement described for the textbook’s E–R diagrams. In UML, a bound shown near one class describes how many instances at that end may be associated with one instance at the opposite end. The source’s example states that 0..* on the E2 side and 0..1 on the E1 side means many-to-one from E2 to E1.

Since UML 1.3, nonbinary relationships can be represented directly with the diamond notation; ordinary binary associations are commonly drawn as lines. Do not confuse E–R aggregation (treating a relationship as a higher-level entity) with UML’s separate use of a small diamond to express containment.

7.10 · Designing for real use

6. Other aspects of database design

A sound conceptual schema is only one part of the overall application. Constraints, workload, performance, permissions, workflows, and anticipated change influence relational and physical design.

6.1 Data constraints and relational design

  • SQL can express primary-key, foreign-key, and check constraints, as well as assertions and triggers.
  • Explicit constraints let the DBMS enforce consistency centrally instead of relying on every application program to enforce the same rule.
  • Known constraints help identify and link information correctly across relations. A unique identifier can link records; a non-unique property such as eye colour cannot uniquely identify a person.
  • Constraint checking can add cost to updates: the DBMS must test constraints and reject invalid changes or run applicable triggers. The cost depends on the type and frequency of updates and on schema design.

6.2 Usage requirements and performance

Throughput

The number of queries or updates (transactions) processed on average per unit of time. Important for high-volume batch processing.

Response time

The time one transaction takes from start to finish, measured as an average or worst case. Important in interactive and time-critical systems.

Throughput and response time are not the same. Better utilization may delay individual transactions while the system runs work efficiently, improving throughput while hurting response time.

Expected query patterns inform index design. Joins generally use more resources than queries without joins; indexes can help joins and selection predicates commonly found in WHERE clauses. But indexes also slow updates because they must be maintained. The read/update mix therefore matters.

6.3 Authorization requirements

Access rights may influence the logical schema. An employee record can combine payroll, job-function, and medical-benefit data, even though separate administrative groups may need different access. Splitting data across relations can make permissions easier to manage; views are another way to control access when data remain in one relation. These concerns become more important when data are distributed across systems.

6.4 Data flow and workflow

Definition A workflow is the combination of data and tasks involved in an organisational process as it moves among users and systems.

Enterprise modeling should capture both the meaning of the data and the business processes that use it: workflows can imply sequences of queries and updates relevant to design.

6.5 Evolving requirements and cross-system data

Database design is not a one-time activity. Requirements and stored data change, potentially affecting conceptual, logical, and physical schemas and application code. Distinguish fundamental constraints likely to remain stable (for example, an instructor ID uniquely identifying one instructor) from policies that may change (for example, whether an instructor may have only one department).

A design should anticipate plausible changes and minimize disruption. When organisations exchange data, different schemas must interoperate; the source notes XML as a widely used data model for representing data exchanged between applications. User needs and domain experts’ knowledge also remain central throughout design and deployment.

7. Exam recap and self-check

7.1 High-yield rules in one place

  • Strong entity: one relation; entity key remains the relation key.
  • Composite attribute: flatten to components. Multivalued attribute: separate relation with owner key + value; owner key is a foreign key.
  • Weak entity: primary key = owner’s primary key + discriminator; foreign key to owner; the example uses delete cascade.
  • Relationship relation: include participating entity keys + descriptive attributes. Primary key depends on cardinality.
  • Redundant identifying relationship: often drop it when the weak entity table already includes the strong key and the relationship has no descriptive attributes.
  • Merge rules: one-to-many relationship attributes can move to the many side; one-to-one attributes can move to either side; many-to-many attributes usually stay on the relationship if they depend on the pair.
  • Specialization/generalization: top-down versus bottom-up; lower-level sets inherit attributes and relationship participation.
  • Disjoint/overlapping describes subclass membership overlap; total/partial describes completeness. These dimensions are independent.
  • Aggregation: treat a relationship and its participating entities as a higher-level unit that can join another relationship.
  • Performance: throughput counts transactions per unit time; response time measures the duration of one transaction. Indexes can accelerate reads but make updates more expensive.

7.2 Check your understanding

  1. Why does a multivalued attribute normally require a separate relation? What is its primary key and which attribute(s) act as a foreign key?
  2. A weak section depends on course. Explain why course_id belongs in the section primary key.
  3. For a binary many-to-one relationship, which entity set’s primary key becomes the relationship relation’s primary key?
  4. When can a relationship relation be merged into an entity relation? What changes if participation is partial?
  5. Why can the ternary proj_guide relationship not be represented faithfully by independent instructor–student and instructor–project links?
  6. Contrast condition-defined membership with user-defined membership.
  7. Give examples of total-disjoint and partial-overlapping specializations, then explain why totality and disjointness are separate constraints.
  8. What modeling problem does aggregation solve in the evaluation-report example?
  9. State one limitation of the subclass-only mapping of a generalization.
  10. Why might a database designer avoid adding indexes to every searchable attribute?
Reveal compact answer cues
  1. One entity can have multiple values; each value needs a distinct tuple. The key is owner primary key + value; owner-key attributes reference the owner relation.
  2. The section discriminator is unique only within a course; the owner key completes identification across courses.
  3. The key of the entity set on the many side.
  4. Merge a compatible many-to-one relationship into the many-side schema; partial participation may require NULLs for missing counterparts.
  5. The independent binary links lose which exact instructor–student–project triples exist.
  6. Condition-defined: student type determines graduate/undergraduate. User-defined: a user assigns employees to teams.
  7. Total-disjoint: all students are either graduate or undergraduate, never both. Partial-overlapping: employees may or may not yet belong to teams and can belong to multiple teams.
  8. It lets an evaluation refer to a particular guidance relationship rather than merely to one of its participating entities.
  9. Overlapping subclasses duplicate common attributes; partial membership leaves superclass instances unrepresented; foreign keys lack one superclass target relation.
  10. Indexes improve reads but require maintenance during updates and consume resources.
