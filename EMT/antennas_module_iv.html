EMT · Module IV:
Antennas and Radiation

First, let's establish what this entire module is trying to teach you.

In the previous topics, we studied electromagnetic waves travelling through space or along transmission lines. Now we're studying how an antenna creates those waves, shapes their direction, and receives electromagnetic energy.

Imagine an antenna connected to a radio transmitter.

The transmitter supplies electrical power. The antenna converts some of that power into electromagnetic radiation. That radiation spreads through space, and another antenna can capture some of it.

Every concept on page 1 describes a different part of this process.

The central question behind page 1 is:

How do we describe where an antenna sends its energy, how strongly it sends it, and how effectively it can capture incoming energy?

Let's build that understanding.

1. Start with radiation: where does the energy go?

Q1. Antenna gain

Imagine standing in the middle of a football stadium with a loudspeaker.

You could distribute the sound equally in every direction, or you could direct most of it towards one particular stand.

An antenna does something similar with electromagnetic energy.

An isotropic radiator spreads energy equally in every direction.

A directional antenna concentrates more energy in selected directions.

The important idea is that gain tells us how strongly an antenna radiates in a particular direction compared with an ideal isotropic reference.

The equation from your PDF:

\[ G(\theta,\phi)=\frac{4\pi U(\theta,\phi)}{P_{in}} \]

And:

\[ \boxed{G=\eta D} \]

Let's decode every symbol.

SymbolPhysical meaning
\(G\)Antenna gain
\(\theta,\phi\)Angles identifying a direction in space
\(U\)Radiation intensity in that direction
\(P_{in}\)Power accepted by the antenna
\(\eta\)Antenna efficiency
\(D\)Directivity

Why is there a \(4\pi\)?

Because a complete sphere covers a solid angle of \(4\pi\) steradians. An isotropic antenna distributes its radiation across that entire sphere.

What does dBi mean?

It expresses antenna gain in decibels relative to an isotropic radiator.

For example:

  • 0 dBi: same directional reference as an isotropic radiator.
  • 3 dBi: approximately twice the radiation intensity in that direction, for the same accepted input power.

Notice something important: gain includes the antenna's losses. Directivity alone does not.

We'll understand that difference shortly.

Q2. Radiation pattern

Gain tells us about radiation strength in a direction.

But what if we want to see the antenna's behaviour in all directions?

That's where the radiation pattern enters.

Imagine attaching a tiny measuring instrument to a rotating arm around an antenna.

You measure the radiated field at different angles:

  • Straight ahead.
  • Slightly to the right.
  • Behind the antenna.
  • Above and below it.

You plot the measurements against direction.

The resulting plot is called the radiation pattern.

Understand these terms:

  • Main lobe: The direction in which the antenna radiates most strongly.
  • Side lobes: Smaller unwanted or secondary radiation regions.
  • Back lobe: Radiation in the opposite direction to the main lobe.
  • Null: A direction where radiation becomes zero or approaches zero.

The pattern can be represented in 2-D or 3-D.

Your PDF mentions E-plane and H-plane plots. These are two different cross-sectional views of the 3-D radiation behaviour.

The essential distinction: The radiation pattern is the complete directional picture. Gain is a measurement of the strength relative to an isotropic reference.

Q3. Radiation intensity

Now imagine you are measuring the radiation coming from an antenna.

You stand 1 metre away and measure the power crossing each square metre.

Then you move to 2 metres away.

The power is now distributed over a larger spherical surface, so the power per square metre decreases.

But how do we describe the amount of radiation being sent into a particular direction without it depending on how far away we stand?

We use radiation intensity.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{U=r^2W_{rad}} \]

Where:

  • \(U\) = radiation intensity, in W/sr.
  • \(r\) = distance from the antenna, in metres.
  • \(W_{rad}\) = radiated power density, in W/m².

Here's the physical connection.

As the wave travels outward, its energy spreads over a larger spherical surface.

The surface area increases with \(r^2\).

Therefore:

\[ W_{rad}\propto\frac{1}{r^2} \]

Multiplying by \(r^2\):

\[ U=r^2W_{rad} \]

The distance dependence disappears in the far field.

Think of it this way: Power density tells you how much power reaches a square metre at your location. Radiation intensity tells you how much power the antenna sends into a particular solid angle.

And what is a solid angle?

It is the 3-D version of an ordinary angle.

A complete circle contains \(2\pi\) radians. A complete sphere contains:

\[ \boxed{4\pi\text{ steradians}} \]

That's why the unit of radiation intensity is watts per steradian (W/sr).

Q4. Isotropic vs practical antenna

Let's compare two antenna models.

Isotropic antenna

Radiation is equal in every direction.

\[ D=1 \]

Ideal reference antenna, not a physically realizable practical antenna.

Practical antenna

Radiation varies with direction.

Its directivity can exceed 1.

Its geometry creates a non-uniform radiation pattern.

Why do we need an isotropic antenna if we cannot physically build one?

Because we need a reference.

Suppose an antenna radiates more strongly in one direction than an isotropic source would with the same total radiated power.

We can quantify that difference.

That's exactly what directivity measures.

2. How concentrated is the radiation?

Q5. Antenna efficiency

Now comes an important distinction.

Suppose the transmitter supplies 100 W to an antenna.

Does the antenna radiate all 100 W?

No.

Some energy can be lost through resistance in the antenna conductors and other losses.

Think of the input power as being divided into two paths:

100 Winput power
Electrical power supplied to the antenna
80 WRadiated power
Leaves as electromagnetic waves
20 WLoss power
Dissipated, mainly as heat

The antenna efficiency tells us what fraction of input power actually becomes radiation.

\[ \boxed{\eta=\frac{P_{rad}}{P_{in}}} \]

In our example:

\[ \eta=\frac{80}{100}=0.8=80\% \]

Your PDF also provides:

\[ \boxed{\eta=\frac{R_r}{R_r+R_L}} \]

What are these resistances?

Imagine replacing the antenna with an equivalent circuit.

  • \(R_r\): Radiation resistance. It represents power leaving the antenna as electromagnetic radiation.
  • \(R_L\): Loss resistance. It represents power wasted inside the antenna, mainly as heat.

Radiation resistance is not an ordinary physical resistor installed in the antenna. It is an equivalent-circuit way of representing radiated power.

We'll study it properly on page 3.

For now, remember the physical idea:

Efficiency tells us how much of the input power survives the antenna's internal losses and becomes radiation.

Q6. Beamwidth

Imagine shining a torch.

A tightly focused torch produces a narrow beam. A floodlight produces a wide beam.

An antenna has a similar directional spread.

The width of its main radiation lobe is called the beamwidth.

Two definitions appear in your PDF.

1. Half-Power Beamwidth (HPBW)

Imagine starting at the centre of the main lobe and moving towards either side.

Eventually, the radiated power falls to half of its maximum value.

The angle between those two half-power points is HPBW.

\[ \boxed{HPBW=\theta_2-\theta_1} \]

Why does your PDF call this \(-3\text{ dB}\)?

Because:

\[ 10\log_{10}(0.5)\approx-3.01\text{ dB} \]

So half power corresponds to approximately \(-3\text{ dB}\).

2. First-Null Beamwidth (FNBW)

Instead of stopping at half-power points, keep moving outward until the radiation reaches its first null on either side.

The angular separation between those two first nulls is FNBW.

Here's why beamwidth matters physically:

A narrow beam concentrates energy into a smaller angular region. This generally means higher directivity.

A wide beam spreads energy over a larger angular region, which can be useful for broadcasting.

3. Directivity, polarization and receiving antennas

Q7. Directivity

Now let's revisit the isotropic antenna.

An isotropic antenna distributes its radiation equally over all directions.

A practical antenna may concentrate more of its radiation in one direction and less in others.

Directivity measures that concentration.

Your PDF gives:

\[ D=\frac{U_{max}}{U_{avg}} \]

Since the total radiated power is spread over \(4\pi\) steradians:

\[ U_{avg}=\frac{P_{rad}}{4\pi} \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{D=\frac{4\pi U_{max}}{P_{rad}}} \]

Let's compare directivity and efficiency.

DirectivityEfficiency
How concentrated is the radiation?How much input power becomes radiation?
Describes directional behaviourDescribes power losses
Ignores lossesIncludes losses

And that's why:

\[ \boxed{G=\eta D} \]

Imagine two antennas with identical directivity.

One is 100% efficient. The other is 50% efficient.

They concentrate radiation equally well, but the first has twice the gain because it wastes less input power.

That distinction is extremely important.

Q8. Polarization

This one is beautiful because it connects directly to the electromagnetic waves we have been studying.

Remember that an electromagnetic wave contains electric and magnetic fields.

The electric field oscillates as the wave travels.

But in which direction does it oscillate?

That orientation is called polarization.

Imagine looking at the tip of the electric-field vector at one fixed location while time passes.

Its movement can trace different paths.

Linear

Tip moves along a straight line.

Circular

Tip traces a circle.

Elliptical

Tip traces an ellipse.

The linear case can be:

  • Vertical polarization.
  • Horizontal polarization.

Circular polarization also has two senses of rotation:

  • RHCP: Right-hand circular polarization.
  • LHCP: Left-hand circular polarization.

Why does polarization matter?

A transmitting antenna and receiving antenna must have compatible polarization to receive energy efficiently. A mismatch can reduce the received signal.

This is not about the direction in which the antenna is physically pointing. It's about the orientation of the electric field.

Q9. Effective aperture

Now reverse the situation.

So far, we've been studying how an antenna transmits energy.

What happens when an electromagnetic wave arrives at an antenna?

The antenna captures some of that incoming energy and delivers electrical power to a connected load.

But how do we describe its ability to capture energy?

We use effective aperture.

Think of incoming radiation as a stream of energy passing through space.

The effective aperture describes the equivalent area through which the incident power density would need to pass to supply the power delivered to the matched load.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{A_e=\frac{P_r}{W_i}} \]

Where:

SymbolMeaning
\(A_e\)Effective aperture, in m²
\(P_r\)Power delivered to the matched load, in W
\(W_i\)Incident power density, in W/m²

Rearranging:

\[ \boxed{P_r=A_eW_i} \]

For example, suppose:

  • Incident power density = \(2\text{ W/m}^2\)
  • Effective aperture = \(3\text{ m}^2\)

Then:

\[ P_r=3\times2=6\text{ W} \]

The antenna delivers 6 W to the matched load under those conditions.

Important: Effective aperture is not necessarily the actual physical metal area of the antenna. It is an equivalent receiving capability.

Q10. Relationship between effective aperture and directivity

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{A_e=\frac{\lambda^2D}{4\pi}} \]

This connects two ideas that we have already learned.

  • Directivity \(D\): How concentrated the antenna's radiation is.
  • Wavelength \(\lambda\): The spatial length of one complete electromagnetic wave cycle.
  • Effective aperture \(A_e\): How effectively the antenna captures incoming energy.

The relationship says that greater directivity corresponds to greater effective aperture at a given wavelength.

Why?

An antenna that concentrates its transmission into a particular direction also has a stronger receiving response from that direction, assuming the corresponding antenna properties and polarization are matched.

Notice the square:

\[ A_e\propto\lambda^2 \]

So wavelength matters significantly.

For the formula on your sheet, directivity is being used. We'll keep that convention as we work through the supplied material.

4. What frequencies and directions can the antenna handle?

Q11. Bandwidth

Imagine an antenna designed to operate around a particular frequency.

Will it work equally well at every frequency?

No.

Its impedance, radiation pattern, gain and other characteristics change with frequency.

Bandwidth describes the range of frequencies over which the antenna remains within acceptable operating limits.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{\text{Percentage bandwidth}= \frac{f_H-f_L}{f_c}\times100\%} \]

Where:

  • \(f_H\): Upper frequency limit.
  • \(f_L\): Lower frequency limit.
  • \(f_c\): Centre frequency.

Suppose an antenna operates acceptably between 90 MHz and 110 MHz.

Its bandwidth is:

\[ BW=110-90=20\text{ MHz} \]

Taking the centre frequency as 100 MHz:

\[ \text{Percentage bandwidth} =\frac{20}{100}\times100 =20\% \]

Physical interpretation: A wider bandwidth means the antenna can operate acceptably over a wider frequency range.

Q12. Front-to-back ratio

One final directional property.

Imagine pointing a directional antenna towards a transmitter.

You want strong radiation in front, but very little radiation behind it.

How do you quantify that?

The front-to-back ratio (F/B) compares radiation in the main-beam direction with radiation exactly opposite to it.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{ F/B\text{ (dB)} =10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{front}}{P_{back}}\right) } \]

Suppose:

\[ P_{front}=100\text{ W},\qquad P_{back}=1\text{ W} \]

Then:

\[ F/B=10\log_{10}(100)=20\text{ dB} \]

A higher front-to-back ratio means relatively less radiation in the backward direction.

This matters for directional communication because unwanted radiation and interference from behind the antenna can be reduced.

Part A. The antenna and the transmission line

Q13. VSWR: Voltage Standing Wave Ratio

First, imagine this physical setup.

You have:

  • A transmitter producing an electromagnetic signal.
  • A transmission line carrying that signal.
  • An antenna connected at the end of that line.

The transmitter sends energy towards the antenna.

Ideally, the antenna should absorb all the incoming power and convert it into radiation.

But what happens when the antenna doesn't accept the incoming signal properly?

Some of the energy gets reflected back towards the transmitter.

This is an impedance mismatch.

First, understand reflection coefficient (\(\Gamma\))

The reflection coefficient tells us how much of the incident voltage wave gets reflected.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{\Gamma=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}} \]

Let's understand the terms.

SymbolMeaning
\(Z_L\)Load impedance, here the antenna impedance
\(Z_0\)Characteristic impedance of the transmission line
\(\Gamma\)Voltage reflection coefficient

Imagine:

\[ Z_L=Z_0 \]

Substituting:

\[ \Gamma=\frac{Z_0-Z_0}{Z_0+Z_0}=0 \]

What does zero mean?

No reflected voltage wave.

The antenna has accepted the incident power without an impedance mismatch at the connection.

Perfect impedance matching
TransmitterIncident wave travels towards antenna
Matched antennaAccepts the incoming power
\(\Gamma = 0\)No reflected wave

So where does VSWR come from?

Here is an interesting phenomenon.

When an incident wave and a reflected wave travel along the same transmission line in opposite directions, they interfere.

At certain locations, their voltages reinforce each other.

At other locations, they partially cancel each other.

This produces a standing-wave pattern along the line.

VSWR measures the ratio of the maximum voltage to the minimum voltage along that line.

\[ \boxed{VSWR=\frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}}} \]

It is also related to the reflection coefficient:

\[ \boxed{VSWR=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}} \]

Now imagine the two extreme conditions.

Perfect match · 1:1\(\Gamma=0\)
No reflected wave. No standing-wave variation.
Mismatch · Greater than 1\(|\Gamma|>0\)
Reflection produces voltage variations.

Your PDF also mentions that, in practice:

\[ VSWR\leq2 \]

is considered acceptable, corresponding approximately to:

\[ |\Gamma|\leq0.33 \]

and return loss of at least 10 dB.

One important distinction: A matched antenna eliminates reflection at the feed point. That does not mean the antenna has zero internal losses. Matching and radiation efficiency are different properties.

Q14. Input impedance

Let's go one step deeper.

We have a transmission line supplying an antenna.

But from the transmission line's perspective, what electrical load is it actually connected to?

It sees the antenna's input impedance.

\[ \boxed{Z_{in}=\frac{V}{I}=R_{in}+jX_{in}} \]

This equation should look familiar from circuit theory.

Impedance is the relationship between voltage and current, including both resistance and reactance.

But the interesting question is: why does an antenna have both?

The resistive part: where does power go?

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{R_{in}=R_r+R_L} \]

We encountered both terms on page 1.

  • \(R_r\): Radiation resistance. Represents power converted into electromagnetic radiation.
  • \(R_L\): Loss resistance. Represents power dissipated inside the antenna.

Think of the antenna's equivalent circuit as containing two power-consuming mechanisms.

One transfers energy away through radiation. The other dissipates energy as heat.

The reactive part: where does energy get stored?

Now comes \(X_{in}\).

Remember that an antenna has electric and magnetic fields surrounding its conducting structure.

These fields can temporarily store energy.

Electric-field energy is associated with capacitance.

Magnetic-field energy is associated with inductance.

This stored energy is exchanged with the antenna as the alternating current changes.

The reactive component describes this behaviour.

\[ Z_{in}=R_{in}+jX_{in} \]

Here:

  • Positive \(X_{in}\) indicates inductive reactance.
  • Negative \(X_{in}\) indicates capacitive reactance.
  • Zero \(X_{in}\) means the input is purely resistive.

Your PDF's half-wave dipole example

\[ \boxed{Z_{in}\approx73+j42.5\ \Omega} \]

Let's decode it:

\[ R_{in}=73\ \Omega \]
\[ X_{in}=42.5\ \Omega \]

The antenna presents a resistive component and an inductive reactive component at the specified operating condition.

This means it isn't purely resistive at its input.

For maximum power transfer, the source and load should be conjugately matched:

\[ \boxed{Z_L=Z_S^*} \]

Here the asterisk represents the complex conjugate.

For a real-valued transmission-line impedance, the corresponding matching condition is simply:

\[ Z_{in}=Z_0 \]

Why should you care?

Because even a well-designed antenna can perform poorly when connected to an incompatible transmission line or source.

Q15. Significance of half-power beamwidth

We studied HPBW on page 1. Your PDF now asks why it matters.

Let's think about an antenna used in radar.

A radar sends electromagnetic energy towards objects and observes the returned signals.

Suppose two aircraft are flying close to each other.

Can the radar distinguish them as two separate objects?

Its angular resolution matters.

A narrower antenna beam can help distinguish objects that are close together in angle.

Your PDF gives four applications:

1. Radiation concentrationNarrower HPBW generally corresponds to higher directivity for a comparable single-main-lobe antenna pattern.

Q16. What are potential functions?

Think about the problem we are trying to solve.

An antenna has currents flowing through its conductor.

Those currents produce magnetic and electric fields.

We want to calculate the fields at different locations in space.

One possible approach would be to solve directly for the electric field \(E\) and magnetic field \(H\) everywhere.

But the equations can become complicated.

So electromagnetic theory introduces two auxiliary mathematical quantities:

A · Magnetic vector potentialA vector field that helps us calculate magnetic and electric fields.
V · Electric scalar potentialA scalar field that helps us calculate the electric field.

These are mathematical helper functions. They are not additional physical waves or additional antennas.

Your PDF gives two defining relationships.

\[ \boxed{\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A} \]

And:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla V-j\omega\mathbf A} \]

Let's understand these individually.

First: magnetic vector potential A

\[ \boxed{\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A} \]

Here:

  • \(\mathbf B\) = magnetic flux density, measured in tesla (T).
  • \(\mathbf A\) = magnetic vector potential.
  • \(\nabla\times\) = curl operator.

What does curl mean physically?

Imagine a vector field represented by tiny arrows throughout space.

Curl measures the local rotational tendency of that field.

The equation tells us that when we calculate the curl of the vector potential, we obtain the magnetic flux density.

And because:

\[ \mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H \]

we also obtain:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf A} \]

So one useful fact emerges:

Once we know A, we can calculate the magnetic field.

Second: electric scalar potential V

Now consider:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla V-j\omega\mathbf A} \]

This has two contributions.

Electric field = contribution 1 + contribution 2
\(-\nabla V\) · Contribution from the scalar potentialSpatial changes in electric potential create an electric field.
\(-j\omega\mathbf A\) · Contribution from the changing vector potentialTime-varying magnetic effects also contribute to the electric field.

You might already recognise the underlying relationship:

\[ \mathbf E=-\nabla V-\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t} \]

This is the time-domain expression.

Your PDF uses phasor notation, where we represent sinusoidally varying quantities using complex amplitudes.

For the \(e^{j\omega t}\) convention:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\longrightarrow j\omega \]

Therefore:

\[ -\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t} \longrightarrow-j\omega\mathbf A \]

That's precisely where the second term comes from.

A useful distinction: In electrostatics, the electric field can be described through the scalar potential alone. In time-varying electromagnetic fields, we also need the vector-potential contribution.

The Lorenz gauge: why do we need it?

Here your PDF introduces something called the Lorenz gauge.

You may wonder: why do we need another condition when we already have equations defining \(A\) and \(V\)?

The answer is that potentials have mathematical flexibility.

Different mathematical choices of potentials can describe the same physical electromagnetic fields.

This freedom is called gauge freedom.

The Lorenz gauge is a particular condition that links the two potentials:

\[ \boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=-j\omega\mu\epsilon V} \]

Let's decode it:

SymbolMeaning
\(\nabla\cdot\)Divergence operator
\(\mathbf A\)Magnetic vector potential
\(\omega\)Angular frequency
\(\mu\)Permeability of the medium
\(\epsilon\)Permittivity of the medium
\(V\)Electric scalar potential

The important thing is what happens after selecting this gauge.

The equations for \(A\) and \(V\) become separate, uncoupled wave equations.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf A+\beta^2\mathbf A=-\mu\mathbf J} \]
\[ \boxed{\nabla^2V+\beta^2V=-\frac{\rho_v}{\epsilon}} \]

Let's slow down and interpret them.

What is the Laplacian, \(\nabla^2\)?

We have encountered gradient, divergence and curl.

The Laplacian is another vector-calculus operator.

It measures how a scalar or vector field varies spatially relative to its surroundings. For a scalar function in Cartesian coordinates:

\[ \nabla^2V= \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2} +\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2} +\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} \]

It appears in wave equations because electromagnetic fields vary through space as well as time.

What is \(\beta\)?

\[ \boxed{\beta=\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}} \]

For a uniform, lossless medium, this is the phase constant, in rad/m.

It describes how rapidly the electromagnetic wave's phase changes with distance.

What are \(\mathbf J\) and \(\rho_v\)?

These are the sources producing the fields.

  • \(\mathbf J\): Volume current density, measured in A/m².
  • \(\rho_v\): Volume charge density, measured in C/m³.

So read the two equations physically:

Vector potential equation\(\nabla^2\mathbf A+\beta^2\mathbf A=-\mu\mathbf J\)
Current density \(\mathbf J\) acts as the source of the vector potential.
Scalar potential equation\(\nabla^2V+\beta^2V=-\rho_v/\epsilon\)
Charge density \(\rho_v\) acts as the source of the scalar potential.

This is the major simplification.

Instead of directly solving the complete electromagnetic field problem, we can solve two potential equations and then reconstruct the fields.

Q19. Far-field approximation

First, imagine that you have a small antenna radiating electromagnetic waves.

Stand very close to it.

You'll find that the surrounding electromagnetic field has a complicated structure. The electric and magnetic fields contain different components, some of which are associated with stored energy around the antenna.

Now walk farther away.

Eventually, the field starts behaving much more like a freely travelling electromagnetic wave.

This region is called the far-field region, also known as the Fraunhofer region.

Why do we even need a far-field approximation?

Because the antenna has a physical size.

Consider a large antenna. Different points along its structure are at slightly different distances from an observation point.

Therefore, the electromagnetic waves produced by different parts of the antenna travel slightly different distances before reaching you.

These differences affect the phase of the waves.

When you're very far away, we can simplify the mathematics without losing the important directional behaviour.

When does the far-field region begin?

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{r>\frac{2D^2}{\lambda}} \]

Along with:

\[ r\gg D,\qquad r\gg\lambda \]

Understand every quantity:

SymbolMeaning
\(r\)Distance from antenna to observation point
\(D\)Largest physical dimension of the antenna
\(\lambda\)Wavelength of the electromagnetic wave

Notice that \(D\) here means the antenna's largest physical dimension. Later, when we study the Hertzian dipole, we'll use \(dl\) for the small current-element length.

They are different quantities.

Why does distance depend on \(D^2/\lambda\)?

Think about two points on a large antenna.

One point is closer to you. Another is slightly farther away.

The waves from these points have a path difference.

As the observation distance increases, this path difference becomes relatively smaller compared with the total propagation distance.

At a sufficiently large distance, we can treat the wavefront as approximately planar over the antenna's angular observation region.

That's the fundamental reason behind the Fraunhofer criterion.

The most important part of this question: two approximations

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{R\approx r} \]

for amplitude, but:

\[ \boxed{R\approx r-r'\cos\psi} \]

for phase.

Why are these different?

Let's visualize the geometry.

Source and observation points

Here, \(R\) represents the actual distance from a tiny source element to your observation point.

1. Amplitude approximation

The potential or field contains a distance factor:

\[ \frac{1}{R} \]

When the observation point is very far away, the difference between the distances to different source elements is relatively small.

So we approximate:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{R}\approx\frac{1}{r}} \]

This simplifies the amplitude calculation.

2. Phase approximation

But look at the propagation term:

\[ e^{-j\beta R} \]

Even a small difference in distance can produce a meaningful phase difference.

Why?

Because the phase depends on:

\[ \beta R \]

where:

\[ \beta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \]

A path difference can represent a significant fraction of a wavelength.

Therefore, we retain the first-order distance correction:

\[ \boxed{R\approx r-r'\cos\psi} \]

Here, \(\psi\) is the angle between the observation direction and the source-position vector.

The important physical lesson: The distance variation is small enough to simplify the amplitude, but it can still be important for phase.

That is why we don't throw away the first-order term in the exponential.

What happens to the electromagnetic fields in the far field?

Your PDF gives three important observations.

First: only the \(1/r\) terms survive.

Near-field expressions can contain:

\[ \frac{1}{r},\qquad\frac{1}{r^2},\qquad\frac{1}{r^3} \]

As we move farther away, the \(1/r^2\) and \(1/r^3\) contributions become negligible compared with \(1/r\).

The radiation field dominates.

Second: the fields become transverse.

Imagine a wave travelling away from the antenna in the radial direction.

In the far field:

  • Electric field is perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
  • Magnetic field is perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
  • Electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other.

This is exactly the transverse electromagnetic wave behaviour we encountered earlier in EMT.

Third: electric and magnetic field magnitudes are related by intrinsic impedance.

Your PDF gives:

\[ \boxed{\frac{E_\theta}{H_\phi}=\eta} \]

Here:

\[ \eta=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}} \]

For free space:

\[ \eta_0\approx377\ \Omega \]

This is the intrinsic impedance of free space.

It describes the ratio between the electric-field magnitude and magnetic-field magnitude of a travelling electromagnetic wave.

Finally, the angular radiation pattern becomes independent of distance in the far field.

The overall field magnitude decreases as \(1/r\), but its directional shape remains the same.

Part 3. How much power actually leaves the antenna?

Q21. Total power radiated by a Hertzian dipole

We now know how strong the electric field is in different directions.

But suppose I ask:

How many watts does this antenna radiate into the entire surrounding space?

We need to add the power flowing outward through every direction.

This is where the Poynting vector enters.

Step 1: Understand power flow

Remember the electromagnetic fields:

  • Electric field \(\mathbf E\).
  • Magnetic field \(\mathbf H\).

Together, they transport energy.

The Poynting vector is:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H} \]

For sinusoidal fields, the time-average power flow in the far field, using peak-amplitude phasors, is:

\[ \boxed{ \langle S_r\rangle=\frac{|E_\theta|^2}{2\eta} } \]

Why does this equation make sense?

In the far field:

\[ E_\theta=\eta H_\phi \]

So:

\[ \frac12|E_\theta H_\phi^*| =\frac{|E_\theta|^2}{2\eta} \]

This gives the average power flowing outward per unit area.

Its unit is:

\[ \mathrm{W/m^2} \]

Step 2: Imagine a giant sphere surrounding the antenna

The antenna sends energy in every direction.

Imagine a spherical surface surrounding it.

The total power radiated is the sum of the power passing through every tiny surface element of this sphere.

Mathematically:

\[ P_{rad}=\int_S \langle S_r\rangle\,dS \]

For a sphere:

\[ dS=r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ P_{rad} = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi \langle S_r\rangle r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi } \]

Notice that we have integrated in both angular directions.

  • \(\theta\) covers the vertical angular direction.
  • \(\phi\) covers the rotation around the antenna axis.

Together they cover the complete sphere.

Step 3: Substitute the Hertzian dipole field

We already know:

\[ E_\theta= \frac{\eta\beta I\,dl}{4\pi r} \sin\theta \]

Ignoring the phase factor when calculating magnitude squared:

\[ |E_\theta|^2= \frac{\eta^2\beta^2I^2(dl)^2}{16\pi^2r^2} \sin^2\theta \]

Substitute this into the average power density:

\[ \langle S_r\rangle= \frac{\eta\beta^2I^2(dl)^2} {32\pi^2r^2} \sin^2\theta \]

Now integrate over the spherical surface.

The angular integration produces:

\[ \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi \sin^3\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = \frac{8\pi}{3} \]

After simplification:

\[ \boxed{ P_{rad}= \frac{\eta(\beta I\,dl)^2}{12\pi} } \]

For free space:

\[ \eta=120\pi,\qquad\beta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \]

Substituting:

\[ P_{rad} = \frac{120\pi}{12\pi} \left(\frac{2\pi I\,dl}{\lambda}\right)^2 \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ P_{rad} = 40\pi^2I^2 \left(\frac{dl}{\lambda}\right)^2 \ \mathrm W } \]

Key physical result

The total radiated power depends on the square of the current and the square of the element's electrical length.

In particular:

\[ \boxed{P_{rad}\propto I^2} \]
\[ \boxed{P_{rad}\propto\left(\frac{dl}{\lambda}\right)^2} \]

This means a very short antenna can have a very small radiated power for a given current.

Convention: The equation above uses peak current amplitude \(I\). If you use RMS current, the equivalent average-power expression has a factor of 2 difference.

Part 2. How does current inside a wire create a vector potential?

Q25. Derive the vector potential function for a small current element

This is one of the most important connections between the previous pages.

On page 2, we learned the general expression for the magnetic vector potential:

\[ \mathbf A(\mathbf r)= \frac{\mu}{4\pi} \int_{v'} \frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')e^{-j\beta R}}{R} \,dv' \]

It looks complicated because it describes an entire volume of current.

But today's problem is much simpler.

We have a tiny straight current element of length \(dl\), positioned at the origin and aligned with the \(z\)-axis.

Step 1. Understand the physical source

Our antenna element has:

  • Length \(dl\), much smaller than wavelength.
  • Uniform current \(I\).
  • Current flowing in the \(z\)-direction.

We write the source current direction as:

\[ \mathbf J\parallel\hat{\mathbf z} \]

Here \(\hat{\mathbf z}\) is a unit vector pointing along the positive \(z\)-axis.

A unit vector specifies direction without changing magnitude.

Step 2. Simplify the general integral

Start with:

\[ \mathbf A(\mathbf r)= \frac{\mu}{4\pi} \int_{v'} \frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')e^{-j\beta R}}{R} \,dv' \]

Because the current element is extremely short, we approximate the distance from its different parts to the observation point as nearly identical:

\[ R\approx r \]

For this infinitesimal source located at the origin, we can also treat the propagation phase as approximately constant over its length.

Thus:

\[ \frac{e^{-j\beta R}}{R} \approx \frac{e^{-j\beta r}}{r} \]

That part can be taken outside the integral.

Our expression becomes:

\[ \mathbf A(\mathbf r) \approx \frac{\mu}{4\pi} \frac{e^{-j\beta r}}{r} \int_{v'}\mathbf J\,dv' \]

Now comes a useful connection.

For a thin conducting element, the volume-current integral over its cross-section and length is equivalent to:

\[ \int_{v'}\mathbf J\,dv' = I\,dl\,\hat{\mathbf z} \]

Why?

Current density multiplied by cross-sectional area gives current:

\[ I=JA \]

Multiplying by the element's length gives:

\[ I\,dl=J(A\,dl) \]

And \(A\,dl\) is the small volume element.

Step 3. Substitute

Therefore:

\[ \mathbf A(\mathbf r)= \frac{\mu}{4\pi} \frac{e^{-j\beta r}}{r} I\,dl\,\hat{\mathbf z} \]

Rearranging:

Derived expression

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf A= \hat{\mathbf z}\frac{\mu I\,dl}{4\pi r} e^{-j\beta r} } \]

This is the vector potential of the small \(z\)-directed current element in your PDF.

Notice what we just accomplished.

We took a general integral over an entire current distribution and reduced it to a compact expression for one tiny current element.

Step 4. Why convert from Cartesian to spherical coordinates?

This is where the geometry becomes important.

Our current element is aligned with the \(z\)-axis.

But the radiation spreads in all directions around it.

So spherical coordinates are more convenient for describing observation points:

  • \(r\): Distance from the origin.
  • \(\theta\): Angle measured from the positive \(z\)-axis.
  • \(\phi\): Rotation around the \(z\)-axis.

Our vector potential currently points along \(\hat{\mathbf z}\).

We need to express that same direction using spherical unit vectors.

The relationship is:

\[ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf z} = \hat{\mathbf r}\cos\theta - \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\sin\theta } \]

This is just a coordinate transformation. We are not changing the physical vector, only describing it using a different coordinate system.

Substitute into our expression.

Let:

\[ C=\frac{\mu I\,dl}{4\pi r}e^{-j\beta r} \]

Then:

\[ \mathbf A=C\hat{\mathbf z} \]
\[ \mathbf A= C(\hat{\mathbf r}\cos\theta -\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\sin\theta) \]

Therefore, its spherical components are:

\[ \boxed{ A_r= \frac{\mu I\,dl}{4\pi r} e^{-j\beta r}\cos\theta } \]
\[ \boxed{ A_\theta= -\frac{\mu I\,dl}{4\pi r} e^{-j\beta r}\sin\theta } \]
\[ \boxed{A_\phi=0} \]

Why is \(A_\phi=0\)?

Because the vector potential points along the dipole axis, and that direction has no component along the azimuthal unit vector.

This is the physical meaning of Q25: We have found the vector potential produced by a tiny current element and expressed it in the coordinate system that best describes radiation around the antenna.

Q26. How are potential functions used to determine electromagnetic fields?

Now let's connect everything from pages 2 and 4.

We have an antenna carrying current.

We want to find the electric and magnetic fields at a point in space.

Instead of calculating everything directly, we use a three-stage procedure.

The electromagnetic field calculation pipeline
Stage 1: Find the potentialsCurrent density \(\mathbf J\) gives \(\mathbf A\). Charge density \(\rho_v\), or the Lorenz gauge, gives \(V\).
Stage 2: Calculate magnetic field\(\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf A\)
Take the curl of the vector potential.
Stage 3: Calculate electric field\(\mathbf E=-\nabla V-j\omega\mathbf A\)
Combine the scalar and vector-potential contributions.

Let's understand why these steps work.

Stage 1: Obtain the potentials

The vector potential comes from the current distribution:

\[ \mathbf A(\mathbf r)= \frac{\mu}{4\pi} \int_{v'} \frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')e^{-j\beta R}}{R}\,dv' \]

The scalar potential comes from the charge distribution:

\[ V(\mathbf r)= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon} \int_{v'} \frac{\rho_v(\mathbf r')e^{-j\beta R}}{R}\,dv' \]

Alternatively, under the Lorenz gauge, the scalar potential can be obtained from the vector potential.

The important distinction:

  • Current distribution determines the vector potential.
  • Charge distribution determines the scalar potential.

Stage 2: Obtain the magnetic field

\[ \boxed{\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf A} \]

Why curl?

Because the vector potential is defined so that:

\[ \mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A \]

And:

\[ \mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H \]

Therefore:

\[ \mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf A \]

We now have the magnetic field.

Stage 3: Obtain the electric field

There are two methods in your PDF.

Method A: From the potentials\(\mathbf E=-\nabla V-j\omega\mathbf A\)
Use this when the potential functions are available.
Method B: From Maxwell's equations\(\mathbf E=\frac{1}{j\omega\epsilon}\nabla\times\mathbf H\)
In a source-free homogeneous region (\(\nabla\times\mathbf H=j\omega\epsilon\mathbf E\)).

Why does Method B work?

Because Ampère-Maxwell's law relates the curl of the magnetic field to the time-varying electric field.

We already have \(\mathbf H\) from Stage 2, so we can use it to obtain \(\mathbf E\).

The important conceptual connection

The potential method and Maxwell-equation method are not two different physical theories.

They are two ways to obtain the same electric field.

One calculates it from the potentials. The other calculates it from the magnetic field using Maxwell's equations, under the specified source-free conditions.

Part 1. Why do we need an ideal antenna model?

Q31. Why is the Hertzian dipole considered an ideal antenna?

First, forget the equations for a moment.

Imagine trying to study how a complicated machine works. Instead of analysing the entire machine, you break it into very small components and understand each component individually.

Then you combine their behaviour to understand the whole machine.

That's essentially what electromagnetic antenna theory does with the Hertzian dipole.

A Hertzian dipole is an idealized, infinitesimally short current element.

Its defining assumptions are:

\[ \boxed{dl\ll\lambda} \]

And:

  • Current amplitude is constant along the element.
  • Current phase is constant along the element.
  • The element is sufficiently short to approximate its current distribution as uniform.

Why can't we construct a perfectly real Hertzian dipole?

Look at an actual half-wave dipole.

Notice the current distribution.

Current is maximum near the centre and gradually decreases towards the open ends.

At the open ends:

\[ I=0 \]

Why?

Because an open end does not provide a conducting path for current to continue flowing out of the wire.

But a Hertzian dipole assumes uniform current throughout its infinitesimal length.

These are different physical models.

The Hertzian dipole is therefore an ideal mathematical model, rather than an antenna that can be constructed with perfectly uniform current all the way to its ends.

Three reasons it is so important

1. Its fields can be calculated exactly.

The simplified current distribution allows us to derive electromagnetic field expressions in closed form.

We can study the near-field, induction-field and radiation-field components.

Remember the different distance dependencies?

\[ \frac{1}{r^3},\qquad \frac{1}{r^2},\qquad \frac{1}{r} \]

The Hertzian dipole gives us a way to understand how these components behave.

2. It is the building block of antenna theory.

Imagine a long conductor.

Instead of treating the entire conductor as one source, we divide it into small current elements.

Each element contributes its own electromagnetic field.

We add their contributions together.

Mathematically:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf E_{\text{total}} = \sum_n \mathbf E_n } \]

For a continuous current distribution, this becomes an integration over the antenna's source distribution.

This is the principle of superposition.

3. It simplifies antenna design and analysis.

By understanding one elementary current source, we can construct mathematical models for more complicated antennas, including dipoles and arrays.

The central idea: A Hertzian dipole is not considered ideal because it is a perfect practical antenna. It is ideal because it gives us a simple, mathematically manageable elementary radiator from which more complicated antenna systems can be studied.

Part 2. Numerical problems

Before solving anything, let's establish one relationship that appears repeatedly.

The three quantities that control wavelength

An electromagnetic wave has:

  • Frequency \(f\): How many oscillation cycles occur per second.
  • Wavelength \(\lambda\): The spatial length of one complete cycle.
  • Propagation velocity \(c\): How quickly the wave travels through free space.

For free space:

\[ \boxed{\lambda=\frac{c}{f}} \]

Where:

\[ c=3\times10^8\text{ m/s} \]

Visualize this:

One wavelength is the distance covered during one complete oscillation cycle.

Here's the important physical connection:

  • Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
  • Lower frequency means longer wavelength.

This relationship will explain almost every antenna-length numerical we encounter.

Part 4. From vector potential to magnetic field

Q9. Find the far-field components of H for a z-directed current element

This is an important derivation because we're returning to Q25.

Remember the tiny antenna element aligned along the \(z\)-axis?

The current flows along the \(z\)-axis.

What happens to the magnetic field around a current-carrying conductor?

It forms circular loops around the conductor.

You can understand this using the right-hand rule.

Point your right thumb in the direction of current. Your curled fingers indicate the magnetic-field direction.

For a \(z\)-directed current element, the magnetic field is in the azimuthal direction:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf H=H_\phi\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}} \]

That's the physical picture we want to translate into mathematics.

Step 1. Start with the vector potential

From Q25:

\[ \mathbf A= \hat{\mathbf z} \frac{\mu I\,dl}{4\pi r}e^{-j\beta r} \]

Now convert the direction into spherical coordinates:

\[ \hat{\mathbf z} = \hat{\mathbf r}\cos\theta -\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\sin\theta \]

Therefore: \(A_r=A_z\cos\theta\), \(A_\theta=-A_z\sin\theta\), \(A_\phi=0\).

Step 2. Use the curl relationship

Remember:

\[ \boxed{\mathbf H=\frac1\mu\nabla\times\mathbf A} \]

We need the curl of the vector potential. In spherical coordinates, its azimuthal component is:

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf A)_\phi = \frac1r \left[ \frac{\partial(rA_\theta)}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial A_r}{\partial\theta} \right] \]

Why does only this component survive? Because the vector potential has no azimuthal component and does not vary with \(\phi\). Consequently: \(H_r=0\), \(H_\theta=0\). Only \(H_\phi\) remains.

After differentiating and simplifying, the expression from your PDF is:

\[ \boxed{ H_\phi= \frac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{4\pi} e^{-j\beta r} \left( \frac{j\beta}{r}+\frac1{r^2} \right) } \]

Notice two terms: \(\dfrac{1}{r^2}\) and \(\dfrac{j\beta}{r}\). The first is a near-field contribution. The second is the radiation-field contribution.

Step 3. Move into the far field

In the far field:

\[ \boxed{\beta r\gg1} \]

The \(1/r^2\) term becomes negligible compared with the \(1/r\) term. We obtain:

\[ H_\phi= j\frac{\beta I\,dl}{4\pi r} \sin\theta e^{-j\beta r} \]

Since \(\beta=\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\), we can also write:

\[ \boxed{ H_\phi= j\frac{I\,dl}{2\lambda r} \sin\theta e^{-j\beta r} } \]
Far-field result
\(H_r = 0\)No radial magnetic field
\(H_\theta = 0\)No polar magnetic field
\(H_\phi = j\frac{\beta I\,dl}{4\pi r}\sin\theta e^{-j\beta r}\)Purely azimuthal loops

Step 4. Connect it to the electric field

From Q20:

\[ E_\theta= j\frac{\eta\beta I\,dl}{4\pi r} \sin\theta e^{-j\beta r} \]

Compare the expressions. The electric field contains an additional factor of \(\eta\). Therefore:

\[ \boxed{E_\theta=\eta H_\phi} \]

This is the relationship between the electric and magnetic field magnitudes in the far field. For free space, \(\eta=377\ \Omega\). Both fields have the same propagation phase and are perpendicular to each other.

What did we actually do? We started with a current inside a tiny wire, constructed its vector potential, took the curl, and obtained the magnetic field. We didn't introduce a completely new formula. We reused the physics from Q25.

Q10. Derive the distant θ-component of electric field

Your PDF lists this as a derivation question but does not provide its worked solution.

Let's understand what the question is asking before attempting the derivation.

Previously, our Hertzian dipole had an extremely small length \(dl\).

Now imagine a dipole extending over a length \(L\).

The physical problem has changed. Instead of one tiny current element, we have many tiny current elements distributed along a conductor.

Each element:

  • Carries some current.
  • Produces its own electric field.
  • Has a slightly different distance to the observation point.
  • Contributes with a slightly different phase.

Therefore, the total electric field is obtained by adding all the individual contributions. This is superposition again.

The far-field geometry gives:

\[ R\approx r-z'\cos\theta \]

So the propagation phase becomes:

\[ e^{-j\beta R} = e^{-j\beta r}e^{j\beta z'\cos\theta} \]

Conceptually, the total field is obtained by integrating the contributions:

\[ E_\theta\propto \frac{e^{-j\beta r}\sin\theta}{r} \int I(z')e^{j\beta z'\cos\theta}\,dz' \]

Here \(I(z')\) describes how current varies along the dipole.

Q11. Current required to radiate 50 W from a Hertzian dipole

Given quantityValue
Radiated power \(P_{rad}\)50 W
Frequency \(f\)60 MHz
Electrical length \(dl/\lambda\)0.1

Find: (a) Required current. (b) Radiation resistance.

First, visualize the problem

Imagine a tiny antenna element carrying alternating current. That current produces electromagnetic radiation, which carries energy away from the antenna. We want to produce 50 W of radiated power. But how much current is required? There's one intermediate quantity we need first: radiation resistance.

Step 1. Calculate radiation resistance first

Remember:

\[ \boxed{R_r=80\pi^2\left(\frac{dl}{\lambda}\right)^2} \]

We are given \(\dfrac{dl}{\lambda}=0.1\). Notice something interesting: we don't need to know the actual antenna length yet. The equation needs only the ratio between antenna length and wavelength.

\[ R_r=80\pi^2(0.1)^2=80\pi^2\times0.01=7.8957 \]
\[ \boxed{R_r=7.90\ \Omega} \]

Why did we calculate this first? Because radiation resistance tells us the relationship between current and radiated power. Without it, we cannot calculate the required current using the power equation.

Step 2. Calculate the current

For peak current:

\[ \boxed{P_{rad}=\frac12I_0^2R_r} \implies I_0=\sqrt{\frac{2P_{rad}}{R_r}}=\sqrt{\frac{2(50)}{7.90}}=\sqrt{\frac{100}{7.90}} \]
\[ \boxed{I_0=3.56\text{ A}} \]

This is the peak current.

Step 3. Convert peak current into RMS current

For a sinusoidal current:

\[ I_{rms}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt2}=\frac{3.56}{1.414} \implies \boxed{I_{rms}=2.52\text{ A}} \]

The question also gives \(f=60\text{ MHz}\). Why? Because we can determine the actual physical length of the antenna.

\[ \lambda=\frac{c}{f}=\frac{3\times10^8}{60\times10^6} \implies \boxed{\lambda=5\text{ m}} \]

We know \(dl=0.1\lambda\), therefore:

\[ dl=0.1\times5 \implies \boxed{dl=0.5\text{ m}} \]

Part 2. Does a short antenna waste most of its power?

Q12. Radiation efficiency of a Hertzian dipole

Given quantityValue
Antenna length\(0.03\lambda\)
Frequency100 MHz
Loss resistance \(R_L\)0.01 Ω

Find radiation efficiency.

This is a particularly interesting question. You might expect an electrically short antenna to have poor efficiency because we already learned that short antennas have small radiation resistance. But look at the supplied loss resistance. It's extremely small. Let's see what happens.

Step 1. Find radiation resistance

\[ \boxed{R_r=80\pi^2\left(\frac{dl}{\lambda}\right)^2} = 80\pi^2(0.03)^2 = 789.57\times0.0009 \implies \boxed{R_r=0.711\ \Omega} \]

Step 2. Calculate efficiency

Remember the efficiency equation from page 1:

\[ \boxed{\eta_r=\frac{R_r}{R_r+R_L}} = \frac{0.711}{0.711+0.01} = \frac{0.711}{0.721} = 0.9861 \implies \boxed{\eta_r=98.6\%} \]

98.6%Radiated (\(R_r = 0.711\ \Omega\))
1.4%Lost (\(R_L = 0.01\ \Omega\))

Step 3. Why is the efficiency so high despite its short length?

Here's the most important conceptual point of Q12. The antenna is electrically short: \(dl=0.03\lambda\). Normally, a short antenna has a small radiation resistance. But efficiency does not depend on radiation resistance alone. It depends on the relationship between radiation resistance and loss resistance: \(\eta_r=R_r/(R_r+R_L)\). Our antenna has \(R_r=0.711\ \Omega\) and \(R_L=0.01\ \Omega\). The loss resistance is tiny compared with radiation resistance. Therefore, most of the supplied power goes into radiation rather than internal losses. That is why the efficiency is 98.6%.

Part 3. The difference between an ideal short antenna and a real short antenna

Your PDF finishes with an important observation: A real short antenna usually has larger loss resistance, and its efficiency can drop sharply.

Imagine two short antennas with identical electrical lengths and therefore identical radiation resistance in this simplified model. However, their internal loss resistances differ.

Antenna A: exceptionally low lossesPDF example: \(R_r=0.711\ \Omega,\ R_L=0.01\ \Omega \implies \eta_r=98.6\%\)
Antenna B: greater internal lossesSame \(R_r=0.711\ \Omega\), larger \(R_L\) makes denominator \(R_r+R_L\) grow, so efficiency drops sharply.

The key is not simply whether an antenna is short. It's how much of the total electrical power is converted into radiation versus dissipated internally.

One more connection to Q31: Remember why we called the Hertzian dipole an ideal antenna? It assumes an infinitesimally short current element with uniform current amplitude and phase. A real short antenna cannot perfectly satisfy all those ideal assumptions. Its actual current distribution, conductor losses and construction influence its practical performance. So the Hertzian dipole is an excellent theoretical starting point, but its idealized properties should not be confused with guaranteed real-world antenna performance.
