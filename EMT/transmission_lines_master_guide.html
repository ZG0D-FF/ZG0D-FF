Why Electromagnetic Field Theory is Mandatory for Transmission Lines

Transmission lines are guided structures designed to transport electromagnetic energy from one terminal to another with minimal attenuation. Canonical forms include coaxial cables, twisted pair lines, parallel-plate lines, and planar microstrips.

Operational Regime Physical Frequency Range Electrodynamic Reality & Circuit Limitations
Low Frequencies (DC to Audio) $f < 100\text{ kHz}$ ($\lambda > 3\text{ km}$) Wavelength $\lambda$ is immensely larger than physical circuit dimensions ($l \ll \lambda$). Voltage and current are spatially uniform across any conductor cross-section. Propagation delay is negligible ($t_{transit} \approx 0$). Lumped element circuit model (KVL, KCL) is completely sufficient.
High Frequencies (RF, Microwave) $f > 100\text{ MHz}$ ($\lambda < 3\text{ m}$) Wavelength becomes comparable to or smaller than circuit line length ($l \ge \lambda/20$). Voltage and current vary drastically in magnitude and phase along the physical wire. Energy travels as electromagnetic waves guided by field interactions. Distributed parameter wave analysis is indispensable.

Primary Engineering Applications (Page 4)

  • RF & Microwave Engineering: Antenna feeds, impedance matching networks, directional couplers, power dividers, and high-speed digital interconnects (preserving signal integrity against reflection glitches).
  • Telecommunications: Long-distance broadband coax, twisted-pair Ethernet, and subsea trunks.
  • Power Systems: High-voltage AC/DC power transmission grids.
  • Medical & Scientific Instrumentation: MRI RF excitation coils, particle accelerator feeds, ultra-fast sampling oscilloscope probes.

Lumped Element Circuit Model vs. Distributed Parameter Model

Feature / Metric Lumped Element Model Distributed Parameter Model
Component Nature Components ($R, L, C$) are physically distinct and concentrated at discrete points. Parameters ($R, L, C, G$) are uniformly distributed along the entire length of the line.
Applicability Criterion Valid only when physical dimensions are much smaller than wavelength ($l \ll \lambda$). Essential when line length is comparable to or greater than wavelength ($l \ge \lambda/20$).
Mathematical Analysis Ordinary algebraic equations & ODEs using Kirchhoff's Laws (KVL, KCL). $V$ and $I$ assumed constant along wires. Partial differential equations (Telegrapher's equations). $V(z,t)$ and $I(z,t)$ vary with both space $z$ and time $t$.
Propagation Delay Assumed instantaneous ($t_{transit} \approx 0$). Finite and significant ($t_{transit} = l/v_p$). Phase shifts must be accounted for.
Parameter Units Discrete scalar values: $\Omega$, $\text{H}$, $\text{F}$. Per-unit-length quantities: $\Omega/\text{m}$, $\text{H/m}$, $\text{F/m}$, $\text{S/m}$.
Canonical Example A simple lumped $RC$ low-pass filter circuit. Coaxial cable, parallel-wire line, or high-speed microstrip.

Universal Physical Invariants ($LC$ and $G/C$)
For all transverse electromagnetic (TEM) lines in a homogeneous medium ($\mu, \varepsilon, \sigma$): $$L \cdot C = \mu\,\varepsilon \iff u_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}}$$ $$\frac{G}{C} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$ These identities hold universally across all geometries regardless of cross-sectional shape!

Complete Derivation of Telegrapher's Differential Equations

Step 1 · Equivalent Circuit Formulation (Page 7)

An incremental length $\Delta z$ of transmission line is modeled with series resistance $R\Delta z$, series inductance $L\Delta z$, shunt conductance $G\Delta z$, and shunt capacitance $C\Delta z$.

Step 2 · Loop Analysis via KVL (Page 8)

Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law around the outer mesh:

$$V(z,t) = R\Delta z\,I(z,t) + L\Delta z\,\frac{\partial I(z,t)}{\partial t} + V(z+\Delta z, t)$$ $$-\frac{V(z+\Delta z, t) - V(z,t)}{\Delta z} = R\,I(z,t) + L\,\frac{\partial I(z,t)}{\partial t}$$

Taking the limit $\Delta z \to 0$ gives the first Telegrapher equation:

$$-\frac{\partial V(z,t)}{\partial z} = R\,I(z,t) + L\,\frac{\partial I(z,t)}{\partial t} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.4)}$$
Step 3 · Node Analysis via KCL (Page 8)

Applying Kirchhoff's current law to the main shunt node:

$$I(z,t) = I(z+\Delta z, t) + G\Delta z\,V(z+\Delta z, t) + C\Delta z\,\frac{\partial V(z+\Delta z, t)}{\partial t}$$ $$-\frac{I(z+\Delta z, t) - I(z,t)}{\Delta z} = G\,V(z+\Delta z, t) + C\,\frac{\partial V(z+\Delta z, t)}{\partial t}$$

Taking the limit $\Delta z \to 0$ gives the second Telegrapher equation:

$$-\frac{\partial I(z,t)}{\partial z} = G\,V(z,t) + C\,\frac{\partial V(z,t)}{\partial t} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.6)}$$
Step 4 · Phasor Transformation & Uncoupling (Pages 9–10)

Assuming harmonic time variation $e^{j\omega t}$, time derivatives transform as $\partial/\partial t \leftrightarrow j\omega$:

$$\frac{dV_s}{dz} = -(R + j\omega L)I_s \quad \text{(Eq. 11.8)}, \quad \frac{dI_s}{dz} = -(G + j\omega C)V_s \quad \text{(Eq. 11.9)}$$

Differentiating Eq. (11.8) and substituting Eq. (11.9):

$$\frac{d^2V_s}{dz^2} = -(R + j\omega L)\frac{dI_s}{dz} = (R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)V_s$$ $$\mathbf{\frac{d^2V_s}{dz^2} - \gamma^2 V_s = 0} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.10)}, \quad \mathbf{\frac{d^2I_s}{dz^2} - \gamma^2 I_s = 0} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.12)}$$ $$\text{where } \mathbf{\gamma = \alpha + j\beta = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)}} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.11)}$$

General Solutions, Traveling Waves & Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$)

The general solutions of the linear homogeneous second-order differential equations are linear combinations of forward ($+z$) and backward ($-z$) propagating waves:

$$V_s(z) = V_0^+\,e^{-\gamma z} + V_0^-\,e^{+\gamma z} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.15)}$$ $$I_s(z) = I_0^+\,e^{-\gamma z} + I_0^-\,e^{+\gamma z} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.16)}$$

Transforming back to the time domain yields the instantaneous wave expression:

$$V(z,t) = \operatorname{Re}\left[V_s(z)e^{j\omega t}\right] = V_0^+\,e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t - \beta z) + V_0^-\,e^{+\alpha z}\cos(\omega t + \beta z) \quad \text{(Eq. 11.17)}$$

Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$) Formulation

The characteristic impedance $Z_0$ is defined as the ratio of positively traveling voltage wave to current wave at any point on the line:

$$Z_0 = \frac{V_0^+}{I_0^+} = -\frac{V_0^-}{I_0^-} = \frac{R + j\omega L}{\gamma} = \frac{\gamma}{G + j\omega C} = \mathbf{\sqrt{\frac{R + j\omega L}{G + j\omega C}} = R_0 + jX_0} \quad \text{(Eq. 11.18, 11.19)}$$ $$\text{Characteristic Admittance: } Y_0 = \frac{1}{Z_0}$$

💡 Lucid Understanding: The 3 Crucial Invariants (Page 16) Expand
  1. Zero Signal Dispersion: In a distortionless line, phase velocity $u_p = \frac{\omega}{\beta} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$ is strictly independent of frequency because $\beta = \omega\sqrt{LC}$ is a purely linear function of $\omega$. Signal harmonics arrive simultaneously, avoiding wave shape smearing!
  2. Impedance & Velocity Parity: Both phase velocity $u_p$ and characteristic impedance $Z_0$ have identical mathematical values for lossless and distortionless lines ($u_p = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}$).
  3. Lossless vs Distortionless Distinction: Every lossless line is distortionless ($\alpha = 0$), but a distortionless line is not necessarily lossless (it suffers finite attenuation $\alpha = \sqrt{RG} > 0$). In power grids, lossless lines minimize waste; in telephone lines, distortionless lines preserve vocal intelligibility.

Example 11.1: Air Line Inductance & Capacitance Evaluation

Example 11.1 Air Transmission Line Analysis
An air line has a characteristic impedance of $70\ \Omega$ and a phase constant of $3\text{ rad/m}$ at $100\text{ MHz}$. Calculate the inductance per meter and the capacitance per meter of the line.
Step 1 · Physical Identification & Parameter Extraction

An air line uses atmospheric air as dielectric ($\sigma \approx 0, \mu_r \approx 1, \varepsilon_r \approx 1$) with low-loss copper conductors ($\sigma_c \to \infty$). Hence, it acts as a lossless line ($R = 0 = G, \alpha = 0$):

$Z_0 = R_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = 70\ \Omega \quad \text{--- (11.1.1)}$
$\beta = \omega\sqrt{LC} = 3\text{ rad/m} \quad \text{--- (11.1.2)}$
$f = 100\text{ MHz} = 100 \times 10^6\text{ Hz} \implies \omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 10^8\text{ rad/s}$
Step 2 · Capacitance per Meter ($C$)

Dividing Eq. (11.1.1) by Eq. (11.1.2) yields:

$$\frac{R_0}{\beta} = \frac{\sqrt{L/C}}{\omega\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\omega C}$$ $$C = \frac{\beta}{\omega R_0} = \frac{3}{(2\pi \times 100 \times 10^6)(70)} = \frac{3}{1.4\pi \times 10^{10}} = \mathbf{6.8209 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F/m}} = \mathbf{68.2\text{ pF/m}}$$
Step 3 · Inductance per Meter ($L$)

From Eq. (11.1.1), squaring both sides gives $L = R_0^2 C$:

$$L = R_0^2 C = (70)^2 \times (68.209 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F/m}) = 4900 \times (68.209 \times 10^{-12}) = \mathbf{3.3422 \times 10^{-7}\text{ H/m}} = \mathbf{334.2\text{ nH/m}}$$

Independent Cross-Check: $u_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(3.3422 \times 10^{-7})(6.8209 \times 10^{-11})}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2.2797 \times 10^{-17}}} = \frac{1}{4.7746 \times 10^{-9}} \approx 2.094 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$. Check via $\omega/\beta = \frac{2\pi \times 10^8}{3} \approx 2.0944 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ (Exact match!).

FINAL RESULTS (Example 11.1):
Capacitance per meter: $C = 68.2\text{ pF/m}$
Inductance per meter: $L = 334.2\text{ nH/m}$

Example 11.2: Complete Distortionless Line Parameter Extraction

Example 11.2 Distortionless Line Parameter Extraction
A distortionless line has $Z_0 = 60\ \Omega$, $\alpha = 20\text{ mNp/m}$, $u = 0.6c$, where $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum ($3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$). Find $R, L, G, C$, and $\lambda$ at $100\text{ MHz}$.
Step 1 · Extract Given Constants
$Z_0 = 60\ \Omega, \quad \alpha = 20\text{ mNp/m} = 0.020\text{ Np/m}$
$u_p = 0.6c = 0.6 \times (3 \times 10^8) = 1.8 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$
$f = 100\text{ MHz} = 10^8\text{ Hz}$
Step 2 · Resistance per Meter ($R$)

For a distortionless line, $RC = GL \iff \frac{C}{L} = \frac{G}{R}$. Therefore:

$$\alpha = \sqrt{RG} = \sqrt{R \cdot \left(\frac{RC}{L}\right)} = R\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} = \frac{R}{Z_0}$$ $$R = \alpha\,Z_0 = (20 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Np/m}) \times (60\ \Omega) = \mathbf{1.20\ \Omega/\text{m}}$$
Step 3 · Inductance per Meter ($L$)

Since $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}$ and $u_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$, dividing the two equations gives:

$$\frac{Z_0}{u_p} = \frac{\sqrt{L/C}}{1/\sqrt{LC}} = L$$ $$L = \frac{Z_0}{u_p} = \frac{60\ \Omega}{1.8 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = \frac{60}{1.8 \times 10^8} = \mathbf{3.3333 \times 10^{-7}\text{ H/m}} = \mathbf{333.3\text{ nH/m}}$$
Step 4 · Capacitance per Meter ($C$)

Multiplying $u_p$ and $Z_0$:

$$u_p\,Z_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \frac{1}{C}$$ $$C = \frac{1}{u_p\,Z_0} = \frac{1}{(1.8 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}) \times 60\ \Omega} = \frac{1}{1.08 \times 10^{10}} = \mathbf{9.2593 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F/m}} = \mathbf{92.59\text{ pF/m}}$$
Step 5 · Conductance per Meter ($G$) & Wavelength ($\lambda$)

Using the distortionless condition $G = \frac{RC}{L} = \frac{\alpha}{Z_0}$:

$$G = \frac{\alpha}{Z_0} = \frac{20 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Np/m}}{60\ \Omega} = \frac{1}{3000}\text{ S/m} = \mathbf{3.3333 \times 10^{-4}\text{ S/m}} = \mathbf{333.3\ \mu\text{S/m}}$$

Wavelength on the line at $f = 100\text{ MHz}$:

$$\lambda = \frac{u_p}{f} = \frac{1.8 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}}{100 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{1.80\text{ m}}$$
FINAL RESULTS (Example 11.2):
$R = 1.20\ \Omega/\text{m}, \quad L = 333.3\text{ nH/m}, \quad C = 92.59\text{ pF/m}, \quad G = 333.3\ \mu\text{S/m}, \quad \lambda = 1.80\text{ m}$

Practice Exercise 11.2: Telephone Line Complex Analysis at 1 kHz

Practice Exercise 11.2 Telephone Audio Transmission Line
A telephone line has $R = 30\ \Omega/\text{km}$, $L = 100\text{ mH/km}$, $G = 0$, and $C = 20\ \mu\text{F/km}$. At $f = 1\text{ kHz}$, obtain: (a) The characteristic impedance of the line ($Z_0$) (b) The propagation constant ($\gamma$) (c) The phase velocity ($u_p$).
Step 1 · Parameter Normalization to SI Units (/meter)
$R = 30\ \Omega/\text{km} = 0.030\ \Omega/\text{m}$
$L = 100\text{ mH/km} = 100 \times 10^{-3}\text{ H}/1000\text{ m} = 10^{-4}\text{ H/m} = 0.10\text{ mH/m}$
$G = 0\text{ S/m}$
$C = 20\ \mu\text{F/km} = 20 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F}/1000\text{ m} = 2 \times 10^{-8}\text{ F/m} = 20\text{ nF/m}$
$f = 1\text{ kHz} = 1000\text{ Hz} \implies \omega = 2\pi f = 2000\pi \approx 6283.1853\text{ rad/s}$
Step 2 · Complex Impedance & Admittance per Meter

Series impedance per meter:

$$Z = R + j\omega L = 0.030 + j(6283.1853 \times 10^{-4}) = 0.030 + j0.6283185\ \Omega/\text{m}$$ $$|Z| = \sqrt{(0.030)^2 + (0.6283185)^2} = \sqrt{0.0009 + 0.394784} = \sqrt{0.395684} \approx 0.629034\ \Omega/\text{m}$$ $$\theta_Z = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{0.6283185}{0.030}\right) = \tan^{-1}(20.94395) \approx 87.2657^\circ$$

Shunt admittance per meter ($G = 0$):

$$Y = G + j\omega C = 0 + j(6283.1853 \times 2 \times 10^{-8}) = j1.256637 \times 10^{-4}\text{ S/m}$$ $$|Y| = 1.256637 \times 10^{-4}\text{ S/m}, \quad \theta_Y = 90.0000^\circ$$
Step 3 · Part (a) Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$)
$$|Z_0| = \sqrt{\frac{|Z|}{|Y|}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.629034}{1.256637 \times 10^{-4}}} = \sqrt{5005.694} = \mathbf{70.7509\ \Omega} \approx \mathbf{70.75\ \Omega}$$ $$\theta_{Z0} = \frac{\theta_Z - \theta_Y}{2} = \frac{87.2657^\circ - 90^\circ}{2} = \frac{-2.7343^\circ}{2} = \mathbf{-1.367^\circ}$$ $$\mathbf{Z_0 = 70.75\,\angle -1.367^\circ\ \Omega} = (70.730 - j1.688)\ \Omega$$
Step 4 · Part (b) Propagation Constant ($\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$)
$$|\gamma| = \sqrt{|Z| \cdot |Y|} = \sqrt{0.629034 \times (1.256637 \times 10^{-4})} = \sqrt{7.90468 \times 10^{-5}} = 8.89083 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^{-1}$$ $$\theta_\gamma = \frac{\theta_Z + \theta_Y}{2} = \frac{87.2657^\circ + 90^\circ}{2} = \frac{177.2657^\circ}{2} = 88.63285^\circ$$ $$\alpha = |\gamma|\cos(\theta_\gamma) = (8.89083 \times 10^{-3}) \cos(88.63285^\circ) = \mathbf{2.1213 \times 10^{-4}\text{ Np/m}}$$ $$\beta = |\gamma|\sin(\theta_\gamma) = (8.89083 \times 10^{-3}) \sin(88.63285^\circ) = \mathbf{8.8883 \times 10^{-3}\text{ rad/m}}$$ $$\mathbf{\gamma = (2.121 \times 10^{-4} + j\,8.888 \times 10^{-3})\text{ m}^{-1}}$$
Step 5 · Part (c) Phase Velocity ($u_p$)
$$u_p = \frac{\omega}{\beta} = \frac{6283.1853\text{ rad/s}}{8.8883 \times 10^{-3}\text{ rad/m}} = \mathbf{7.06905 \times 10^5\text{ m/s}} \approx \mathbf{7.069 \times 10^5\text{ m/s}}$$

Notice: At 1 kHz audio, $u_p \approx 707\text{ km/s}$ is much slower than the speed of light in vacuum due to the large shunt capacitance and series inductance ($u_p \approx 0.24\% c$).

FINAL RESULTS (Practice Exercise 11.2):
(a) Characteristic Impedance: $Z_0 = 70.75\,\angle -1.367^\circ\ \Omega$
(b) Propagation Constant: $\gamma = (2.121 \times 10^{-4} + j\,8.888 \times 10^{-3})\text{ m}^{-1}$
(c) Phase Velocity: $u_p = 7.069 \times 10^5\text{ m/s}$
