Magnetic Fields and Maxwell Equations

Exam notes based directly on the supplied lecture PDF. Source wording is retained without paraphrasing.

1. Magnetic Monopole does not exist...Gauss’s Law

Unlike electric flux lines, magnetic flux lines always close upon themselves. This is because it is not possible to have isolated magnetic poles (or magnetic charges).

Ψ = ∫S B · dS
∮S B · dS = 0
∮S B · dS = ∫V ∇ · B dv = 0
∇ · B = 0

2. Magnetic Boundary Conditions

∮S B · dS = 0
B1n ΔS − B2n ΔS = 0
B1n = B2n
μ1H1n = μ2H2n

It shows that the normal component of B is continuous at the boundary. It also shows that the normal component of H is discontinuous at the boundary; H undergoes some change at the interface.

∮L H · dl = I
K · Δw = H1t · Δw + H1n · Δh/2 + H2n · Δh/2
−H2t · Δw − H2n · Δh/2 − H1n · Δh/2
H1t − H2t = K

This shows that the tangential component of H is also discontinuous.

3. Lorentz Force

F = qv × B
F = qE + qv × B

4. Faraday’s law

According to Faraday’s experiments, a static magnetic field produces no current flow; but in a closed circuit, a time-varying field produces an induced voltage (called electromotive force or simply emf) that causes a flow of current.

Vemf = −dλ/dt = −N dΨ/dt
emf = ∮L E · dl = −d/dt ∫S B · dS
Vemf = ∮L E · dl = −∫S (∂B/∂t) · dS
∫S (∇ × E) · dS = −∫S (∂B/∂t) · dS
∇ × E = −∂B/∂t

5. Ampere’s Law

Ampère’s circuit law states that the line integral of H around a closed path is the same as the net current Ienc enclosed by the path.

∮L H · dl = Ienc

By applying Stokes’s theorem to the left-hand side

Ienc = ∮L H · dl = ∫S (∇ × H) · dS
Ienc = ∫S J · dS
∇ × H = J

observe that ∇ × H = J ≠ 0; that is, a magnetostatic field is not conservative.

6. Displacement Current – Modification of Maxwell’s equation

For static EM fields, we recall that

∇ × H = J

divergence of the curl of any vector field is identically zero

∇ · (∇ × H) = 0 = ∇ · J

The continuity of current in however, requires that

∇ · J = −∂ρv/∂t ≠ 0

Thus eqs. are obviously incompatible for time-varying conditions.

∇ × H = J + Jd
∇ · (∇ × H) = 0 = ∇ · J + ∇ · Jd
∇ · Jd = −∇ · J = ∂ρv/∂t = ∂/∂t (∇ · D) = ∇ · ∂D/∂t
Jd = ∂D/∂t
∇ × H = J + ∂D/∂t

This is Maxwell’s equation (based on Ampère’s circuit law) for a time-varying field. The term Jd = ∂D/∂t is known as displacement current density and J is the conduction current density (J = σE). The insertion of Jd into eq. above was one of the major contributions of Maxwell.

Without the term Jd, the propagation of electromagnetic waves (e.g., radio or TV waves) would be impossible. At low frequencies, Jd is usually neglected compared with J. However, at radio frequencies, the two terms are comparable.
