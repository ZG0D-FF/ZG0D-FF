1. Transmission Lines Overview

A transmission line consists of two or more parallel conductors used to transmit electromagnetic energy and signals between a source (generator, transmitter) and a load (antenna, receiver, circuit). Typical physical configurations include:

  • Coaxial cable: inner conductor surrounded by a concentric dielectric sheath and metallic outer braid shield. Widely used for broadband RF.
  • Two-wire line (twin-lead): two parallel insulated cylindrical conductors.
  • Planar / microstrip line: thin metallic strip separated from a conducting ground plane by a dielectric substrate. Standard for high-frequency microwave integrated circuits (MMICs).

Distributed vs. lumped circuits: when the signal frequency is high enough that the physical line length $l \ge \lambda/20$, voltage and current vary along the spatial dimension $z$, requiring distributed-parameter analysis ($R, L, G, C$ per unit length).

2. Distributed Parameters & Telegrapher's Equations

A differential segment of length $\Delta z$ is modeled with four distributed parameters:

  • $R$ – series resistance per unit length ($\Omega/\text{m}$), due to finite conductor conductivity $\sigma_c$.
  • $L$ – series loop inductance per unit length ($\text{H/m}$), due to magnetic flux linkage.
  • $G$ – shunt conductance per unit length ($\text{S/m}$), due to dielectric leakage current.
  • $C$ – shunt capacitance per unit length ($\text{F/m}$) between the two conductors.

Time-harmonic telegrapher's equations

$$-\frac{dV(z)}{dz}=(R+j\omega L)\,I(z)$$
$$-\frac{dI(z)}{dz}=(G+j\omega C)\,V(z)$$

Wave equations for voltage and current

Differentiating again gives second-order wave equations:

$$\frac{d^2V(z)}{dz^2}-\gamma^2V(z)=0,\qquad \frac{d^2I(z)}{dz^2}-\gamma^2I(z)=0$$

Complex propagation constant ($\gamma$)

$$\gamma=\alpha+j\beta=\sqrt{(R+j\omega L)(G+j\omega C)}$$

Here $\alpha$ is the attenuation constant ($\text{Np/m}$ or $\text{dB/m}$) and $\beta$ is the phase constant ($\text{rad/m}$).

Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)

Ratio of the forward-traveling voltage wave to the forward-traveling current wave:

$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{R+j\omega L}{G+j\omega C}}\ \ [\Omega]$$

4. Reflection Coefficient & VSWR

Voltage reflection coefficient at the load ($\Gamma_L$)

The ratio of the reflected voltage wave amplitude to the incident voltage wave amplitude at the load terminal $z=0$:

$$\Gamma_L=\frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+}=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}=|\Gamma_L|\,e^{j\theta_\Gamma}$$

Special terminations

  • Matched load ($Z_L=Z_0$): $\Gamma_L=0$. No reflections, all power delivered to the load (flat line).
  • Short circuit ($Z_L=0$): $\Gamma_L=-1=1e^{j\pi}$. Complete reflection, voltage node at the load.
  • Open circuit ($Z_L=\infty$): $\Gamma_L=+1=1e^{j0}$. Complete reflection, voltage antinode at the load.
  • Purely reactive load ($Z_L=\pm jX$): $|\Gamma_L|=1$. Total reflection of power.

Voltage standing wave ratio (VSWR, $s$)

The ratio of maximum to minimum voltage amplitude along the line:

$$s=\text{VSWR}=\frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}}=\frac{1+|\Gamma_L|}{1-|\Gamma_L|}\qquad(1\le s\lt\infty)$$

Inverting for the reflection coefficient magnitude:

$$|\Gamma_L|=\frac{s-1}{s+1}$$

5. Input Impedance & Impedance Matching

General input impedance equation

At a distance $l$ from a load $Z_L$ on a lossless line with characteristic impedance $Z_0$:

$$Z_{\text{in}}(l)=Z_0\left[\frac{Z_L+jZ_0\tan(\beta l)}{Z_0+jZ_L\tan(\beta l)}\right]$$

Extreme impedances along the line

$$Z_{\text{in,max}}=sZ_0,\qquad Z_{\text{in,min}}=\frac{Z_0}{s}$$

6. Transit Time Effect

The transit time delay $t_{\text{transit}}$ experienced by an electromagnetic wave traveling across a line of length $l$ at phase velocity $v_p$:

$$t_{\text{transit}}=\frac{l}{v_p}$$

Transit time effects become significant when the line length is a substantial fraction of the wavelength:

$$l\ge\frac{\lambda}{20}\iff t_{\text{transit}}\ge\frac{T}{20}$$

When this threshold is crossed, ordinary lumped-circuit Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws (KVL/KCL) fail, and distributed transmission-line wave analysis is required.

7. Rectangular Waveguides & Modes

A rectangular waveguide is a hollow metallic pipe of rectangular cross-section (broad wall dimension $a$, narrow wall dimension $b$, with $a \gt b$) used to guide microwave and millimeter-wave signals via successive reflections from the metallic walls.

Supported modes

  • Transverse electric (TE) modes ($E_z=0,\ H_z\ne0$): the electric field is entirely transverse to the direction of propagation ($z$).
  • Transverse magnetic (TM) modes ($H_z=0,\ E_z\ne0$): the magnetic field is entirely transverse to the direction of propagation ($z$).
  • TEM mode is not supported: a hollow single-conductor waveguide cannot support transverse electromagnetic waves ($E_z=0,\ H_z=0$). A TEM field would need a transverse potential obeying Laplace's equation, and with a single closed conductor at one potential that field is zero everywhere.

Boundary conditions on the metallic walls

$$E_t=0\quad\text{(tangential electric field vanishes on conductor walls)}$$
$$B_n=0\quad\text{(normal magnetic flux vanishes on conductor walls)}$$

8. Cutoff Frequency, Wavelength & Dominant Mode

Cutoff frequency ($f_{c,mn}$)

The frequency below which waves are rapidly attenuated (evanescent) and above which propagation occurs:

$$f_{c,mn}=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2}$$

Here $m, n$ are integers giving the number of half-wave field variations along dimensions $a$ and $b$.

Cutoff wavelength ($\lambda_{c,mn}$)

$$\lambda_{c,mn}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2}}$$

Dominant mode ($\text{TE}_{10}$)

The dominant mode has the lowest cutoff frequency (longest cutoff wavelength). For a standard rectangular waveguide with $a \gt b$:

$$\text{Dominant mode: }\mathbf{TE}_{10}\quad(m=1,\ n=0)$$
$$f_{c,10}=\frac{c}{2a},\qquad\lambda_{c,10}=2a$$

Broad wall requirement: for the dominant $\text{TE}_{10}$ mode to propagate at operating frequency $f$ (with $\lambda_0=c/f$): $\ f\gt f_c\iff a\gt\dfrac{\lambda_0}{2}$

Degenerate modes

Modes that share identical cutoff frequencies are called degenerate modes. In a rectangular guide, $\text{TE}_{11}$ and $\text{TM}_{11}$ are always degenerate.

Guided wavelength ($\lambda_g$)

The wavelength of the guided wave measured along the longitudinal axis of propagation:

$$\lambda_g=\frac{\lambda_0}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{f_c}{f}\right)^2}}=\frac{\lambda_0}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_c}\right)^2}}\gt\lambda_0$$
  • As the operating frequency approaches cutoff ($f\to f_c$): $\lambda_g\to\infty$.
  • At very high frequencies ($f\gg f_c$): $\lambda_g\approx\lambda_0$.

9. Phase Velocity ($v_p$) vs. Group Velocity ($v_g$)

Phase velocity ($v_p$)

The velocity at which a point of constant phase advances through the waveguide:

$$v_p=\frac{\omega}{\beta}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{f_c}{f}\right)^2}}\gt c$$

$v_p$ is always strictly greater than the speed of light $c$. This does not violate relativity because phase velocity carries no energy or information.

Group velocity ($v_g$)

The velocity at which electromagnetic energy and information travel:

$$v_g=\frac{d\omega}{d\beta}=c\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{f_c}{f}\right)^2}\lt c$$

Fundamental velocity product relation

$$v_p\cdot v_g=c^2$$

10. Core Exam Derivations

Derivation 1: Transmission line equations from distributed parameters

Apply KVL and KCL to an elemental line length $\Delta z$:

$$\text{KVL: }v(z,t)-R\Delta z\,i(z,t)-L\Delta z\frac{\partial i(z,t)}{\partial t}-v(z+\Delta z,t)=0$$

Dividing by $\Delta z\to0$ in sinusoidal steady state:

$$\frac{dV(z)}{dz}=-(R+j\omega L)\,I(z)$$
$$\text{KCL: }i(z,t)-G\Delta z\,v(z+\Delta z,t)-C\Delta z\frac{\partial v(z+\Delta z,t)}{\partial t}-i(z+\Delta z,t)=0$$
$$\frac{dI(z)}{dz}=-(G+j\omega C)\,V(z)$$
Derivation 2: VSWR in terms of the load reflection coefficient

Total voltage wave along the line, with $z'$ measured from the load: $V(z')=V_0^+e^{j\beta z'}\left(1+\Gamma_Le^{-j2\beta z'}\right)$.

$$V_{\max}=|V_0^+|\,(1+|\Gamma_L|)$$
$$V_{\min}=|V_0^+|\,(1-|\Gamma_L|)$$
$$\text{VSWR}=s=\frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}}=\frac{1+|\Gamma_L|}{1-|\Gamma_L|}$$
Derivation 3: Phase velocity $v_p \gt c$ in a waveguide

Dispersion relation in a waveguide: $\beta=\sqrt{\omega^2\mu\varepsilon-k_c^2}=\dfrac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}$.

$$v_p=\frac{\omega}{\beta}=\frac{\omega}{\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}}$$

Since $\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}\lt1$ for propagating modes ($f\gt f_c$), the denominator is less than 1, proving $v_p\gt c$.
