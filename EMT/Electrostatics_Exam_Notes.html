1. Coulomb’s Law

Coulomb's Law states that the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, acting along the line joining them.

Scalar Formulation
$$F = \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2} \quad \text{[Newtons (N)]}$$
Permittivity of free space: $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \text{ F/m} \approx \frac{10^{-9}}{36\pi} \text{ F/m}$
Coulomb constant: $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$
Vector Formulation (Point 1 to Point 2)

Force $\mathbf{F}_{12}$ exerted by charge $Q_1$ located at $\mathbf{r}_1$ on charge $Q_2$ located at $\mathbf{r}_2$:

$$\mathbf{F}_{12} = \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_{12}^2}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{R12} = \frac{Q_1 Q_2 (\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 |\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1|^3}$$

By Newton's Third Law: $\mathbf{F}_{21} = -\mathbf{F}_{12}$. Like charges repel ($\mathbf{F}_{12}$ in direction of $\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1$); opposite charges attract.

Principle of Superposition

For $N$ point charges $Q_1, Q_2, \dots, Q_N$ acting on charge $Q$ at position $\mathbf{r}$:

$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{Q_k(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_k)}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_k|^3}$$

2. Electric Field Intensity ($\mathbf{E}$)

The electric field intensity $\mathbf{E}$ is the force per unit positive test charge placed at a point in the field as the test charge approaches zero:

$$\mathbf{E} = \lim_{q\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{F}}{q} \quad \text{[V/m or N/C]}$$
Point Charge Source

Field at field point $\mathbf{r}$ due to source charge $Q$ at source point $\mathbf{r}'$:

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_R = \frac{Q(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}')}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|^3}$$
Discrete System of Charges
$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{Q_k(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_k)}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_k|^3}$$

3. Continuous Charge Distributions (Applications)

Summary of Differential Charge Elements
Distribution TypeCharge DensityDifferential ChargeElectric Field $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$
Line Charge$\rho_L$ (C/m)$dQ = \rho_L \, dl'$$\int_L \frac{\rho_L \, dl'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_R$
Surface Charge$\rho_S$ (C/m²)$dQ = \rho_S \, dS'$$\int_S \frac{\rho_S \, dS'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_R$
Volume Charge$\rho_v$ (C/m³)$dQ = \rho_v \, dv'$$\int_V \frac{\rho_v \, dv'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_R$
Infinite Line Charge (Along Z-Axis)

Consider an infinite uniform line charge of density $\rho_L$ extending along the z-axis from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$:

$$\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_L}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 \rho}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_\rho \quad \text{[Purely radial, independent of } z \text{ and } \phi\text{]}$$
💡 For a finite line segment between subtended angles $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$:
$\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_L}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \rho}\left[(\sin\alpha_2 - \sin\alpha_1)\hat{\mathbf{a}}_\rho + (\cos\alpha_1 - \cos\alpha_2)\hat{\mathbf{a}}_z\right]$
Infinite Sheet of Charge (XY-Plane)

Uniform surface charge density $\rho_S$ on an infinite plane:

$$\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_S}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_n$$

Where $\hat{\mathbf{a}}_n$ is the unit normal vector pointing away from the sheet. Note that $|\mathbf{E}|$ is completely independent of the distance from the sheet!

Parallel Plate Capacitor (Two Oppositely Charged Sheets)
$$\mathbf{E} = \begin{cases} \frac{\rho_S}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_n & \text{between the plates} \\ 0 & \text{outside the plates} \end{cases}$$

4. Gauss’s Law & Electric Flux Density ($\mathbf{D}$)

Electric Flux & Displacement Field

Electric flux density $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E} \text{ [C/m}^2\text{]}$. Total flux $\Psi$ passing through closed surface $S$:

$$\Psi = \oint_S \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enclosed}}$$
Differential / Point Form (Maxwell's 1st Equation)
$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v \iff \oint_S \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D})\,dv = Q_{\text{enc}}$$
Standard Exam Problem: Uniformly Charged Sphere (Radius $a$, Density $\rho_0$)

Case 1: Inside the sphere ($r \le a$)

Construct spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$:

$$\oint \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = D_r (4\pi r^2) = Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_0^r \rho_0(4\pi r'^2)\,dr' = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_0$$
$$D_r = \frac{\rho_0 r}{3} \implies \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_0 r}{3\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_r \quad (r \le a)$$

Case 2: Outside the sphere ($r \ge a$)

Total charge $Q = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \rho_0$:

$$\oint \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = D_r (4\pi r^2) = Q$$
$$D_r = \frac{\rho_0 a^3}{3r^2} = \frac{Q}{4\pi r^2} \implies \mathbf{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_r \quad (r \ge a)$$

5. Electric Potential Difference ($V$)

Work Done & Potential Definition

Work done in moving charge $Q$ from point $A$ to point $B$ against electric field $\mathbf{E}$:

$$W = -Q \int_A^B \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} \implies V_{AB} = \frac{W}{Q} = V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$$
Conservative Nature & Maxwell’s Equation (Static Field)

In an electrostatic field, the work done along any closed path is zero (path-independent):

$$\oint_L \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0 \iff \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0 \quad \text{[Maxwell's 2nd Electrostatic Equation]}$$
Electric Field from Potential Gradient
$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$$
Poisson’s & Laplace’s Equations

Substituting $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E} = -\varepsilon\nabla V$ into $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_v$:

$$\nabla^2 V = -\frac{\rho_v}{\varepsilon} \quad \text{[Poisson's Equation]}$$ $$\nabla^2 V = 0 \quad \text{[Laplace's Equation, for charge-free region } \rho_v = 0\text{]}$$

6. Dielectrics & Polarization

An applied electric field $\mathbf{E}$ creates or aligns microscopic electric dipole moments within a dielectric material, inducing polarization vector $\mathbf{P}$:

$$\mathbf{P} = \lim_{\Delta v\to 0}\frac{\sum_{k} \mathbf{p}_k}{\Delta v} \quad \text{[C/m}^2\text{]}$$
Bound Charges (Polarization Charges)
$$\rho_{ps} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{a}}_n \quad \text{[Surface bound charge density]}$$ $$\rho_{pv} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P} \quad \text{[Volume bound charge density]}$$
Electric Flux Density in Dielectrics
$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P} = \varepsilon_0 (1 + \chi_e)\mathbf{E} = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \mathbf{E} = \varepsilon \mathbf{E}$$
$\chi_e = \varepsilon_r - 1$ = Electric Susceptibility (dimensionless)
$\varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}$ = Relative Permittivity / Dielectric Constant
Dielectric Breakdown & Dielectric Strength

When the electric field exceeds the dielectric strength of the insulator (approx. $3 \times 10^6 \text{ V/m}$ for dry air), electrons are stripped from atomic bonds, causing permanent avalanche breakdown and high conductivity.

8. Charge Conservation & Relaxation Time ($\tau_r$)

Continuity Equation

Charge conservation states that current flowing out of a closed surface equals the negative rate of change of enclosed charge:

$$I_{\text{out}} = \oint_S \mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{dQ_{\text{enc}}}{dt} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_V \rho_v \, dv$$ $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial \rho_v}{\partial t} \quad \text{[Point Form of Continuity Equation]}$$
Charge Relaxation Time ($\tau_r$)

Using Ohm's law $\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$ and Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_v}{\varepsilon}$:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = \nabla\cdot(\sigma\mathbf{E}) = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\rho_v$$ $$\frac{\partial\rho_v}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\rho_v = 0 \implies \rho_v(t) = \rho_{v0} e^{-t/\tau_r}$$
$$\tau_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} \quad \text{[Relaxation Time in seconds]}$$
💡 For good conductors (Copper): $\tau_r \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-19} \text{ s}$ (free charges migrate to surface instantaneously).
For good insulators (Quartz): $\tau_r \approx 51.2 \text{ days}$ (charges remain trapped in the interior).

9. Electrostatic Boundary Conditions

Boundary conditions specify how $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ change across the interface between two distinct media:

1. Tangential Component (From $\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$)
$$E_{t1} = E_{t2} \iff \frac{D_{t1}}{\varepsilon_1} = \frac{D_{t2}}{\varepsilon_2}$$

Conclusion: The tangential component of electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is always continuous across any interface.

2. Normal Component (From $\oint \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}$)
$$D_{n1} - D_{n2} = \rho_S \iff \varepsilon_1 E_{n1} - \varepsilon_2 E_{n2} = \rho_S$$

If no free surface charge exists at the interface ($\rho_S = 0$):

$$D_{n1} = D_{n2} \iff \varepsilon_1 E_{n1} = \varepsilon_2 E_{n2}$$

Conclusion: The normal component of $\mathbf{D}$ is continuous across a charge-free boundary; the normal component of $\mathbf{E}$ is discontinuous.

Law of Refraction for Field Lines
$$\frac{\tan\theta_1}{\tan\theta_2} = \frac{\varepsilon_{r1}}{\varepsilon_{r2}}$$

Where $\theta_1, \theta_2$ are the angles made by field vectors with the normal to the boundary.

10. Conductor Boundary Conditions

For a perfect electrical conductor under electrostatic equilibrium ($\sigma \to \infty$):

  • Inside the Conductor: $\mathbf{E} = 0$, $\mathbf{D} = 0$, $\rho_v = 0$. No electric field or volume charge can reside in the interior.
  • Equipotential Body: The entire conductor is at the same uniform potential ($V = \text{constant}$).
  • Tangential Field at Surface: $E_t = 0$. (No surface currents under statics).
  • Normal Field at Surface:
    $$D_n = \rho_S \implies E_n = \frac{\rho_S}{\varepsilon}$$
Summary Rule: Electrostatic field lines must always terminate or originate perpendicularly to a conductor's surface: $\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_S}{\varepsilon}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_n$.
