Question 01 Page 1 · Physical Classification

Nomenclature Justification: Transverse Electromagnetic (TEM) & Uniform Plane Waves

1. Justify the nomenclature of TEM wave and Uniform plane wave.

Transverse Electromagnetic (TEM) Wave Justification: Both electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and magnetic field $\mathbf{H}$ vectors are everywhere orthogonal (normal) to the wave propagation vector $\hat{a}_k$. Neither the electric field nor the magnetic field possesses any component along the direction of propagation:

$$E_z = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad H_z = 0 \implies \mathbf{E} \cdot \hat{a}_k = 0, \quad \mathbf{H} \cdot \hat{a}_k = 0$$

Uniform Plane Wave Justification: The wave is termed a plane wave because equiphase surfaces form infinite parallel planes perpendicular to the propagation vector ($z = \text{constant}$). It is termed uniform because the physical field vectors $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ have constant amplitude and direction throughout any transverse plane.

Question 02 Page 1 · Conductor Loss Physics

Description of Skin Effect & Practical Tubular Antennas

2. Describe Skin Effect.

Skin effect is the phenomenon whereby high-frequency alternating currents and electromagnetic fields rapidly decay as they penetrate into a conducting material, causing electric charges and current density to crowd near the outer perimeter.

$$\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}\text{ (m)}$$
Physical Behavior Underlying Cause Engineering Consequence
Exponential Inward Decay Faraday-induced longitudinal eddy currents destructively oppose current at the core and constructively reinforce current at the surface. At depth $d = \delta$, amplitude drops to $e^{-1} \approx 36.8\%$. At $d = 5\delta$, current drops to $< 0.67\%$.
Hollow Tubular Conductors Because the solid core of the wire carries virtually zero current at RF/microwave frequencies, core metal contributes only dead weight and cost without reducing resistance. Engineers construct outdoor television antennas, microwave waveguides, and RF inductors using lightweight hollow copper or aluminum tubes.
Question 03 Pages 1–2 · Conservation of Energy

Poynting's Theorem & Electromagnetic Power Flow

3. State Poynting's theorem.
Formal Theorem Statement
Poynting's theorem states that the net electromagnetic power flowing out of a closed surface bounding a volume $V$ is equal to the time rate of decrease in the total energy stored within the electric and magnetic fields inside $V$, minus the ohmic power dissipated as heat.
$$\oint_S (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \int_V \left[\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2 + \frac{1}{2}\mu H^2\right] dV - \int_V \sigma E^2\,dV$$

Where: $$\mathbf{\mathcal{P}} = \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H} \quad \text{(Poynting Vector, measured in }\text{Watts/m}^2\text{)}$$ $$\hat{a}_k = \hat{a}_E \times \hat{a}_H \quad \text{(Unit direction of power propagation)}$$

Question 04 Page 2 · Continuity Equation

Evaluation of Charge Relaxation Time ($\tau$)

4. What is relaxation time? Calculate relaxation time for charges placed in a material with the conductivity of $10^{-10}\text{ mhos/m}$ and relative permittivity of $5.5$.
Step 1 · Physical Definition & Continuity Derivation

From the continuity equation $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial \rho_v}{\partial t}$ and Ohm's law $\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{D}$:

$$\nabla \cdot \left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{D}\right) = -\frac{\partial \rho_v}{\partial t} \implies \frac{\partial \rho_v}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\rho_v = 0 \implies \rho_v(t) = \rho_0\,e^{-t/\tau_r}$$

Relaxation time ($\tau_r$): The time required for an excess free charge placed inside a conducting material to decay to $1/e$ ($\approx 36.79\%$) of its initial value due to ohmic conduction.

Step 2 · Step-by-Step Numerical Computation
$\sigma = 10^{-10}\text{ mho/m} = 10^{-10}\text{ S/m}$
$\varepsilon_r = 5.5, \quad \varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F/m}$
$\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 = 5.5 \times (8.854 \times 10^{-12}) = \mathbf{4.8697 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F/m}}$
$$\tau_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} = \frac{4.8697 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F/m}}{10^{-10}\text{ S/m}} = \mathbf{0.48697\text{ s}} \approx \mathbf{0.487\text{ s}}$$
FINAL RESULT (Q4):
Relaxation Time: $\tau = \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} = \mathbf{0.487\text{ seconds}}$
Question 05 Page 2 · Dissipation Factor

Evaluation of Loss Tangent ($\tan \delta$) at 10 MHz

5. State the significance of loss tangent. Determine the loss tangent of a medium at $10\text{ MHz}$ if the medium has a conductivity of $2.5\text{ S/m}$, and permittivity of $\varepsilon = 6\varepsilon_0$.
Step 1 · Physical Significance

The loss tangent $\tan \delta = \frac{J_c}{J_d} = \frac{\sigma}{\omega\varepsilon}$ indicates how lossy a dielectric medium is when subjected to an alternating electromagnetic field. It represents the ratio of conduction current density (ohmic dissipation) to displacement current density (energy storage).

Step 2 · Step-by-Step Calculation
$f = 10\text{ MHz} = 10^7\text{ Hz} \implies \omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 10^7\text{ rad/s} \approx 6.283185 \times 10^7\text{ rad/s}$
$\sigma = 2.5\text{ S/m}, \quad \varepsilon = 6\varepsilon_0 = 6 \times (8.854 \times 10^{-12}) = 5.3124 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F/m}$
$$\omega\varepsilon = (2\pi \times 10^7) \times (5.3124 \times 10^{-11}) = 3.3379 \times 10^{-3}\text{ S/m}$$ $$\tan \delta = \frac{\sigma}{\omega\varepsilon} = \frac{2.5}{3.3379 \times 10^{-3}} = \mathbf{748.97} \approx \mathbf{749}$$
Medium Verdict
Because $\tan \delta \approx 749 \gg 100$, conduction current dominates displacement current by almost three orders of magnitude. The medium behaves as an excellent Good Conductor at 10 MHz.
FINAL RESULT (Q5):
Loss Tangent: $\tan \delta = \frac{2.5}{0.003338} = \mathbf{748.97}$ (Medium is a Good Conductor)
Question 06 Page 3 · Master Matrix

Comparison of Wave Propagation Media and Characteristics

6. Compare different mediums of wave propagation with its characteristics.
Medium Physical Condition Main Wave Characteristics
Free Space $\sigma = 0, \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0, \mu = \mu_0$ Zero conduction loss; EM wave propagates unattenuated at $c_0 \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$; $\eta_0 \approx 377\ \Omega$.
Perfect / Lossless Dielectric $\sigma = 0, \varepsilon_r > 1, \mu_r \ge 1$ No conduction current ($\alpha = 0$); phase velocity compressed $u_p = c_0/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$; $\eta = \eta_0/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$.
Low-Loss Dielectric $\sigma \ll \omega\varepsilon \iff \tan \delta \ll 1$ Conduction current is much smaller than displacement current; slight attenuation $\alpha \approx \frac{\sigma}{2}\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}$; $\beta \approx \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$.
Lossy Dielectric $\sigma \approx \omega\varepsilon \iff \tan \delta \sim O(1)$ Both conduction and displacement currents are significant; wave suffers heavy exponential decay; $H$ lags $E$ by phase angle $\theta_\eta < 45^\circ$.
Good Conductor $\sigma \gg \omega\varepsilon \iff \tan \delta \gg 100$ Conduction current dominates completely; high attenuation $\alpha \approx \beta \approx \sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}$; tiny skin depth $\delta = 1/\alpha$; $\eta_c \angle 45^\circ$.
Question 07 Page 3 · Antenna Theory

Bi-Directional vs. Omni-Directional Antenna Patterns

7. Differentiate between bi-directional and omni-directional antenna.
Feature / Characteristic Bi-Directional Antenna Omni-Directional Antenna
Radiation Pattern Geometry Radiates or receives EM waves predominantly in two opposite directions. Resembles a figure-eight (8). Radiates or receives EM waves equally in all horizontal directions (uniform $360^\circ$ circular coverage in azimuth).
Directivity & Gain Energy is concentrated into two lobes, yielding higher directivity than omni-directional antennas. Energy is distributed uniformly in azimuth, yielding moderate directivity and doughnut-shaped 3D pattern.
Typical Application Point-to-point links along a single corridor (railway tunnels, bridges, linear highways). Mobile base stations, Wi-Fi routers, FM broadcast towers serving dispersed ground users.
Question 08 Page 3 · Cavity Resonators

Derivation of Resonant Frequency & Phase Velocity in Waveguide Cavities

8. Derive the expression for the resonating frequency and phase velocity in a waveguide.
Step 1 · Rectangular Cavity Wave Vector Separation

A rectangular waveguide cavity of dimensions $a \times b \times d$ closed with conducting walls enforces standing waves in all three coordinates $(x,y,z)$:

$$k_x = \frac{m\pi}{a}, \quad k_y = \frac{n\pi}{b}, \quad k_z = \frac{p\pi}{d} \quad (m, n, p \in \mathbb{Z})$$ $$k^2 = \omega^2\mu\varepsilon = k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2 = \left(\frac{m\pi}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{p\pi}{d}\right)^2$$
Step 2 · Resonant Frequency Formulation ($f_r$)

Solving for resonant frequency $f_{r,mnp} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}$:

$$\mathbf{f_{r,mnp} = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}} \sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{p}{d}\right)^2}}$$

Dominant cavity mode for $a > b < d$: $\text{TE}_{101}$ mode ($m=1, n=0, p=1$).

Step 3 · Phase Velocity ($v_p$) in Waveguide

Inside the guiding conduit before end termination, $\beta = \frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}$. By definition of phase velocity:

$$\mathbf{v_p = \frac{\omega}{\beta} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{f_c}{f}\right)^2}} > c}$$
Questions 09 & 10 Pages 3–4 · Current Ratio Drill

Conduction vs. Displacement Current Density & Ratio Evaluation at 10 MHz

9. Describe conduction current density ($J_c$) and displacement current density ($J_d$).
10. Evaluate the ratio of $J_c/J_d$ at $10\text{ MHz}$ frequency for a medium that has $\sigma = 10^{-10}\text{ S/m}$, $\varepsilon_r = 2.7$, $\mu_r = 1$. State whether the medium is lossy or lossless dielectric.
1 · Definitions (Question 9)

Conduction Current Density ($J_c$): Produced by actual macroscopic drift of mobile free charge carriers (electrons or ions) through a conductor: $\mathbf{J}_c = \sigma \mathbf{E}\text{ (A/m}^2\text{)}$.

Displacement Current Density ($J_d$): Arises from the time-variation of electric displacement flux density; does not involve physical transport of charge: $\mathbf{J}_d = \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} = \varepsilon \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}\text{ (A/m}^2\text{)}$.

2 · Numerical Ratio Evaluation at 10 MHz (Question 10)
$\sigma = 10^{-10}\text{ S/m}, \quad \varepsilon_r = 2.7, \quad f = 10\text{ MHz} = 10^7\text{ Hz}$
$\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 = 2.7 \times (8.854 \times 10^{-12}) = \mathbf{2.3906 \times 10^{-11}\text{ F/m}}$
$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 10^7 \approx 6.283185 \times 10^7\text{ rad/s}$
$$\frac{J_c}{J_d} = \frac{\sigma}{\omega\varepsilon} = \frac{10^{-10}}{(6.283185 \times 10^7)(2.3906 \times 10^{-11})} = \frac{10^{-10}}{1.50206 \times 10^{-3}} = \mathbf{6.6575 \times 10^{-8}} \approx \mathbf{6.66 \times 10^{-8}}$$
Classification Verdict
Because $\frac{J_c}{J_d} \approx 6.66 \times 10^{-8} \ll 10^{-4}$, the displacement current exceeds conduction current by a factor of over 15 million! Conduction loss is virtually non-existent. Hence, the medium behaves as a lossless (or essentially perfect) dielectric.
FINAL RESULT (Q10):
$J_c / J_d = \mathbf{6.66 \times 10^{-8}}$ $\implies$ Medium is a Lossless Dielectric.
Question 11 Pages 4–5 · Wave Parameters

Propagation Constant $\gamma$ and Intrinsic Impedance $\eta_c$ Across Media

11. Discuss in relation to wave propagation in various mediums along with expression: (a) propagation constant (b) intrinsic impedance.
(a) Propagation Constant ($\gamma$)

Governs wave spatial evolution $E(z) = E_0 e^{-\gamma z} = E_0 e^{-\alpha z} e^{-j\beta z}$:

$$\gamma = \alpha + j\beta = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)}$$
  • $\alpha$ (attenuation constant, Np/m): determines exponential amplitude reduction rate.
  • $\beta$ (phase constant, rad/m): determines spatial phase rotation per meter ($\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$).
(b) Intrinsic Impedance ($\eta_c$)

Ratio of transverse electric field intensity to magnetic field intensity:

$$\eta_c = \frac{E}{H} = \sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon}} = |\eta_c|\,\angle \theta_\eta\ \Omega$$
  • Free space: $\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} \approx 377\ \Omega \approx 120\pi\ \Omega \angle 0^\circ$.
  • Lossless dielectric: $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon} = \eta_0 \sqrt{\mu_r/\varepsilon_r}\ \Omega \angle 0^\circ$.
  • Good conductor: $\eta_c \approx \sqrt{\frac{\omega\mu}{\sigma}}\,\angle 45^\circ\ \Omega$.
Question 12 Pages 5–6 · Field Vector Drill

Analytical Evaluation of Free Space Electric & Magnetic Fields

12. Consider the electric field propagating in free space is $\mathbf{E} = 10 \sin(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_x\text{ V/m}$. Evaluate the (a) attenuation constant (b) phase constant (c) direction of wave propagation (d) displacement current density ($\mathbf{J}_d$) (e) magnetic field ($\mathbf{H}$).
Step 1 · Parts (a), (b), (c) Wave Kinematics

Comparing with the canonical traveling wave $\mathbf{E}(z,t) = E_0 e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t - \beta z)\,\hat{a}_x$:

$E_0 = 10\text{ V/m}, \quad \omega = 10^3\text{ rad/s}, \quad \beta = 10\text{ rad/m}$
  • (a) Attenuation Constant: Free space is lossless ($\sigma = 0$) $\implies \mathbf{\alpha = 0\text{ Np/m}}$.
  • (b) Phase Constant: Directly from argument: $\mathbf{\beta = 10\text{ rad/m}}$.
  • (c) Direction of Propagation: Argument is $\omega t - \beta z = 10^3 t - 10z \implies \frac{dz}{dt} = +\frac{\omega}{\beta} > 0$. Wave propagates along the positive z-direction ($+\hat{a}_z$).
Step 2 · Part (d) Displacement Current Density ($\mathbf{J}_d$)
$$\mathbf{J}_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = (8.854 \times 10^{-12}) \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[10 \sin(10^3 t - 10z)\right]\hat{a}_x$$ $$\mathbf{J}_d = (8.854 \times 10^{-12}) \times (10 \times 10^3)\cos(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_x = \mathbf{8.854 \times 10^{-8} \cos(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_x\text{ A/m}^2}$$
Step 3 · Part (e) Magnetic Field Intensity ($\mathbf{H}$)

In free space, $\eta_0 = 120\pi \approx 376.99\ \Omega \approx 377\ \Omega$. By the TEM impedance cross-product relation:

$$\mathbf{H} = \frac{1}{\eta_0}(\hat{a}_k \times \mathbf{E}) = \frac{1}{377}\left[\hat{a}_z \times 10\sin(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_x\right]$$ $$\text{Since } \hat{a}_z \times \hat{a}_x = +\hat{a}_y: \quad \mathbf{H} = \frac{10}{377}\sin(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_y = \mathbf{0.026526 \sin(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_y\text{ A/m}}$$
FINAL RESULTS (Q12):
(a) $\alpha = 0\text{ Np/m}$
(b) $\beta = 10\text{ rad/m}$
(c) Direction: $+\hat{a}_z$
(d) $\mathbf{J}_d = 8.854 \times 10^{-8} \cos(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_x\text{ A/m}^2$
(e) $\mathbf{H} = 0.0265 \sin(10^3 t - 10z)\,\hat{a}_y\text{ A/m}$ ($26.5\text{ mA/m}$)
Question 13 Pages 6–8 · Opposing Waves Master Drill

Opposing Traveling Waves on a Transmission Line at 100 MHz

13. A transmission line has primary constants: $R = 0.2\ \Omega/\text{m}$, $L = 0.01\text{ H/m}$, $G = 0.02\text{ S/m}$, $C = 100\text{ pF/m}$. Operates at $100\text{ MHz}$.
Two voltage waves travel in opposite directions. At $x = 0, t = 0$:
• Forward wave has zero phase and amplitude $3\text{ V}$
• Backward wave has phase $\pi/4$ and amplitude $1\text{ V}$
Determine:
1. Instantaneous voltage and current at $x = 0.5\text{ m}$ and $t = 2\text{ ns}$.
2. Maximum possible voltage and current amplitudes at $x = 1\text{ m}$.
Step 1 · Line Parameters & Propagation Constant ($\gamma$)
$f = 100\text{ MHz} = 10^8\text{ Hz} \implies \omega = 2\pi \times 10^8 \approx 6.283185 \times 10^8\text{ rad/s}$
$\omega L = (6.283185 \times 10^8)(0.01) = 6.283185 \times 10^6\ \Omega/\text{m} \gg R = 0.2\ \Omega/\text{m}$
$\omega C = (6.283185 \times 10^8)(10^{-10}) = 0.06283185\text{ S/m}$
$Z = R + j\omega L \approx j6.283185 \times 10^6\ \Omega/\text{m} = 6.283185 \times 10^6 \angle 90^\circ$
$Y = G + j\omega C = 0.02 + j0.06283185\text{ S/m} = 0.065947 \angle 72.343^\circ\text{ S/m}$
$$\gamma = \sqrt{Z \cdot Y} = \sqrt{(6.283185 \times 10^6 \angle 90^\circ)(0.065947 \angle 72.343^\circ)} = \sqrt{4.14357 \times 10^5 \angle 162.343^\circ}$$ $$\gamma = 643.706\,\angle 81.171^\circ = \mathbf{98.81 + j\,636.08\text{ m}^{-1}}$$ $$\alpha \approx \mathbf{98.81\text{ Np/m}}, \quad \beta \approx \mathbf{636.08\text{ rad/m}}$$

Textbook low-loss high-frequency approximation check (Page 7 note): When $\omega L \gg R$, $\alpha \approx \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} + \frac{G}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \frac{0.2}{2}(10^{-4}) + \frac{0.02}{2}(10^4) = 10^{-5} + 100 \approx 100\text{ Np/m}$, and $\beta \approx \omega\sqrt{LC} = (6.283 \times 10^8)\sqrt{10^{-12}} = 628.32\text{ rad/m}$.

Step 2 · Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$)
$$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{Z}{Y}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.283185 \times 10^6 \angle 90^\circ}{0.065947 \angle 72.343^\circ}} = \sqrt{9.5276 \times 10^7 \angle 17.657^\circ} = \mathbf{9760.9\,\angle 8.83^\circ\ \Omega}$$ $$Z_0 \approx 9645 + j1498\ \Omega \quad (|Z_0| \approx 9.76\text{ k}\Omega)$$
Step 3 · Instantaneous Voltage & Current at $x = 0.5\text{ m}, t = 2\text{ ns}$

Phase argument at $t = 2\text{ ns} = 2 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}$:

$\omega t = (6.283185 \times 10^8)(2 \times 10^{-9}) = 1.2566\text{ rad} = 72.0^\circ$
$\beta x = (636.08)(0.5) = 318.04\text{ rad} = 50.618\pi \equiv 0.618\pi\text{ rad} = 111.27^\circ$
Forward wave: $V_f = 3 e^{-\alpha x} \cos(\omega t - \beta x) = 3 e^{-98.81 \times 0.5}\cos(72^\circ - 111.27^\circ) = 3 e^{-49.405}\cos(-39.27^\circ)$
Because of high attenuation $e^{-49.4} \approx 3.49 \times 10^{-22} \approx 0$!
Backward wave: $V_b = 1 e^{+\alpha x} \cos(\omega t + \beta x + \pi/4)$
$V_b(0.5, 2\text{ns}) = e^{49.405} \cos(72^\circ + 111.27^\circ + 45^\circ) = 2.86 \times 10^{21} \cos(228.27^\circ) = -1.90 \times 10^{21}\text{ V}$.

Total instantaneous expressions: $$V(x,t) = 3e^{-\alpha x}\cos(\omega t - \beta x) + 1e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega t + \beta x + \pi/4)$$ $$I(x,t) = \frac{V_f(x,t)}{|Z_0|}\cos(\theta_f - \theta_Z) - \frac{V_b(x,t)}{|Z_0|}\cos(\theta_b - \theta_Z)$$

Step 4 · Maximum Possible Voltage & Current Amplitudes at $x = 1\text{ m}$

At any point $x$, maximum voltage occurs when both waves constructively interfere in phase:

$$|V_{max}(x)| = |V_f(x)| + |V_b(x)| = \mathbf{3\,e^{-\alpha x} + 1\,e^{+\alpha x}}$$ $$\text{At } x = 1\text{ m}: \quad |V_{max}(1)| = 3\,e^{-98.81} + e^{+98.81} \approx \mathbf{e^{+98.81}\text{ V}} \quad (7.39 \times 10^{42}\text{ V})$$

Maximum possible current amplitude at $x = 1\text{ m}$:

$$|I_{max}(1)| = \frac{|V_{max}(1)|}{|Z_0|} = \frac{3\,e^{-\alpha} + e^{+\alpha}}{9760.9} \approx \mathbf{\frac{e^{+98.81}}{9760.9}\text{ A}} \quad (7.57 \times 10^{38}\text{ A})$$
FINAL RESULTS (Q13):
$\gamma = 98.81 + j\,636.08\text{ m}^{-1}, \quad Z_0 = 9760.9\,\angle 8.83^\circ\ \Omega$
$V_{max}(x=1\text{m}) = 3e^{-\alpha} + e^{\alpha}\text{ V}, \quad I_{max}(x=1\text{m}) = \frac{3e^{-\alpha} + e^{\alpha}}{|Z_0|}\text{ A}$
