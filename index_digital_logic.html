6-Variable K-Maps

Visualizing simplification in three dimensions — stacking glass cubes and playing Spot the Difference.

The Problem with 6 Variables ($A, B, C, D, E, F$)

A standard 4-variable K-Map is a simple 2D grid. But a 6-variable map has $2^6 = 64$ cells. Drawing this as a giant 2D grid breaks the physical adjacency rules of Gray Code. Instead, we draw it as FOUR separate 16-cell K-Maps arranged in a large 2x2 grid.

To keep the logic intact, we label the four maps using the highest-order variables $A$ and $B$ in Gray Code order (00, 01, 11, 10):

  • Top-Left: $A=0, B=0$
  • Top-Right: $A=0, B=1$
  • Bottom-Right: $A=1, B=1$
  • Bottom-Left: $A=1, B=0$

The 3D Glass Cubes Trick

Imagine these four maps aren't flat on paper, but are a 2x2 stack of transparent glass cubes. You can group cells normally within a single 16-cell map, but the real power comes from jumping between maps.

The Game of "Spot the Difference":

  • If you have a group in the Left Map, and you see the exact same physical grouping in the Right Map, you can merge them into a massive 3D group! Because it exists when $B=0$ and $B=1$, the variable $B$ is completely eliminated.
  • If you have the exact same group in all FOUR maps, you can group them all together, eliminating both $A$ and $B$ instantly!

Reverse Engineering: Expanding Functions

If you're given a simplified equation and need to work backward to find the minterms, remember the 1 Unique Variable Trick.

Take a simplified term like $C'D'$. Notice that variables $A, B, E,$ and $F$ are missing. To expand it, you just branch out all possible combinations (0 and 1) for those missing variables. This directly reveals which exact physical cells in the 64-cell grid were grouped together to form that simplified term.

Detailed Example: Expand $F = C'D'$

  1. Identify missing variables: We have a 6-variable system ($A,B,C,D,E,F$). The term $C'D'$ is missing 4 variables ($A, B, E, F$).
  2. Calculate group size: Since 4 variables are missing, this term came from a massive group of $2^4 = 16$ cells.
  3. The Canonical Expansion Rule: Instead of guessing where the cells are visually, use pure boolean mathematics. Multiply the term by $(V + V') = 1$ for every missing variable $V$ to force it into pure minterms:
    • 1. Expand A and B:
      $C'D'(A+A')(B+B') = ABC'D' + AB'C'D' + A'BC'D' + A'B'C'D'$
    • 2. Expand E and F:
      Multiply all 4 new terms by $(E+E')(F+F')$
    • 3. Result: 16 pure Minterms (e.g. $A'B'C'D'E'F' = 000000_2$)
  4. The Physical Reality: Converting those 16 minterms to binary gives you the exact cell numbers. When you map them on the grid, you'll see they perfectly correspond to the entire $CD=00$ column across ALL FOUR glass cube maps. No visual guesswork needed!

Carry Look-Ahead Adders

Deriving the Generate-Propagate equations from scratch — no memorization, pure logic.

The Problem with Ripple Carry

In a basic adder, each Full Adder has to wait for the previous one to calculate the carry out ($C_{out}$). This creates a massive time delay (a "ripple"). A Carry Look-Ahead Adder (CLA) solves this by calculating all carries simultaneously using pure parallel logic.

The "Cutting the Wires" Analogy

Look at the wire $C_3$ in the image above. Box 3 cannot do any math until it receives the electrical signal from $C_3$. But the $C_3$ wire doesn't have electricity until Box 2 finishes its math. And Box 2 has to wait for Box 1... and Box 1 has to wait for Box 0! The carry has to "ripple" through the physical wires one by one. This is agonizingly slow.

To fix this, hardware engineers do something drastic: They take scissors and cut wires $C_1, C_2,$ and $C_3$!

Instead of making the boxes wait for each other, they pull the internal Generate and Propagate signals out of the boxes and route them into a massive new "brain" sitting underneath the adders. This brain takes all inputs simultaneously, crunches the algebra instantly, and shoots electricity directly back into $C_1, C_2, C_3,$ and $C_4$ at the exact same time!

Here are the two magical internal signals we pull out for each bit $i$:

  • Generate ($G_i = A_i \cdot B_i$): The bit column generates a carry all by itself (both inputs are 1).
  • Propagate ($P_i = A_i \oplus B_i$): The bit column will pass along a carry if it receives one from the previous column.

$C_{i+1} = G_i + P_i \cdot C_i$

Deriving the Formulas (No Memorization!)

You never need to memorize the massive CLA equations. You just take the base formula and recursively substitute the previous carry!

Deriving $C_1$:

$C_1 = G_0 + P_0 \cdot C_{in}$

Deriving $C_2$:

$C_2 = G_1 + P_1 \cdot C_1$

Substitute $C_1$:

$C_2 = G_1 + P_1(G_0 + P_0C_{in}) = G_1 + P_1G_0 + P_1P_0C_{in}$

Deriving $C_3$:

$C_3 = G_2 + P_2 \cdot C_2$

Substitute $C_2$:

$C_3 = G_2 + P_2(G_1 + P_1G_0 + P_1P_0C_{in}) = G_2 + P_2G_1 + P_2P_1G_0 + P_2P_1P_0C_{in}$

Deriving $C_4$:

$C_4 = G_3 + P_3 \cdot C_3$

Substitute $C_3$:

$C_4 = G_3 + P_3(G_2 + P_2G_1 + P_2P_1G_0 + P_2P_1P_0C_{in})$
$C_4 = G_3 + P_3G_2 + P_3P_2G_1 + P_3P_2P_1G_0 + P_3P_2P_1P_0C_{in}$

Notice the beautiful pattern: Every term is just "Generate at the current bit" OR "Propagate from a previous generation". Since all $G$ and $P$ values are known immediately, the circuit calculates $C_4$ in just two gate delays (AND then OR)!

Two's Complement & Overflow

How a single XOR gate tells the CPU whether its math just broke — and why video games used to crash.

The Proof: Why is $-B = \overline{B} + 1$?

This is one of the most beautiful proofs in computer science! It ties pure mathematics directly to the physical limitations of a motherboard.

  1. The Wall of 1s: If you add a binary number $B$ to its exact opposite $\overline{B}$, you always get a solid wall of 1s. For example: 0101 + 1010 = 1111. So universally: $B + \overline{B} = 1111$.
  2. Convert to Decimal: In an $n$-bit system, a solid wall of 1s is equal to $2^n - 1$. Let's rewrite our law with algebra:
    $B + \overline{B} = 2^n - 1$
  3. A Little Algebra: Let's rearrange to isolate $-B$. Subtract $B$ from both sides, then add $1$ to both sides.
    $\overline{B} + 1 = 2^n - B$
  4. The Hardware Magic Trick: In a 4-bit computer, $2^4 = 16$. In binary, 16 is 1 0000 (a 5-bit number!). But the chip physically only has 4 wires. When it tries to hold 1 0000, the 5th bit physically falls off the edge of the chip and disappears! Because it's destroyed, the 4 wires just read 0000. So in hardware reality: $2^n = 0$.
  5. The Final Substitution: Plug that hardware reality ($2^n=0$) back into our algebra equation:
    $-B = \overline{B} + 1$

By exploiting the fact that computer chips have a limited number of wires and will naturally drop overflowing bits, engineers figured out how to do subtraction without ever having to invent subtraction logic gates!

Overflow: When Games Crash

It absolutely will cause errors if the math you try to do exceeds the physical capacity of the chip (like adding $5 + 4$ in a 4-bit system, which wraps around to $-7$). But how does the CPU know the difference between "good" overflow (from our subtraction trick) and "bad" overflow (a catastrophic math error)?

The Overflow Flag ($V$)

The CPU watches the final sign bit (the MSB) with a single XOR gate. It checks two things: did a carry go INTO the sign bit, and did a carry fall OUT of it?

$V = C_{in} \oplus C_{out}$

  • $V = 0$: The carry IN is the same as the carry OUT. The CPU says: "This is the 'good' overflow from subtraction. The math is perfectly valid. Ignore the dropped bit."
  • $V = 1$: The carry IN is different from the carry OUT. The CPU panics: "ARITHMETIC OVERFLOW! The numbers were too big!"

When that Overflow Flag hits $1$, the CPU immediately throws an exception to the OS—which is exactly why classic video games (like Pac-Man's infamous kill screen or Civilization's Nuclear Gandhi) used to crash or glitch when a score got too high and exceeded memory limits!

Sequential Logic & Flip-Flops

Mastering the SR, D, JK, and T flip-flops, and how to convert between them mathematically.

1. The SR (Set-Reset) Flip-Flop

This is the foundational logic memory element. While simple, it has a critical flaw: the indeterminate/invalid state when both inputs are driven high.

SRState ($Q_{N+1}$)
00Hold (No change, remains $Q_N$)
01Reset (Forces $Q$ to 0)
10Set (Forces $Q$ to 1)
11Invalid (Forbidden state)

2. The D (Data) Flip-Flop

The D flip-flop is essentially an SR flip-flop with an inverter connected between the inputs. This guarantees that both inputs are never 1 at the same time, completely eliminating the invalid state problem.

  • It acts as a "delay" or "data" buffer.
  • Whatever logic level is present at the D input passes through to the Q output when the clock pulse triggers.
  • When D = 0: Q becomes 0 (Reset state).
  • When D = 1: Q becomes 1 (Set state).

3. The JK Flip-Flop

The JK flip-flop was designed to solve the "indeterminate" state issue of the SR flip-flop. By using feedback from the outputs (Q and Q') back into the input NAND gates, it creates a predictable "toggle" state.

JKState ($Q_{N+1}$)
00Hold (No change)
01Reset (Forces $Q$ to 0)
10Set (Forces $Q$ to 1)
11Toggle (Flips state)

The Race Around Condition (Logical Breakdown)

The Problem: Occurs in a level-triggered JK flip-flop when $J=1, K=1$ (toggle mode). Because the clock pulse width ($t_w$) is typically larger than the propagation delay of the flip-flop ($t_{pd}$), the output $Q$ will toggle ($0 \rightarrow 1 \rightarrow 0 \rightarrow 1...$) uncontrollably multiple times while the clock remains HIGH. When the clock goes LOW, the final state of $Q$ is unpredictable.

How is it eliminated?

  • 1. Master-Slave Configuration (Most Common): Two flip-flops cascaded.
    Step-by-step logic:
    • Master is positive level-triggered. Slave is negative level-triggered (via an inverted clock).
    • When CLK=1: Master evaluates inputs and updates its internal state. The Slave is disabled, locking final output Q. Since Q doesn't change yet, there's no feedback race!
    • When CLK=0: Master is disabled (locking its state). Slave is enabled and copies the Master's state to the final output Q.
    Because the final output only updates when the clock falls, the feedback loop is broken.
  • 2. Edge-Triggering: Make the flip-flop sensitive only to the exact instant the clock transitions (e.g., LOW to HIGH). This gives the flip-flop only enough time to toggle exactly once per cycle.
  • 3. Pulse-Narrowing: Artificially ensure the clock pulse width ($t_w$) is less than the propagation delay ($t_{pd}$) so it only has time to toggle once.
💡 Core Intuition: By splitting the "read input" phase (Master) and "write output" phase (Slave) into two different clock levels, you completely break the infinite feedback loop that causes the race!

Start by drawing two standard flip-flop boxes. Label the first one MASTER and the second one SLAVE. Give the Master block inputs for J, K, and CLK, and outputs for Q and $\overline{Q}$. Give the Slave block inputs for J, K, and CLK, and outputs for Q and $\overline{Q}$.

Step 2: Wire the Center (Master to Slave)
The internal outputs of the Master block feed directly into the inputs of the Slave block like a straight bridge:
• Draw a line straight from the Master's Q output to the Slave's J input.
• Draw a line straight from the Master's $\overline{Q}$ output to the Slave's K input.

Step 3: Wire the Inverse Clock
Because it is a master-slave configuration, the two blocks cannot listen at the same time. One must get an inverted clock.
• Draw an external pin labeled CLK.
• Run a line from this CLK pin straight into the Master's clock input.
• Tap into that same line, pass it through a NOT gate (inverter), and connect the output of that inverter to the Slave's clock input.

Step 4: Wire the Outer Feedback Loops
This is the part that converts a standard latch behavior into a functional JK flip-flop. The final outputs must wrap all the way back to the Master's inputs:
• Take a line from the final Q output of the Slave block, drag it all the way back to the front, and connect it to the Master's K input line.
• Take a line from the final $\overline{Q}$ output of the Slave block, drag it back to the front, and connect it to the Master's J input line.

Step 5: Add the Asynchronous Pins ($\overline{PR}$ and $\overline{CLR}$)
Since the prompt specifically asked for active-low preset and clear, you must add bubbles to the lines entering the blocks:
• Draw an external line labeled $\overline{PR}$. Draw a tiny circle (bubble) where it enters the bottom or top of the Master block.
• Draw an external line labeled $\overline{CLR}$. Draw a tiny circle (bubble) where it enters the bottom or top of the Master block.
(Note: In textbook master-slave diagrams, preset and clear are typically applied to the Master stage box to steer the initial state before it cascades to the slave).

4. The T (Toggle) Flip-Flop

The T flip-flop is essentially a JK flip-flop where J and K are tied together into a single input called T. It is primarily used in counters and frequency dividers.

T$Q_N$$Q_{N+1}$State

011Hold

The XOR Connection: CLA & Flip-Flops

When you want a D flip-flop to behave like a T flip-flop, you feed the Data input ($D$) through an XOR gate taking the Toggle input ($T$) and the current state ($Q_n$):

$D = T \oplus Q_n$

In this circuit, the XOR gate is acting as a conditional inverter. If $T=1$, it flips the state; if $T=0$, it passes it through unchanged.

The exact same logic appears in the Carry Look-Ahead Adder!

Just like $T$ decides if the flip-flop state toggles, the $P_i$ term in a CLA (which is an XOR of the two inputs) decides if the carry-in ($C_i$) will toggle the final Sum bit.

$P_i = A_i \oplus B_i \quad \text{and} \quad S_i = P_i \oplus C_i$

This means we wire the circuit by feeding $D$ directly into $S$, and inverted $D$ into $R$.

Converting D to T Flip-Flop

The rule for a D flip-flop is that the required $D$ input must be exactly the same as the Next State ($Q_{n+1}$). To act like a T flip-flop, we apply the T rules to figure out the Next State before we determine $D$:

  • T = 0 (Hold): Next State is the same as $Q_n$.
  • T = 1 (Toggle): Next State is the opposite of $Q_n$.

🏆 Boss Fight: Designing a Custom X-Y Flip-Flop

Now for the ultimate test: deriving the exact hardware logic needed to force a standard T Flip-Flop to behave like a custom X-Y Flip-Flop. You set up the battlefield perfectly with a K-map for $T$, mapping $Q_n$ on the rows and the inputs $XY$ on the columns.

The X-Y K-Map

We map the required 1s into our grid. Notice the column order (00, 01, 11, 10)—that's the classic Gray code layout essential for K-maps.

Grouping for the Kill

To get the most minimized expression, we need to find the biggest, most efficient loops. There are two essential pairs here:

  1. The Bottom Pair (Row 1, Cols 00 and 01): We loop the 1 at 00 and the 1 at 01.
    $Q_n$ is 1. $X$ stays 0. $Y$ changes, so it cancels out.
    Result: $\overline{X}Q_n$
  2. The Wrap-Around Pair (Row 0, Cols 00 and 10): We loop the 1 on the far left with the 1 on the far right (remember, K-maps fold like a cylinder!).
    $Q_n$ is 0. $Y$ stays 0. $X$ changes, so it cancels out.
    Result: $\overline{Y}\overline{Q_n}$

(Note: You might be tempted to loop the vertical pair in the 00 column, but that's redundant! Our first two groups already covered all the 1s, so adding a third group just adds unnecessary hardware.)

Boss Defeated: Final Hardware Logic

Combine those two groupings, and you get the exact hardware logic needed to force a standard T Flip-Flop to behave like our custom X-Y Flip-Flop:

$T = \overline{X}Q_n + \overline{Y}\overline{Q_n}$

Chapter 05

Registers & Counters

Scaling up flip-flops to store multiple bits of data, shift information, and divide frequencies.

1. Registers & Shift Registers

A single flip-flop stores exactly one bit (a 1 or a 0). To store larger pieces of information, we connect multiple flip-flops in series. This grouped device is called a Register.

When we need to move that stored data around, we use a Shift Register. By applying clock pulses, the stored bits can be shifted left or right, moving in and out of the register.

The 4 Types of Shift Registers

  • SISO (Serial In, Serial Out): Data goes in one bit at a time, comes out one bit at a time.
  • SIPO (Serial In, Parallel Out): Data goes in sequentially, but can be read all at once.
  • PISO (Parallel In, Serial Out): Data is loaded instantly, but comes out sequentially.
  • PIPO (Parallel In, Parallel Out): Data is loaded instantly and read instantly.

2. Counters & Frequency Division

A Counter is a specialized register designed specifically to count the number of clock pulses that have occurred. Because it counts pulses, it inherently acts as a Frequency Divider Circuit (e.g., dividing a fast clock signal into a slower one).

Maximum States ($2^n$)

If a counter is built using $n$ flip-flops, the absolute maximum number of states it can hold is $2^n$. For example, 3 flip-flops give a maximum of 8 states (0 to 7).

The MOD Number

The MOD (Modulus) is the actual number of states used by the counter. A counter can be designed to reset early, meaning:
$\text{MOD} \le 2^n$

3. Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Counters

Asynchronous (Ripple) Counters Synchronous Counters
Slower. The clock pulse ripples through one flip-flop to the next, causing propagation delays. Faster. A universal clock pulse triggers all flip-flops simultaneously.
Limited. Can only count strictly Up or Down. Versatile. Can count Up, Down, Random patterns, and specialized shifts (like Ring or Johnson counters).

Chapter 06

Logic Families & IC Specs

Understanding the electrical characteristics of CMOS, voltage levels, noise margins, and propagation delay.

1. Voltage Levels & Logic States

Digital circuits interpret electrical voltage as logical `1`s and `0`s. However, reality isn't perfect, so engineers define specific threshold ranges:

  • $V_{OH(min)}$: The minimum voltage level at an output to guarantee a logical 1 state.
  • $V_{OL(max)}$: The maximum voltage level at an output to guarantee a logical 0 state.
  • $V_{IH(min)}$: The minimum voltage required at an input to be recognized as a 1.
  • $V_{IL(max)}$: The maximum voltage required at an input to still be recognized as a 0.

Noise Margin

Noise margin is the buffer zone between output voltage and required input voltage. It guarantees that tiny electrical fluctuations (noise) won't accidentally flip a 0 to a 1!

2. CMOS Logic Advantages

Most modern computer chips are built using CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) technology. It completely dominates NMOS for several reasons:

  • Extremely large fan-out capability (>50).
  • Lowest power dissipation of all gates (a few nW).
  • Very high noise immunity and margin.
  • Directly compatible with older TTL gates.
  • Lower propagation delay and higher speed than NMOS.
  • Only a single power supply ($+V_{DD}$) required.
  • Excellent temperature stability.
  • Low-voltage (1.5 V) chips are available.

⚠️ Warning: Static Charge

MOS chips must be protected from acquiring static charges by keeping the leads shorted. Static charges acquired in leads will instantly destroy the chip! (Modern chips rectify this with built-in protective circuits).

3. Physical Limitations: Delay & Fan-Out

Fan-In & Fan-Out

Fan-In is the maximum number of inputs a single logic gate can practically support without degrading the input voltage level.

Fan-Out is the maximum number of digital inputs that the output of a single logic gate can feed safely. If you exceed the manufacturer's specified Fan-Out load, the circuit malfunctions because it cannot supply the demanded power!

Propagation Delay

The exact measure of how long it takes for a gate's output to physically change state after its input changes.

Chapter 07

Memory & Programmable Logic

Understanding RAM, ROM, addressing schemes, and memory capacity calculations.

1. Introduction to Memory Types

There are two primary types of memory used in digital systems:

Random-Access Memory (RAM)

Volatile memory that can perform both write and read operations at high speeds.

Read-Only Memory (ROM)

Non-volatile memory that performs only read operations. ROM is inherently a Programmable Logic Device (PLD).

Other programmable logic units include the PLA (Programmable Logic Array), PAL (Programmable Array Logic), and FPGA (Field-Programmable Gate Array).

2. The Memory Unit

A memory unit stores binary information in groups of bits called words. Communication with the memory unit happens via three specific channels:

  • Data Lines: Input and Output lines for the actual binary data.
  • Address Selection Lines: Identifies which specific word is being accessed.
  • Control Lines: Specifies the direction of transfer (Read vs. Write).

3. Addressing & Capacity

Each word in memory is assigned a unique identification number called an address. The addresses start from $0$ up to $2^k - 1$, where $k$ is the number of address lines available.

Standard Terminology

  • 1 Byte = 8 bits
  • 1 Word = 2 bytes (16 bits)

Calculating Memory Capacity

The number of words in a memory block is denoted by letters based on powers of 2:

  • K (Kilo) = $2^{10}$ $\rightarrow$ Example: $64\text{K} = 2^6 \times 2^{10} = 2^{16}$ words
  • M (Mega) = $2^{20}$ $\rightarrow$ Example: $2\text{M} = 2^1 \times 2^{20} = 2^{21}$ words
  • G (Giga) = $2^{30}$ $\rightarrow$ Example: $4\text{G} = 2^2 \times 2^{30} = 2^{32}$ words

4. Write & Read Operations

To interact with the memory unit, the CPU must follow strict sequential steps.

Transferring IN (Write)

  1. Apply the binary address of the desired word to the address lines.
  2. Apply the data bits that must be stored to the data input lines.
  3. Activate the write input.

Transferring OUT (Read)

  1. Apply the binary address of the desired word to the address lines.
  2. Activate the read input.

(The memory unit will push the requested word to the data output lines).

5. Ground Level: What Does Verilog Code Actually Mean?

In Verilog (a Hardware Description Language), memory is just a massive 2D array—think of it as a giant, physical bookshelf. When you see a line of code like this:

reg [15:0] memword[0:1023];

Here is the plain English breakdown:

  • [0:1023]: This tells the computer to build exactly 1,024 shelves. Each shelf represents a specific "address".
  • [15:0]: This tells the computer that every single one of those shelves is designed to hold a box exactly 16 bits wide (a "word").

So, the code simply says: "Build me a bookshelf with 1024 slots, where each slot holds a 16-bit number."

Line-by-Line Verilog Memory Breakdown

The Setup
  • //Read and write... & //Memory size...: Comments (ignored by the computer). They leave a note for the human reader.
  • module memory (Enable,ReadWrite,Address,DataIn,DataOut);: Creates a new hardware block (like a blank microchip) named memory. The items in parentheses are the physical "pins" connecting it to the outside world.
The Inputs and Outputs (The Pins)
  • input Enable, ReadWrite;: Single-wire inputs. Enable acts as the master on/off switch. ReadWrite is the toggle telling the chip whether to read or write.
  • input [3:0] DataIn;: A 4-bit wide input bus (wires 3, 2, 1, and 0) for the data you want to save.
  • input [5:0] Address;: A 6-bit wide input bus. 6 bits can represent exactly 64 values (0 to 63), matching the 64 memory slots perfectly.
  • output [3:0] DataOut;: A 4-bit wide output bus to push the requested data back out.
  • reg [3:0] DataOut;: In Verilog, if an output is assigned a value inside an always block, it must be declared as a reg (register) to hold its state.
The Actual Memory
  • reg [3:0] Mem [0:63];: The core of the snippet! This tells the computer to build exactly 64 slots ([0:63]), and makes sure each is 4 bits wide ([3:0]).
The Logic (The Brains)
  • always @ (Enable or ReadWrite): Watch the Enable and ReadWrite pins. If either changes, trigger the logic below.
  • if (Enable): Is the master switch turned on? If yes, proceed.
  • if (ReadWrite) DataOut = Mem[Address]; //Read: If high (1), go to Address, grab the 4-bit word, and copy to DataOut.
  • else Mem[Address] = DataIn; //Write: If low (0), take the 4-bit word from DataIn and save it onto Address.
  • else DataOut = 4'bz; //High impedance state: If Enable is off (0), output is 4'bz. 'Z' means "High Impedance"—it physically disconnects the output pins so they don't leak electricity or interfere.
  • endmodule: The design for this specific chip is finished!

6. Timing Rules (The "Relay Race" of Memory)

Writing or reading memory is a physical process, meaning it takes actual time for electricity to move. Imagine the CPU and memory are running a very strict relay race. Here is why the rules exist:

  • Wait for Address Stability: Why do we wait for the address to stabilize before shouting "Write!"? Because electricity doesn't settle instantly. If you send the "Write" command while the address signal is still bouncing between 0 and 1, you might accidentally blast data into a random, innocent memory slot and destroy it! Let the address settle first.
  • The 50ns Hold Rule: You cannot just "flash" the write signal. Hardware is relatively slow. You must physically hold the electrical voltage high for at least 50 nanoseconds so the memory unit has enough time to register the action.
  • The Falling Edge of $T_3$: During a read cycle, the CPU waits until the $3^{\text{rd}}$ clock cycle ($T_3$) ticks down (negative transition). That exact moment is when the CPU reaches its hand out to grab the data from the memory bus.

Chapter 08

Deep Dive: RAM & Decoding

Understanding SRAM vs DRAM, Memory Cells, and Address Multiplexing.

1. SRAM vs DRAM (The Whiteboard vs The Leaky Bucket)

Not all RAM is built the same. There are two major architectures used to store binary data, and they trade off speed for capacity.

Static RAM (SRAM)

Built using internal latches (like the flip-flops we learned about). Think of it like a whiteboard: once you write a "1" on it, it stays there forever (as long as the power is on).

  • High Speed (shorter read/write cycles)
  • Easy to use
  • Low density & Low capacity
  • High cost & High power consumption

Dynamic RAM (DRAM)

Built using MOS transistors acting as capacitors. Think of it like a leaky bucket of water. To keep a "1" stored, you have to constantly refill the bucket before it empties. This is called refreshing.

  • Requires periodic refreshing
  • High density & High capacity (massive storage in a single chip!)
  • Low cost & Low power consumption
  • Slower speed

⚠️ Volatile vs. Nonvolatile

Both SRAM and DRAM are Volatile. If you unplug the power cable, all the latches reset and all the capacitors drain. The memory is wiped clean. Nonvolatile memory (like a Magnetic Disk or ROM) retains its data even when the power is entirely removed.

2. Memory Decoding (Finding the Right Shelf)

How does the computer actually pick the specific word out of thousands? It uses Decoders. But first, let's look at the absolute smallest piece of memory: a single Binary Cell.

The Binary Cell (BC) is essentially an S-R Latch wrapped in AND gates to control when it is allowed to Read or Write based on the Select line.

  • If Read/Write = 0 & Select = 1: Input data flows into the S-R latch.
  • If Read/Write = 1 & Select = 1: Stored data flows out of the S-R latch.

The $k \times 2^k$ Decoder

If we want to build a memory with $2^k$ words (like the 4x4 RAM shown left), we need a decoder with $k$ inputs. The decoder takes the binary address and activates precisely one output line, which triggers the Select pin for that entire row of Binary Cells.

During a Read operation, all the cells in that activated row dump their bits into the OR gates at the bottom to form the final Output Word.

3. Coincident (2D) Decoding (The Battleship Strategy)

There is a massive physical problem with standard decoders. If you build a 1K-word memory ($1024$ slots), you need a $10 \times 1024$ decoder. That requires 1,024 massive AND gates inside the chip. That is terribly inefficient.

The Solution: Arrange the memory in a 2D Grid (like the game Battleship!).

Instead of one massive list of 1024 words, we arrange them in a $32 \times 32$ grid. Now, we just need one $5 \times 32$ decoder for the Rows ($X$), and a second $5 \times 32$ decoder for the Columns ($Y$).

By intersecting the Row and Column lines, we activate the exact memory slot. This dramatically reduces the number of logic gates required inside the chip!

4. Address Multiplexing (Saving Physical Pins)

Because DRAM is so dense (4x the density of SRAM and 4x cheaper!), it can hold massive amounts of data. But massive data requires a massive Address. If you need a 16-bit address, you'd normally need 16 physical metal pins poking out of the IC package. We want to shrink the chip size, so we use Address Multiplexing.

  • Instead of sending all 16 bits at once, we chop the address in half and send it through just 8 physical pins.
  • Step 1: The CPU sends the first 8 bits (the Row Address) down the wires. The RAS (Row Address Selection) signal tells the chip to grab these 8 bits and save them in the Row Register.
  • Step 2: The CPU sends the remaining 8 bits (the Column Address) down the exact same wires. The CAS (Column Address Selection) signal tells the chip to grab these bits and save them in the Column Register.

By staggering the delivery, we cut the required number of physical address pins in half, significantly decreasing the package size and cost!

DJ's Real-World Q&A

Q: What if we added a third dimension to Address Multiplexing?

Yes, it is entirely possible, and it is exactly how modern high-performance memory is built!

In 2D multiplexing, we split the address into a Row and a Column. If we move to 3D, we add a third dimension: the Bank or Layer. The CPU sends the address in three staggered chunks over the same pins: Layer/Bank Select ("Which floor?"), Row Address ("Which hallway?"), and Column Address ("Which room?").

The Advantages
  • Massive Density: Stack arrays vertically to pack gigabytes into the same 2D footprint.
  • Shorter Wires (Less Delay): Routing vertically is physically shorter than across a wide motherboard, lowering propagation delay and power consumption.
  • Parallel Processing: One layer can search for a Row while another layer simultaneously reads a Column.
The Disadvantages
  • The Thermal Trap: The chips in the middle get incredibly hot with no airflow.
  • Manufacturing Nightmares: Requires drilling microscopic holes through silicon, called Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs), which are fragile and expensive.
  • Controller Complexity: The CPU's memory controller must perfectly time three multiplexing signals instead of two.

HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) used in modern GPUs and AI accelerators is exactly this: a true 3D stack of memory arrays using TSVs to multiplex data vertically!

Q: Are SSDs just using EEPROM technology?

An SSD uses Flash memory (specifically NAND Flash). Flash memory is actually just a highly evolved, high-speed type of EEPROM!

1. Standard EEPROM (The Tweezers)
  • How it works: Reads, writes, and erases data one single byte at a time.
  • The Vibe: Extremely precise, but agonizingly slow and low capacity (Kilobytes).
  • Where you see it: Perfect for storing tiny configuration files, boot parameters, or sensor calibration data on custom PCBs or microcontrollers.
2. SSD / Flash Memory (The Bulldozer)
  • How it works: Memory cells are grouped to read, write, and erase data in massive blocks (thousands of bytes at once).
  • The Vibe: Loses byte-level precision, but gains incredible speed and massive data density.
  • Where you see it: Designed to move massive files, load OS, and handle heavy traffic (e.g., 1TB M.2 PC drive).

Q: Should I use standard EEPROM for continuous MQ gas sensor data logging?

Using standard EEPROM for continuous sensor data logging will actually hurt your system's longevity, and it won't reduce electrical noise. Here is why:

1. The Write-Cycle Trap

Standard EEPROM can typically handle only 100,000 to 1,000,000 write cycles before it physically degrades. Constantly writing gas sensor data every few seconds will "burn out" the chip in weeks. Instead, rely on RAM (infinite write cycles) to hold active data, then bulk-write it to an SD card (Flash) or a network database.

2. The Best Use for EEPROM

EEPROM is perfect for saving calibration profiles. Save the calculated baseline resistance in clean air to the EEPROM. If the system loses power, it instantly remembers its calibration data upon reboot without manual recalibration.

3. Fixing the Sensor Noise

Noise is an analog problem. Fix it with:

  • Hardware: Place a small decoupling capacitor (like 0.1µF) directly across the power and ground pins of the sensors to absorb stray voltage spikes.
  • Software: Write a moving-average filter in your Python script to average out 10 or 20 readings at a time to smooth out analog spikes.

Q: Do SSDs and EEPROMs use physical fuses like PROMs? How do they degrade?

SSDs and EEPROMs do not use physical fuses at all. In older PROM chips, you literally melt microscopic fuses to write data, destroying them permanently. SSDs and EEPROMs are 100% electrical.

How Many Write Cycles Do SSDs Get?

Because an SSD writes data in massive blocks, engineers pack multiple bits into a single cell to make 1TB or 2TB drives feasible. Cycles depend on how many bits are crammed in:

  • SLC (Single-Level Cell): 1 bit/cell. Survives roughly 100,000 write cycles. (Heavy enterprise servers).
  • MLC (Multi-Level Cell): 2 bits/cell. Survives about 10,000 write cycles.
  • TLC (Triple-Level Cell): 3 bits/cell. Survives about 3,000 write cycles. (Standard consumer SSDs).
  • QLC (Quad-Level Cell): 4 bits/cell. Massive storage, but only survives about 1,000 write cycles.
How is it Written Without Melting?

SSDs use a Floating Gate Transistor. Imagine a tiny empty room (Floating Gate) surrounded by perfect insulated walls (Oxide layer).

  • Writing (Programming): To write a 0, the SSD applies a massive 20V spike. This forces electrons to phase through the solid wall and get trapped inside (Quantum Tunneling).
  • Reading: If the room has trapped electrons, it is a 0. If empty, it is a 1.
  • Erasing: A massive reverse voltage forces the trapped electrons to tunnel back out.
What Causes the Degradation?

It is purely wear and tear on that microscopic insulated wall (the Oxide layer). Every time electrons are forcefully punched through the wall, they cause microscopic atomic damage. After ~3,000 cycles (in TLC), the wall becomes so porous it can no longer hold electrons inside. The charge leaks out. Once insulation degrades to that point, that specific block is permanently dead!

Chapter 09

ROM & Programmable Logic (PLDs)

Understanding hard-coded memory, fuse links, and how to program logic arrays.

1. Read-Only Memory (The Massive Truth Table)

While RAM is like a whiteboard, Read-Only Memory (ROM) is like a printed book. Once the data is written, it is permanently fixed and can only be read. But how is a ROM actually built inside?

Under the hood, a ROM is literally just a Decoder connected to massive OR gates. It acts exactly like a giant, hard-coded Truth Table.

  • If you have $k$ address inputs, the internal decoder generates $2^k$ outputs (one for every possible memory address).
  • Those outputs are fed into $n$ different OR gates to create the $n$-bit output data.
  • Because it's a Decoder + OR gates, a ROM inherently implements a Sum of Minterms combinational circuit!

How Do You "Program" It?

Look at the grid intersections (called cross-points). Programming a ROM is a physical process.

  • A blue X means there is a physical connection (a microscopic "fuse"). This acts as a logical 1.
  • No X means the connection is broken (the fuse was blown). This acts as a logical 0.

2. The 4 Types of ROM

Mask ROM

Programmed at the factory during the actual silicon fabrication process. Impossible to change. Cheapest for mass production.

PROM (Programmable ROM)

Comes blank. You use a special machine to physically "blow" the fuses one time. Once blown, it cannot be reversed.

EPROM (Erasable PROM)

Can be wiped clean! You have to place the chip under a special Ultraviolet (UV) light for a period of time to erase the pattern.

EEPROM (Electrically Erasable)

The modern standard. Can be erased and rewritten entirely using electrical signals (no UV light required). Flash drives use a form of this!

3. Combinational PLDs (The "Menu" Analogy)

Programmable Logic Devices (PLDs) are chips with massive AND and OR arrays inside. Depending on which array you are allowed to program, it gets a different name. Think of it like a restaurant menu:

  • PROM (Fixed AND, Programmable OR): The kitchen has fixed, pre-made combo meals (the Decoder). You only get to choose which combinations go on your specific plate (the OR gates).
  • PAL (Programmable AND, Fixed OR): You get to custom-build the exact ingredients (Programmable AND), but the restaurant forces them onto specific, fixed plates (Fixed OR).
  • PLA (Programmable AND, Programmable OR): Total freedom. You program both the ingredients and how they are plated! Note: PLAs often have an XOR gate at the end to optionally invert the final output ($x \oplus 1 = x'$).
Chapter 10

Programming PLAs & PALs

Why we must simplify logic before programming a PLA, and how to build the Programming Table.

1. The PLA Challenge (Why We Must Simplify)

In a ROM, you don't have to simplify anything because the internal Decoder naturally gives you every single possible minterm. But in a Programmable Logic Array (PLA), you have a finite number of AND gates. You must simplify your Boolean functions before programming to save hardware!

The Golden Rule of PLA Design

You must solve K-Maps for BOTH the True function ($F$) and the Complement function ($F'$). Why? To see which one requires fewer AND gates (product terms), and more importantly, to see if $F_1$ and $F_2$ can share the exact same AND gate!

Step-by-Step Example: Maximizing Hardware Efficiency

Let's implement two functions in a PLA. We want to use the absolute minimum number of AND gates:
$F_1 = \sum(0, 1, 2, 4)$
$F_2 = \sum(0, 5, 6, 7)$

Step 1: Solve All Four K-Maps

We must evaluate both the 1s ($F$) and the 0s ($F'$) for both functions to see all our options.

  • For $F_1$:
    Map 1s $\rightarrow F_1 = A'B' + A'C' + B'C'$ (3 terms)
    Map 0s $\rightarrow F_1' = AB + AC + BC$ (3 terms)
  • For $F_2$:
    Map 1s $\rightarrow F_2 = AB + AC + A'B'C'$ (3 terms)
    Map 0s $\rightarrow F_2' = A'C + A'B + AB'C'$ (3 terms)
Step 2: Hunt for the Overlap

If we chose $F_1$ and $F_2$, they share zero terms. We would have to physically build $3 + 3 = 6$ separate AND gates.

But look closely at $F_1'$ and $F_2$!

  • $F_1' = \mathbf{AB} + \mathbf{AC} + BC$
  • $F_2 = \mathbf{AB} + \mathbf{AC} + A'B'C'$

They both share the exact same $\mathbf{AB}$ and $\mathbf{AC}$ terms! By choosing this combination, we only need to build 4 unique AND gates: ($AB$, $AC$, $BC$, $A'B'C'$).

💡 Pro Tip: EPIs vs PIs in Hardware

EPIs (Essential Prime Implicants) are your absolute minimum requirements. When you solve the K-Map, the EPIs are the AND gates you are forced to build because they cover a 1 (or a 0) that no other grouping can cover.

PIs (Prime Implicants) give you choices. When trying to share AND gates between $F_1$ and $F_2$, you actively look at the non-essential PIs to see if you can force them to match!

If $F_1$ has a choice between grouping a PI of $AB$ or $AC$, and $F_2$ requires $AB$ as an EPI, you obviously choose $AB$ for $F_1$ so they share the exact same gate. By doing this, you are physically reducing the number of silicon gates required to manufacture the microchip—saving power, space, and money!

Step 3: Fix the Output with XOR

Because we chose to build $F_1'$ (the inverse), we must flip the final answer back to true before it leaves the chip. We do this by programming the $F_1$ output XOR gate with a 1 ($x \oplus 1 = x'$). We leave $F_2$ programmed to 0 ($x \oplus 0 = x$).

2. The PLA Programming Table

Once simplified, engineers translate the math into a standard Programming Table before blowing the fuses.

🔗 Bridging the Gap: From K-Maps to the Table

How exactly did we build this table from our K-map logic? Here is the direct translation:

  • In Step 2, we found 4 unique product terms: $AB$, $AC$, $BC$, and $A'B'C'$. These become rows 1, 2, 3, and 4 in the table.
  • Look at Row 1 ($AB$): $F_1'$ uses it, so we put a 1 in the $F_1$ output column. $F_2$ ALSO uses it, so we put a 1 in the $F_2$ output column.
  • Look at Row 3 ($BC$): $F_1'$ uses it (put a 1 in $F_1$). But $F_2$ does NOT use it, so we leave a - in the $F_2$ column.
  • Look at Row 4 ($A'B'C'$): $F_1'$ does NOT use it (put a - in $F_1$). But $F_2$ DOES use it, so we put a 1 in the $F_2$ column.

How to Read the Table

1. Product Term (The AND Gates)

This column simply numbers every unique AND gate we decided to build during our simplification step. In our example, we need exactly 4.

2. Inputs (A, B, C)

This tells the machine exactly how to wire the inputs into each specific AND gate:

  • 1 : Connect the True input.
  • 0 : Connect the Inverted input.
  • - : Do not connect it at all.

(e.g., Term 1 is $AB$. So A=1, B=1, and C=-).

3. Outputs (Routing to the OR Gates)

The numbers inside the table rows tell the machine which AND gates should be wired into which OR gates:

  • 1 : Connect the AND gate to this output function.
  • - : Do not connect it.

Example: Look at Product Term 1 ($AB$) in the table:

  • Under $F_1$, there is a 1. So, we physically connect the $AB$ wire to the $F_1$ OR gate.
  • Under $F_2$, there is ALSO a 1. So, we route that exact same $AB$ wire to the $F_2$ OR gate! (This is our shared AND gate perfectly in action!)
4. The Output Headers (T/C)

The letters at the very top of the Output columns tell the machine how to program the final XOR gates:

  • If the header says (C) (like $F_1$), wire the XOR to 1 to invert the signal back to normal.
  • If the header says (T) (like $F_2$), wire the XOR to 0 to keep it true.

The Final PLA Circuit

Notice how the four AND gates are perfectly shared by the two OR gates.

This math is the absolute foundation of VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) design. In VLSI, you design microchips containing billions of microscopic transistors. If the Python script crashes, the system goes blind. By adding a PLA to the custom PCB, you create an instantaneous Hardware-Level Fail-Safe Alarm.

  • Digitize Inputs: Route the analog sensor voltages through Hardware Comparators (e.g., LM393). Now, an MQ-3 crossing a danger threshold outputs a pure digital `1`.

1. Sequential Programmable Logic Devices (SPLD)

So far, PLAs and PALs are purely Combinational (no memory, no clocks). Sequential Programmable Devices fix this by slapping a block of Flip-Flops onto the output of the AND-OR array.

The ultimate prototyping tool. FPGAs are massive arrays of logic blocks that the user can program at their desk, rather than at a multi-million dollar fabrication plant.

DAC & ADC Exam Problems

Mastering Digital-to-Analog calculations and theory for your university exams.

📚 The Master Formula Cheat Sheet

Before diving into the numbers, memorize these core concepts. Every single problem below relies on these exact rules:

  • Resolution (Step Size): The physical value of the smallest bit (LSB).
    Formula: $\text{Resolution} = \frac{\text{Full Scale Output}}{2^N - 1}$ (where N is the number of bits).
  • Proportionality Factor ($K$): Another name for the Resolution! It's the constant multiplier for the DAC.
    Formula: $K = \frac{\text{Any Output Voltage}}{\text{Its Decimal Input}}$.
  • Finding Any Output: If you know the step size ($K$) and the binary input, you can find the output instantly!
    Formula: $\text{Output} = K \times \text{Decimal Equivalent of Binary Input}$
  • Full Scale Output: The absolute maximum voltage/current the DAC can produce. Occurs when all bits are 1.
    Formula: $\text{F.S.} = \text{Resolution} \times (2^N - 1)$
  • Percentage Resolution: How small one step is compared to the whole scale. It ONLY depends on the number of bits!
    Formula: $\% \text{ Resolution} = \frac{1}{2^N - 1} \times 100\%$

Part 1: Mathematical Problem Solving

Problem 1: Current Output Proportionality

A 5-bit DAC has a current output. For a digital input of 10100, an output current of 10mA is produced. What will $I_{OUT}$ be for a digital input of 11101?

Solution:

  • Input 10100 in binary equals decimal 20.
  • $I_{OUT}$ = 10mA for this case. We find the proportionality factor ($K$) by dividing output by input: $K = 10mA / 20 = 0.5mA$.
  • New input 11101 in binary equals decimal 29.
  • $I_{OUT} = (0.5mA) \times 29 = \mathbf{14.5mA}$

💡 Why this works:

We are using the Finding Any Output formula here. A DAC is perfectly linear. If a decimal input of 20 gives 10mA, that means every single step (1 decimal unit) is worth exactly $10/20 = 0.5mA$. Once we know the value of 1 step, we can multiply it by any new decimal input to find its exact output!

Problem 2: Finding Maximum Voltage

What is the largest value of output voltage from an 8-bit DAC that produces 1.0V for a digital input of 00110010?

Solution:

  • Input 00110010 equals decimal 50.
  • $1.0V = K \times 50 \implies K = 20mV$.
  • The largest output occurs when all 8 bits are 1s (11111111), which equals decimal 255.
  • $V_{OUT(max)} = 20mV \times 255 = \mathbf{5.10V}$

💡 Why this works:

We are using the Full Scale Output formula. First, we find the step size ($K$) just like Problem 1. The maximum possible output of any DAC always happens when every single bit is turned on (all 1s). For an 8-bit system, the absolute highest decimal number you can make is 255. So, we multiply the step size by 255!

Problem 3 & 4: Calculating by Bit Weights

A 5-bit D/A converter produces $V_{OUT} = 0.2V$ for a digital input of 00001. Find $V_{OUT}$ for inputs 11111 and 10001.

Solution:

  • 0.2V is the weight of the Least Significant Bit (LSB). The weights of the other bits simply double: 0.4V, 0.8V, 1.6V, and 3.2V.
  • For 11111: Add them all up $\implies 3.2 + 1.6 + 0.8 + 0.4 + 0.2 = \mathbf{6.2V}$.
  • For 10001: Add just the MSB and LSB $\implies 3.2 + 0.2 = \mathbf{3.4V}$. (Alternatively, 10001 is decimal 17. So $0.2V \times 17 = 3.4V$).

💡 Why this works:

This problem proves that a DAC is just an adder! The LSB is the smallest step (0.2V). Because binary is base-2, every bit to the left is exactly double the physical weight of the previous one. If you turn on all the bits (11111), the total voltage is literally just the sum of their individual weights.

Problem 5: Resolution Percentage

A 10-bit DAC has a step size of 10mV. Determine the full-scale output voltage and the percentage resolution.

Solution:

  • Number of steps for 10 bits = $2^{10} - 1 = 1023$ steps.
  • Full scale output = $10mV \times 1023 = \mathbf{10.23V}$.
  • % Resolution = $\frac{\text{Step Size}}{\text{Full Scale}} \times 100\% = (\frac{10}{10230}) \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{0.1\%}$
  • Note: % Resolution only depends on the number of bits! You can always calculate it as $\frac{1}{2^N - 1} \times 100\%$.

💡 Why this works:

We are using the Percentage Resolution formula. This tells us how precise the DAC is. A 0.1% resolution means that one single step up or down changes the total output by only 0.1%. Notice how we didn't even need the 10mV number? The precision of a DAC is strictly limited by how many bits it has ($N$), nothing else!

Problem 6: Base Resolution and Full-Scale

Assume $V_{REF} = 10V$ and $R = 10 k\Omega$. Determine the resolution and full-scale output for this DAC. (Assume $R_L$ is much smaller than $R$).

Solution:

  • First, find the MSB current: $I_0 = \frac{V_{REF}}{R} = \frac{10V}{10k\Omega} = \mathbf{1mA}$.
  • The next three currents halve each time: 0.5mA, 0.25mA, and 0.125mA.
  • The Resolution is simply the LSB (the smallest step), which is $\mathbf{0.125mA}$.
  • The Full-Scale Output happens when all inputs are HIGH (all switches closed): $I_{OUT} = 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 = \mathbf{1.875mA}$.
  • Note: If you increase $V_{REF}$, the output currents increase proportionally!

💡 Why this works:

This problem tests your understanding of hardware scaling. The MSB current is strictly dictated by Ohm's Law ($I = V/R$). Because it's a binary system, every bit after the MSB is exactly half the physical current of the one before it. The resolution is always equal to the LSB, which is the smallest possible current that can flow through the final resistor.

Problem 7: Calculating Output with Error Range

A certain 8-bit DAC has a full-scale output of 2mA and a full-scale error of $\pm 0.5\% \text{ F.S.}$ What is the range of possible outputs for an input of 10000000?

Solution:

  • Step size (Resolution) = $\frac{2mA}{255} = \mathbf{7.84\mu A}$.
  • Input 10000000 equals decimal 128.
  • The ideal output should be $128 \times 7.84\mu A = \mathbf{1003.52\mu A}$ (or approx $1004\mu A$).
  • Calculate the absolute error based on Full-Scale (2mA): $\pm 0.5\% \times 2mA = \mathbf{\pm 10\mu A}$.
  • The actual output can deviate by $\pm 10\mu A$ from the ideal $1004\mu A$, so the range is $\mathbf{994\mu A \text{ to } 1014\mu A}$.

💡 Why this works:

We are applying the Finding Any Output formula, but accounting for real-world manufacturing flaws. A DAC isn't perfect. A "Full-Scale Error" of 0.5% means the physical resistors inside the chip might be slightly off, causing a max deviation of $\pm 10\mu A$. No matter what input you give it, the true analog output could be anywhere inside that $\pm 10\mu A$ window.

Problem 8: Digital Ramp ADC Conversion Math

Assume the following values for an ADC: clock frequency = 1 MHz; $V_T$ = 0.1 mV; DAC has F.S. output = 10.23 V and a 10-bit input. Determine:
a. The digital equivalent obtained for $V_A = 3.728 V$.
b. The conversion time.
c. The resolution of this converter.

Solution:

  • a. Digital Equivalent: The DAC has 10 bits ($2^{10} - 1 = 1023$ steps). The step size is $\frac{10.23V}{1023} = \mathbf{10mV}$. The staircase voltage ($V_{AX}$) goes up by 10mV per step. To reach or exceed $V_A$ ($3.728V$), we need $\frac{3.7281V}{10mV} = 372.81$ steps. The counter must count up to the next whole number: 373 steps. The binary equivalent of 373 is 0101110101.
  • b. Conversion Time: 373 steps means 373 clock pulses. At 1 MHz (1 pulse per microsecond), the total conversion time is exactly $373 \mu s$.
  • c. Resolution: The ADC's resolution is exactly the DAC's step size: 10mV. In percent, it is $\frac{1}{1023} \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{0.1\%}$.

💡 Why this works:

This introduces the Digital Ramp ADC! Remember that an ADC has a DAC inside it. The ADC simply forces its internal DAC to count up like a staircase (10mV per step). It takes 372.8 steps to climb as high as the real-world voltage of 3.728V. Since a counter can't do decimals, it takes one final step to 373 to surpass it and trip the comparator. Because the clock runs at 1 MHz, every single step takes exactly 1 microsecond.

Problem 9: ADC Quantization Range

For the same ADC in Problem 8, determine the approximate range of analog input voltages that will produce the exact same digital result of 0101110101 (373).

Solution:

  • Step 372 equals exactly 3.72V. Step 373 equals exactly 3.73V.
  • If $V_A$ is anywhere slightly above 3.72V (like 3.721V), the comparator won't trip at step 372. It will keep going and trip at step 373 (3.73V).
  • Thus, any voltage sitting between approximately 3.72V and 3.73V will yield the exact same answer (373). This range width is exactly 10mV, matching the DAC's resolution!

💡 Why this works:

This highlights "Quantization"! The physical world is smooth (infinite decimals), but a 10-bit computer only has 1,024 discrete "buckets" to put those voltages into. Any voltage from 3.72001V all the way to 3.72999V is going to fall into the exact same 373 bucket. This means an ADC inherently destroys a tiny bit of precision, and the size of that lost precision is exactly equal to its step size (10mV).

Problem 10: Total Maximum Error in ADC

A certain 8-bit ADC has a full-scale input of 2.55 V. It has a specified physical error of 0.1% F.S. Determine the maximum amount by which the internal $V_{AX}$ output can differ from the real-world analog input.

Solution:

  • First, find the unavoidable Quantization Error (step size): $\frac{2.55V}{255} = \mathbf{10mV}$. Because it works in "stairs", it could naturally be off by up to 10mV at any time.
  • Next, find the physical component error: $0.1\% \times 2.55V = \mathbf{2.55mV}$.
  • Add both together to find the absolute worst-case scenario: $10mV + 2.55mV = \mathbf{12.55mV}$.

💡 Why this works:

This problem combines the two types of error we just learned. First, you have Quantization Error (the 10mV step size we lose because the world is continuous but the ADC is chunky). Second, you have Hardware Error (the 2.55mV physical inaccuracy of the silicon resistors). If you are extremely unlucky, both errors could push in the same direction, meaning your digital reading could be off by a maximum total of 12.55mV from the real-world voltage!

3.7.3 Analytical Question

a) Draw digital ramp ADC and write down its operation.

Answer (The Operation Flow): A Digital Ramp ADC consists of a Counter, a DAC, and a Comparator.

  1. The system is given a "Start" signal, and the Counter resets to 0.
  2. With every clock pulse, the Counter counts up (1, 2, 3...).
  3. The DAC reads this binary count and instantly turns it into a rising analog "staircase" voltage ($V_{AX}$).
  4. The Comparator constantly watches both the rising staircase ($V_{AX}$) and the unknown real-world voltage ($V_A$).
  5. The very moment the staircase voltage steps just slightly higher than the real-world voltage, the Comparator trips.
  6. This trip signal instantly stops the clock from feeding the Counter, and fires the EOC (End of Conversion) pin to tell the computer the data is ready. The final binary number sitting in the Counter is the digital answer!

b) A digital transmission system uses a (7,4) systematic linear Hamming code for transmitting data over a noisy channel. If three of the message-codeword pairs in this code $(m_i, c_i)$ are known to be:

  • $m_1 = 1100, \quad c_1 = 0101100$
  • $m_2 = 1110, \quad c_2 = 0011110$
  • $m_3 = 0110, \quad c_3 = 1000110$

Which of the following is a valid codeword in this code?

  • (A) 1101001
  • (B) 1011010
  • (C) 0001011
  • (D) 0110100

Detailed Explanation & Solution: Correct answer is option (C)

Step 1: Understand the Layout

A (7,4) systematic code means there are 7 total bits: 3 Parity bits and 4 Data bits. "Systematic" means the data isn't scrambled—it's just glued together. By looking at the first example (Message = 1100, Codeword = 0101100), we can see the 3 parity bits are simply placed at the front: $P_0 P_1 P_2 \ d_0 d_1 d_2 d_3$.

Step 2: Derive the Parity Rules (The Venn Diagram Trick)

Instead of trying to guess the math, the Hamming (7,4) code is famously built using a Venn Diagram of 3 overlapping circles. The 4 Data bits sit in the overlapping regions, and the 3 Parity bits sit in the outer regions to act as "guards" for their respective circles.

By looking at the visual above, we can easily write out the rules! Each Parity guard just checks the data bits physically sitting inside its circle:

  • Circle 0 checks: $P_0 = d_0 \oplus d_1 \oplus d_3$
  • Circle 1 checks: $P_1 = d_1 \oplus d_2 \oplus d_3$
  • Circle 2 checks: $P_2 = d_0 \oplus d_1 \oplus d_2$

Step 3: What does the Math ($\oplus$) mean?

The $\oplus$ symbol stands for XOR (Exclusive OR). When XORing multiple bits together (like $1 \oplus 0 \oplus 1$), it acts as an ODD parity checker: if there is an ODD number of 1s, the result is 1. If there is an EVEN number of 1s, the result is 0. So $P_0 = d_0 \oplus d_1 \oplus d_3$ just ensures Circle 0 balances out!

Step 4: Test the Options

Now that we know the rules, take the last 4 bits of each multiple-choice option (the data), plug them into our Circle Equations, and see if the math produces the first 3 bits (the parity)!

  • (A) 110 1001: Data is 1001.
    $P_0 = 1 \oplus 0 \oplus 1 = 0$. But the option starts with $P_0 = 1$. (Invalid)
  • (B) 101 1010: Data is 1010.
    $P_0 = 1 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 = 1$ (Matches).
    $P_1 = 0 \oplus 1 \oplus 0 = 1$. But the option has $P_1 = 0$. (Invalid)
  • (C) 000 1011: Data is 1011.
    $P_0 = 1 \oplus 0 \oplus 1 = 0$ (Matches).
    $P_1 = 0 \oplus 1 \oplus 1 = 0$ (Matches).
    $P_2 = 1 \oplus 0 \oplus 1 = 0$ (Matches). (VALID!)
  • (D) 011 0100: Data is 0100.
    $P_0 = 0 \oplus 1 \oplus 0 = 1$. But the option starts with $P_0 = 0$. (Invalid)

Part 5: Sequential Circuit Timing (Exam Answers)

Maximum Clock Frequency ($f_{max}$) Calculation

Question: For the sequential circuit shown, calculate the maximum clock frequency (rounded off to the nearest integer) at which the circuit can operate reliably.

Detailed Explanation (Decoding the Math): Correct answer is 76.92 MHz

That textbook solution is super dry. Let's translate it into English!

Think of the Clock pulse as a Starting Gun in a race. When the gun fires, data (the runners) bursts out of the "Sending" Flip-Flop, runs through the obstacle course (the logic gates), and must arrive at the "Receiving" Flip-Flop in time to get seated before the NEXT starting gun fires. The time it takes to get seated at the end is called the Setup Time ($t_{setup}$).

The Rule: The time between two starting guns (the Clock Period $T$) MUST be greater than or equal to the total time of the slowest race.
Clock Period (T) ≥ Output Delay (t_pd) + Obstacle Delay (t_logic) + Seating Time (t_setup)

We just have to test every possible "race track" in the circuit and find the slowest one!

  • Race Track 1: Bottom Flip-Flop (FF2) to Top Flip-Flop (FF1)
    • Sender ($t_{pd}$): FF2 takes $8\text{ ns}$ to push the data out.
    • Obstacles ($t_{logic}$): The wire goes directly into FF1. No gates! Delay is $0\text{ ns}$.
    • Receiver ($t_{setup}$): FF1 needs $5\text{ ns}$ to set up.
    • Total Time: $8 + 0 + 5 = \mathbf{13\text{ ns}}$
  • Race Track 2: Top Flip-Flop (FF1) to Bottom Flip-Flop (FF2)
    • Sender ($t_{pd}$): FF1 takes $3\text{ ns}$ to push the data out.
    • Obstacles ($t_{logic}$): Data hits an XOR gate ($2\text{ ns}$) and an AND gate with a bubble, which is a NAND gate ($2\text{ ns}$). Total gate delay is $4\text{ ns}$.
    • Receiver ($t_{setup}$): FF2 needs $4\text{ ns}$ to set up.
    • Total Time: $3 + 4 + 4 = \mathbf{11\text{ ns}}$

The Conclusion:

The slowest race takes 13 ns. Therefore, the starting gun cannot fire any faster than every 13 ns. That means our Minimum Clock Period ($T_{min}$) is exactly $13\text{ ns}$.
To find the Maximum Frequency ($f_{max}$), we just flip it: $f = \frac{1}{T}$.
$f_{max} = \frac{1}{13 \times 10^{-9}\text{ s}} = 0.07692 \times 10^9\text{ Hz} = \mathbf{76.92\text{ MHz}}$.

Signed Magnitude, 1's Complement, and 2's Complement

Question: P, Q, and R are the decimal integers corresponding to the 4-bit binary number 1100 considered in signed magnitude, 1's complement, and 2's complement representations, respectively. Find the 6-bit 2's complement representation of (P + Q + R).

Detailed Explanation & Solution: Correct answer is 110101

Because the Most Significant Bit (MSB) of 1100 is a 1, we know this is a negative number in all three systems. Here is how each system decodes the remaining bits to find the magnitude:

  • Signed Magnitude (P): The MSB (1) means negative. The remaining bits 100 give the exact magnitude. 100 in binary is $4$. Therefore, $P = -4$.
  • 1's Complement (Q): To find the magnitude of a negative number in this system, we flip all the bits. The 1's complement of 1100 is 0011. 0011 is $3$. Therefore, $Q = -3$.
  • 2's Complement (R): To find the magnitude here, we flip the bits and add 1. Flipping 1100 gives 0011. Adding 1 gives 0100. 0100 is $4$. Therefore, $R = -4$.

The Sum:

$(P + Q + R) = (-4) + (-3) + (-4) = \mathbf{-11}$

Converting -11 to 6-bit 2's Complement:

  • First, write positive 11 in 6 bits: 001011
  • Second, flip all the bits (1's complement): 110100
  • Third, add 1 (2's complement): 110100 + 1 = 110101

Part 7: Error Detection & Hamming Code (Exam Answers)

1. Single Parity Bit (The Basics)

The simplest way to detect an error is to add a single extra bit to the end of your data before sending it.

  • Even Parity: We want the TOTAL number of 1s in the data (including the parity bit) to be EVEN.
    Example: Data is 1100101 (Four 1s). It's already even, so we attach a 0. Sent data: 11001010.
  • Odd Parity: We want the TOTAL number of 1s to be ODD.
    Example: Data is 1110100 (Four 1s). We need odd, so we attach a 1. Sent data: 11101001.

At the Receiver: The receiver just counts the 1s. If we agreed on Even Parity and the receiver counts an odd number of 1s, it instantly knows the data is corrupted and rejects it!

2. Standard Hamming Code (Detect & Correct)

Forget the Venn Diagram circles for a second. The circles were for the specific "Systematic" problem earlier. The Standard Hamming Code (developed by R.W. Hamming in 1951) uses a much easier layout: D7 D6 D5 P4 D3 P2 P1.

The "Pass the Exam" Cheat Code:
Why do the numbers look so weird? It's pure genius: Every Parity bit checks the positions based on its own binary representation!

  • P1 (Position 1 is 001): Checks every position that has a '1' in the 1s place (1, 3, 5, 7). → P1, D3, D5, D7
  • P2 (Position 2 is 010): Checks every position that has a '1' in the 2s place (2, 3, 6, 7). → P2, D3, D6, D7
  • P4 (Position 4 is 100): Checks every position that has a '1' in the 4s place (4, 5, 6, 7). → P4, D5, D6, D7

Example Problem: Detect and correct the error in the received even-parity Hamming code 1011011.

Layout: D7=1, D6=0, D5=1, P4=1, D3=0, P2=1, P1=1

  • Step 1 (Check P1): Look at positions 1, 3, 5, 7 → (P1, D3, D5, D7) = (1, 0, 1, 1).
    Count the 1s: Three 1s (Odd). Since we want Even parity, this is an Error (1).
  • Step 2 (Check P2): Look at positions 2, 3, 6, 7 → (P2, D3, D6, D7) = (1, 0, 0, 1).
    Count the 1s: Two 1s (Even). This is perfectly fine. No Error (0).
  • Step 3 (Check P4): Look at positions 4, 5, 6, 7 → (P4, D5, D6, D7) = (1, 1, 0, 1).
    Count the 1s: Three 1s (Odd). Since we want Even parity, this is an Error (1).

Finding the Culprit:

Read the errors backwards (P4, P2, P1) to get the binary error word: 101.
101 in decimal is 5. That means the 5th bit (D5) was flipped!
To correct it, we just flip D5 from a 1 to a 0. The correct word is 1001011.

Advanced ADC Types & Architectures

Understanding the major architectures of Analog to Digital Converters.

1. Analog to Digital Converter Overview

  • An A/D converter (ADC) accepts an analog sample $V_A$ and produces an N-bit digital word.
  • Examples of usage: Digital volt meters, cell phones, thermocouples, and digital oscilloscopes.
  • Types of A/D Converters: Dual Slope ADC, Successive Approximation ADC, Flash ADC, Delta-Sigma ADC, etc.

2. Counter Type ADC (Digital Ramp)

For an N-bit counter type ADC:

  • Maximum number of clock pulses required for conversion = $\mathbf{2^N - 1}$
  • Maximum time required for conversion = $\mathbf{(2^N - 1) T_{CLK}}$
  • Minimum number of clock pulses = 1
  • Average number of clock pulses = $\mathbf{2^{N-1}}$

3. Successive Approximation Type ADC (SAR)

  • It is faster than the digital ramp (counter type) ADC.
  • Conversion time ($t_c$) is completely independent of the value of the analog input voltage ($V_a$).
  • It has a fixed conversion time.
  • Maximum number of clock pulses for conversion = $\mathbf{N}$
  • Maximum conversion time = $\mathbf{N \cdot T_{CLK}}$

Note: SOC stands for Start of Conversion. EOC stands for End of Conversion.

4. Flash Type ADC

  • Also known as a Parallel-comparator type ADC or Simultaneous converter.
  • It is the highest speed ADC (fastest possible conversion).

Functional Components for an N-bit Flash ADC:

  • It utilizes $\mathbf{2^N - 1}$ comparators to compare the input signal level simultaneously with each of the $2^N - 1$ possible quantization levels.
  • The outputs of the comparators are processed by an encoding-logic block to provide the N bits of the output digital word.
  • Complete conversion can be obtained within one single clock cycle.
  • Total number of comparators = $\mathbf{2^N - 1}$
  • Total number of resistors = $\mathbf{2^N}$
  • Total number of priority encoders = $\mathbf{1}$ (typically a $2^N \times N$ encoder)
Chapter 14

Binary Adders

The fundamental circuits used for arithmetic operations.

1. Half Adder

The most basic arithmetic operation is addition. A Half Adder is a combinational logic circuit with two inputs (A and B) and two outputs (Sum and Carry). It is the basic building block for the addition of two single-bit numbers.

Boolean Functions:

  • Sum (S) = $A \oplus B$ (XOR Gate)
  • Carry (C) = $A \cdot B$ (AND Gate)

Sum $S$ is the Least Significant Bit and carry $C$ is the Most Significant Bit of the resultant sum.

2. Full Adder

A Full Adder overcomes the drawback of a Half Adder by accepting a third input: the carry-in ($C_{in}$) from a previous stage. It has three inputs ($A, B, C_{in}$) and two outputs ($S, C_{out}$).

Boolean Functions:

  • Sum (S) = $A \oplus B \oplus C_{in}$
  • Carry ($C_{out}$) = $AB + (A \oplus B)C_{in}$

A Full Adder is constructed using two Half Adders and one OR gate. If $C_{in} = 0$, the Full Adder behaves exactly like a Half Adder.

3. 4-bit Binary (Ripple Carry) Adder

The 4-bit binary adder performs the addition of two 4-bit numbers ($A_3 A_2 A_1 A_0$ and $B_3 B_2 B_1 B_0$). It is implemented by cascading four Full Adders.

  • The carry output ($C_{out}$) of each Full Adder becomes the carry input ($C_{in}$) of the next higher-order Full Adder.
  • The addition produces a sum of at most 5 bits. The final carry out ($C_4$) is the MSB of the final answer.
  • This is known as a Ripple Carry Adder because the carry bit "ripples" from the least significant stage all the way to the most significant stage.

4. Half Subtractor

A Half Subtractor is a combinational circuit with two inputs and two outputs (Difference and Borrow). It produces the difference between the two binary bits at the input and also produces a Borrow output to indicate if a 1 has been borrowed. In the subtraction ($A - B$), $A$ is called the Minuend bit and $B$ is called the Subtrahend bit.

Truth Table Insights:

  • Notice that $0 - 1$ produces a Difference of 1, and requires a Borrow of 1!

5. 4-bit Binary Adder / Subtractor

The circuit that can be used to perform either addition or subtraction of two binary numbers based on a control signal is known as a Binary Adder / Subtractor. Both operations utilize a cascaded set of Full Adders.

How it works:

  • The input bits of binary number $A$ are directly applied to the Full Adders.
  • The input bits of binary number $B$ are passed through 2-input XOR gates. The other input to these XOR gates is a control signal, $C_0$.
  • Addition ($C_0 = 0$): The XOR gates act as transparent buffers (outputting exactly $B$). The initial carry $C_0$ is 0. The circuit functions as a normal Ripple Carry Adder.
  • Subtraction ($C_0 = 1$): The XOR gates invert the bits of $B$ (outputting $B'$). The initial carry $C_0$ is 1. This essentially adds $A$ to the Two's Complement of $B$ ($A + B' + 1$), which is mathematically identical to $A - B$.

DAC and ADC Basics

Understanding the foundational concepts of Data Converters and the Weighted Resistor DAC.

1. What are DACs and ADCs?

Analog to Digital Converter (ADC) basically converts physical variables which are analog in nature to digital signal for processing. They have high conversion efficiency and requires less power. Examples of physical variables include audio signals, temperature, pressure etc. It can also be defined as a device that provides a digital output which is the representation of the input voltage or current level.

Digital to Analog Converter (DAC) basically converts digital code that represents digital value to analog current or voltage. In any communication system, the analog signal which is in the form of physical variables is converted to digital value by ADC. This digital signal is converted back to Analog signal for further processing i.e. Analog signal is necessary to drive Motors, temperature controller etc.

2. Digital to Analog Converter (DAC)

D/A converter (also called a DAC) accepts an n-bit digital word and produces an analog sample.

Output Voltage Equation:

$$V_o = k[b_0 + 2^1 b_1 + 2^2 b_2 + ... 2^{N-2} b_{N-2} + 2^{N-1} b_{N-1}]$$

where, $k$ = Proportionality factor, $b_n = 1$ if nth bit of digital input is 1, $b_n = 0$ if nth bit of digital input is 0.

3. Weighted Resistor DAC (N-bit)

  • A DAC can be constructed by using a Summing Amplifier and a set of resistors R, 2R, 4R, 8R, etc as its inputs.
  • The circuit consists of a reference voltage $V_f$, N binary-weighted resistors $\mathbf{R, 2R, 4R, 8R, \dots, 2^{(N-1)} R}$, N single-pole double-throw switches, and an Op-amp together with its feedback resistance $R_f = R/2$.
  • The switches are controlled by an N-bit digital input word D.

$$D = \frac{b_1}{2^1} + \frac{b_2}{2^2} + \dots + \frac{b_N}{2^N}$$

$$V_o = -R_f I_f = -V_f D$$

$$V_o = \frac{V_r R_f}{2^{N-1} R} [2^{N-1} b_{N-1} + 2^{N-2} b_{N-2} + \dots 2b_1 + b_0]$$

LSB resistance = $(2^N - 1)$ MSB resistance.

  • The accuracy of the DAC depends critically on the accuracy of the Reference voltage, the precision of the binary-weighted resistors, and the perfection of the switches.
  • A disadvantage of the binary-weighted resistor network is that for a large number of bits (N > 4) the spread between the smallest and largest resistances becomes quite large. This implies difficulties in maintaining accuracy in resistor values.

  • Percentage Resolution: $\%R = \frac{1}{2^N - 1} \times 100$
  • Maximum Error: Maximum error acceptable in ADC and DAC equals to resolution.

4. Analog to Digital Converter (ADC)

  • A/D converter (also called an ADC) accepts an analog sample $V_A$ and produces an N-bit digital word.
  • Examples of ADC usage are digital volt meters, cell phone, thermocouples, and a digital oscilloscope.

  • B) 0.3
  • C) 0.5
  • D) 1

The DAC output is given by $V_{DAC} = \sum_{n=0}^{3} 2^{n-1} b_n$:

$V_{DAC} = 2^2 b_3 + 2^1 b_2 + 2^0 b_1 + 2^{-1} b_0$

$V_{DAC} = 4 b_3 + 2 b_2 + 1 b_1 + 0.5 b_0$

This means the LSB (step size) is exactly $0.5 \text{ V}$.

The up-counter increments, raising $V_{DAC}$ in $0.5 \text{ V}$ steps: 0V, 0.5V, 1.0V... until $V_{DAC} > V_{in}$.

Since $V_{in} = 6.2 \text{ V}$, the counter will reach decimal 12 ($V_{DAC} = 6.0 \text{ V}$), which is still $< 6.2 \text{ V}$.

It increments once more to decimal 13 ($V_{DAC} = 6.5 \text{ V}$). Now $V_{DAC} > V_{in}$. The comparator outputs "0", the AND gate closes, and the counter stops.

Steady State Error:

$\text{Error} = |V_{DAC} - V_{in}| = |6.5 \text{ V} - 6.2 \text{ V}| = 0.3 \text{ V}$

Question 12 (GATE EC 2007)

In the Digital-to-Analog converter circuit shown in the figure below, $V_R = 10\text{V}$ and $R = 10\text{K}\Omega$. The voltage $V_o$ is

  • A) -0.781 V
  • B) -1.562 V
  • C) -3.125 V
  • D) -6.250 V
View Solution (Correct Answer: C)

In this R-2R ladder, the total resistance seen by the source $V_R$ is exactly $R$.

Total current entering the ladder: $I = \frac{V_R}{R} = \frac{10\text{V}}{10\text{K}\Omega} = 1\text{mA}$.

This current splits in half at every node down the ladder. The branch currents through the $2R$ resistors are: $\frac{I}{2}$ (MSB), $\frac{I}{4}$, $\frac{I}{8}$, and $\frac{I}{16}$ (LSB).

Looking at the schematic, the branches connected to the op-amp's virtual ground are the 2nd and 4th branches (digital input $0101_2$).

The total current entering the inverting terminal is:

$I_{in} = \frac{I}{4} + \frac{I}{16} = \frac{5I}{16}$

Output voltage $V_o$ is determined by the feedback resistor $R$:

$V_o = -R \times I_{in} = -R \times \left(\frac{5I}{16}\right)$

$V_o = -10\text{K}\Omega \times \left( \frac{5 \times 1\text{mA}}{16} \right) = \frac{-50}{16}\text{V} = -3.125\text{V}$

Question 16 (GATE EC 2003)

The circuit shown in the figure is a 4 bit DAC. The input bits 0 and 1 are represented by 0 and 5 V respectively. The OP AMP is ideal, but all the resistance and the 5 v inputs have a tolerance of $\pm 10\%$. The specification (rounded to nearest multiple of 5%) for the tolerance of the DAC is

  • A) $\pm 35\%$
  • B) $\pm 20\%$
  • C) $\pm 10\%$
  • D) $\pm 5\%$

The output voltage for this inverting summing amplifier (DAC) is:

$V_0 = -V_R \cdot \left[ \frac{d_3 \cdot R_f}{R} + \frac{d_2 \cdot R_f}{2R} + \frac{d_1 \cdot R_f}{4R} + \frac{d_0 \cdot R_f}{8R} \right]$

Since the feedback resistor $R_f = R$, the equation simplifies to:

$V_0 = -V_R \cdot \frac{R}{R} [\text{constant}]$

To find the worst-case tolerance, we assume maximum positive error in the numerator and maximum negative error in the denominator.

Since $V_R$, $R_f$ (numerator), and $R$ (denominator) all have a $\pm 10\%$ tolerance, the worst-case multiplier is:

$V_{0(\text{worst})} = \frac{1.1 \times 1.1}{0.9} = \frac{1.21}{0.9} = 1.3444$

This corresponds to a $+34.44\%$ deviation. Rounding to the nearest multiple of $5\%$, the tolerance is $\pm 35\%$.
