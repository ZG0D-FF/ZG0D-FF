Sample Essay 1: Full Text

📄 Essay

In allocating limited resources, governments must prioritize sectors that yield the most significant benefits for society. Public health and education are foundational pillars impacting a nation's well-being and economic growth. While arts and culture enrich society, they should not precede essential services like healthcare and education.

Investing in public health ensures a healthier population, reducing disease burden and increasing productivity. For instance, countries that allocate substantial budgets to healthcare often experience lower mortality rates and higher life expectancy. A study by the World Health Organization indicates that nations prioritizing healthcare spending have witnessed improved overall health outcomes.

Education, similarly, is a catalyst for economic development and social mobility. An educated populace contributes to a skilled workforce, driving innovation and competitiveness. According to UNESCO, each additional year of schooling can increase a person's income by up to 10%, highlighting the economic benefits of investing in education.

While arts and culture are vital for preserving heritage and fostering creativity, they often rely on private patronage and can thrive without substantial government funding. In contrast, public health and education systems require consistent and significant investment to function effectively.

Therefore, governments should allocate resources primarily to public health and education, ensuring the foundational needs of society are met before directing funds to arts and culture.

Sample Essay 2: Full Text

📄 Essay

Technological advancements have undeniably transformed our lives, offering once-unimaginable conveniences. However, these innovations have also introduced complexities that have complicated our lives.

One significant area where technology has added complexity is in the realm of communication. The proliferation of smartphones and social media platforms has led to an "always-on" culture, blurring the lines between work and personal life. A study by the American Psychological Association found that constant connectivity can lead to increased stress levels, as individuals feel compelled to respond to messages and emails promptly, regardless of the time or day.

Moreover, the rapid pace of technological change necessitates continuous learning and adaptation. Professionals across various industries must keep up with new tools, software updates, and platforms to remain relevant. This constant need to upskill can be overwhelming and time-consuming, diverting attention from other essential tasks.

Additionally, the convenience of online services has led to concerns about data privacy and security. With the increasing amount of personal information shared online, individuals are more vulnerable to data breaches and identity theft. According to a report by the Identity Theft Resource Center, there were over 1,100 data breaches in the United States in 2020 alone, exposing millions of records.

In conclusion, while technological advancements have brought significant benefits, they have also introduced complexities that have complicated our lives. Acknowledging and addressing these challenges is essential to ensure that technology enhances, rather than hinders, our quality of life.

Module 2, Topic 1 — Analytical Writing

GRE Argument & Issue Task Parsing • University of Engineering & Management, Kolkata • ESEP-V

The GRE Analytical Writing Assessment (AWA) consists of two 30-minute timed tasks: the Issue Task, where you take and defend a position on a general statement, and the Argument Task, where you critique the logical soundness of someone else's argument rather than state your own opinion. Both are scored on a 0–6 scale.

1. The Issue Task — Building a Case

You are given a one- or two-sentence opinion claim on education, technology, society, or leadership. Your job is to evaluate it and build a convincing, well-supported argument within 30 minutes.

Think Like a Defense Lawyer

The prompt is best treated as a courtroom case statement rather than an invitation for a personal opinion. You are the lead lawyer building a case, not a bystander sharing a view.

  • Thesis Statement — your opening statement / verdict.
  • Body Examples — your key eyewitness evidence.
  • Addressing the Counterpoint — anticipating opposing counsel's objection.

What Graders Value Most

  • Depth over brevity — aim for roughly 500–600 well-reasoned words with no fluff.
  • Critical thinking — show why your position holds up under realistic conditions, not just that you hold it.
  • Logical flow — clear paragraph transitions that act like road signs for the reader.

The 5-20-5 Minute Strategy

  • Minutes 0–5, Outline — deconstruct the prompt, choose your stance, and pick 2–3 real examples.
  • Minutes 5–15, Intro & Body 1 — write a clear thesis and the first body paragraph with your strongest evidence.
  • Minutes 15–25, Body 2 & Refutation — write the second evidence point and address counterargument nuances.
  • Minutes 25–30, Polish — review spelling and sentence flow, and confirm the conclusion matches the thesis.

The 5-Paragraph Blueprint

  • Paragraph 1, Introduction — reframe the prompt statement, acknowledge its complexity, state your thesis, and preview your main supporting points.
  • Paragraphs 2–3, Body — topic sentence + a concrete example (see S.H.E.E.P.) + an explicit explanation of why it proves your thesis.
  • Paragraphs 4–5, Rebuttal & Conclusion — address the limit of the opposing view, counter it logically, then synthesize the thesis without introducing new ideas.

Stance Strategy: Avoid Extremes

The trap — absolute thinking: avoid claims like "I completely agree without exception," since real-world issues are rarely black or white (e.g. "technology is 100% harmful to human intelligence" reads as naive).

The win — qualified nuance: use a qualified stance such as "While X has validity in specific scenarios, Y is overall more persuasive because..."

The S.H.E.E.P. Framework — Sourcing Examples

A quick-recall device for where to draw supporting examples:

  • Science & Technology — AI breakthroughs, space exploration, medical vaccines, history of automation.
  • History & Economics — the Industrial Revolution, the Great Depression, historic leadership decisions.
  • Education & Politics — national education policy, government regulation, civic-rights movements.

(The remaining two letters draw on Personal/observed experience and additional domain-specific knowledge — the goal is to never be caught without a ready example.)

The T-E-C Structure for a Nuanced Essay

  • T — Thesis: takes a clear, qualified position rather than an absolute one.
  • E — Evidence: a specific, real-world case that directly proves the thesis's claim, not a vague generalization.
  • C — Counterevidence: an objection is conceded, then rebutted — absorbing the counterpoint before explaining why the thesis still holds.
Worked Illustration — Prompt: "Reliance on tech weakens human critical thinking."
A qualified thesis reframes this as: technology alters how we think rather than weakening mental capacity, supported by evidence such as GPS navigation shifting drivers' cognitive effort from memorizing routes to complex traffic decisions, and medical AI letting radiologists shift focus from detection to treatment planning.

Winning Essay Techniques

  • Signpost words — guide the reader with clear transitions: "Furthermore," "Conversely," "Consequently," "In spite of this."
  • The "So What?" rule — never just narrate an example; spend 2–3 sentences explaining why it directly proves the thesis.
  • Concede & rebut — acknowledge the counterargument ("Critics argue that...") and then show why its premise is limited or flawed.
  • Formal academic tone — avoid slang and contractions (don't, can't); keep vocabulary elevated yet crisp.

2. The Argument Task — Evaluating Logic, Not Opinion

The Argument Task presents a short passage in which someone draws a conclusion from stated evidence. Unlike the Issue Task, you do not argue for or against the conclusion itself — you critique whether the reasoning used to reach it is logically sound.

What to Look For When Parsing the Argument

  • Unstated assumptions — gaps the author must be taking for granted for the conclusion to follow from the evidence given.
  • Alternative explanations — other plausible causes for the same evidence that the author has not ruled out.
  • Weak or unrepresentative evidence — small samples, biased surveys, or evidence that does not clearly generalize to the conclusion.
  • Faulty analogies or comparisons — situations presented as equivalent that differ in relevant ways (different population, era, scale, or context).
  • Vague or shifting terms — key words used loosely, or a term whose meaning quietly changes between the evidence and the conclusion.

Suggested Structure

  • Paragraph 1 — briefly restate the argument's conclusion and identify that you will examine the reasoning that supports it.
  • Paragraphs 2–4 — each paragraph isolates one specific logical flaw, explains why it weakens the argument, and states what additional evidence would resolve it.
  • Final paragraph — summarize how, taken together, these gaps leave the argument unconvincing as written.

The 5-20-5 timing strategy applies equally to the Argument Task, substituting flaw-identification for thesis-building in the outline stage.

Self-Check: Analytical Writing — 6 MCQs

Q1

What is the GRE Analytical Writing Assessment score scale?

  • A. 0–10
  • B. 0–6
  • C. 1–5
  • D. 0–100
Show Answer
B — Both the Issue and Argument tasks are scored on a 0–6 scale.

Q2

How much time is given for each AWA task?

  • A. 20 minutes
  • B. 45 minutes
  • C. 30 minutes
  • D. 60 minutes
Show Answer
C — Each task — Issue and Argument — has a strict 30-minute limit.

Q3

In the courtroom analogy for the Issue Task, what does the thesis statement represent?

  • A. Eyewitness evidence
  • B. Opposing counsel's objection
  • C. Your opening statement / verdict
  • D. The judge's ruling
Show Answer
C — The thesis is framed as the writer's opening statement or verdict, as the lead lawyer building a case.

Q4

According to the scoring priorities, what should an Issue Task response aim for?

  • A. As many examples as possible, regardless of depth
  • B. 500–600 well-reasoned words with no fluff
  • C. A strictly one-sided, absolute position
  • D. Under 200 words for maximum concision
Show Answer
B — Graders value depth over brevity — roughly 500–600 well-reasoned words.

Q5

In the 5-20-5 minute strategy, what should happen in the first 5 minutes?

  • A. Write the conclusion
  • B. Deconstruct the prompt, choose a stance, and pick 2–3 examples
  • C. Proofread for spelling errors
  • D. Write the strongest body paragraph
Show Answer
B — The opening 0–5 minutes are for outlining: deconstructing the prompt, choosing a stance, and selecting examples.

Q6

Why is "Technology is 100% harmful to human intelligence" considered a weak thesis?

  • A. It is too short
  • B. It is an example of absolute thinking that sounds naive and uncritical
  • C. It does not mention technology directly
  • D. It uses contractions
Show Answer
B — Absolute claims without qualification are flagged as the 'trap' — real-world issues are rarely black or white.
