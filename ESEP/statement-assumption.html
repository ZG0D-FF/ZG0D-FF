Statement and Assumption

Critical Reasoning for SSC / IBPS / RRB / CAT / GRE-GMAT style exams

1. Introduction: Critical Reasoning in Competitive Exams

Critical Reasoning is a core Verbal/Logical Ability component in almost every major competitive and admission examination — SSC CGL/CHSL, IBPS/SBI PO & Clerk, RRB NTPC, insurance exams (LIC AAO, NIACL), CAT, XAT, MAT, CMAT, and GRE/GMAT Analytical sections. Unlike grammar or vocabulary items, these questions do not test language knowledge — they test how tightly a candidate can reason from a given statement to what it logically requires, implies, or does not require.

The "Statement and Assumption" question type is one of the most frequently repeated formats across these exams because it directly measures a skill engineers and managers use daily: distinguishing what a piece of information actually establishes from what the reader is quietly supplying on their own.

2. What Is an Assumption?

An assumption is something that is taken for granted or supposed, without being explicitly stated in a passage or argument. It is the unstated premise that must be true for the statement's stated action, claim, or conclusion to make logical sense.

Working definition for exam purposes: An assumption is implicit — it is never written in the statement itself, yet the statement's speaker clearly believes it and relies on it.
EXAMPLE

A notice reads, "All employees must submit their medical certificates by Friday to be eligible for reimbursement."

  • Implicit assumption: Employees are capable of obtaining and submitting medical certificates within this timeframe.
  • NOT an assumption: "Some employees may not submit on time" — this is a possible outcome, not something the statement takes for granted.

3. Assumption vs. Inference vs. Conclusion — Do Not Confuse These

TermDefinitionDirection of Reasoning
AssumptionAn unstated premise the statement relies on to hold trueWorks backward — what must have been true BEFORE the statement was made
InferenceA conclusion drawn FROM the given factsWorks forward — what follows AFTER the statement
ConclusionThe main point an argument is explicitly building towardStated (or near-stated) within the passage itself

4. The Negation Test (Most Important Rule for This Topic)

Almost every competitive exam question of this type is best solved using the Negation Test, developed for GMAT/CAT-style critical reasoning and equally valid for SSC/Bank exams:

  1. Negate the assumption (make it false / reverse it).
  2. Check: does the original statement's claim, action, or plan still make logical sense if the assumption is false?
  3. If the statement falls apart or becomes illogical → the assumption IS implicit (necessary).
  4. If the statement still stands even without it → the assumption is NOT implicit (irrelevant or too broad).
Exam tip: An assumption must be (i) implicit — never stated, (ii) necessary — the statement collapses without it, and (iii) minimal — it should not be broader, more general, or more extreme than what the statement requires.

5. Common Statement Types Seen in Exams

  • Advertisements and public notices ("Join our institute for guaranteed placement")
  • Official circulars, policy statements, government orders
  • Warning signs and instructions ("Do not touch the wet paint")
  • Newspaper reports and editorial claims
  • Cause–effect and recommendation statements ("The company should increase the ad budget to boost sales")

6. Standard Traps to Avoid

  • Choosing an option that is TRUE in general but not something the statement itself depends on.
  • Choosing an option that is too broad/extreme (uses words like "all," "always," "never," "only") when the statement only requires a modest, specific assumption.
  • Mistaking a possible consequence (inference) for an assumption (something presupposed before the fact).
  • Assuming outside/real-world knowledge not warranted by the statement's own logic.

7. Step-by-Step Strategy for the Exam

  1. Read the statement once and identify exactly what action, claim, or recommendation is being made.
  2. Ask: "What does the speaker need to believe is true for this action/claim to make sense?"
  3. Apply the Negation Test to each option/assumption given.
  4. Eliminate options that are too general, are restatements of the statement, or introduce new unrelated information.
  5. Select the option that is both necessary and minimal.

8. Worked Example

STATEMENT

"To reduce the number of road accidents in the city, the traffic police have decided to install speed cameras at all major intersections."

ASSUMPTION

Drivers who know they are being monitored by speed cameras are likely to reduce their speed.

NEGATION CHECK

If drivers do NOT reduce speed even when monitored, installing cameras would not help reduce accidents — the plan collapses. Since the statement's entire logic depends on this belief, it IS a valid, necessary assumption.
