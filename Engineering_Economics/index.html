Economic Decision Making

Decision-making is the broad topic because it deals with aspect of everyday human existence. The main focus is to develop the tools to properly analyze and solve the economic problems that are commonly faced by engineers. Even complex situations can be broken down into components to form a sensible solution. The understanding of decision – making process is important because the tools of decision – making is useful for obtaining realistic comparisons between alternatives so that I can make better decision.

Engineering economic analysis focuses on cost, revenues and benefits that occur at different times. Most of the things that engineers design calls for spending money in the design and building stages and after completion revenues or benefits occur – usually for years. Thus the economic analysis of cost, benefits and revenues occurring over time is called engineering economics.

Example: A civil engineer designs a road or a dam, the construction costs occurs in near future but the benefits to users begin when construction is finished and continue for a long period of time.

Element in decision – making

  1. To have a decision – making situation there must be at-least two alternatives available. If only one course of action is available, there can be no decision – making because there is nothing to decide.
  2. The decision should be rational. (Out of five horses running on a race track if someone chooses its betting horse by closing eyes and pointing finger on a number then it is not rational)

Engineering Costs

Fixed cost: Constant or unchanging regardless of the level of output or activity. This includes the cost of building, the machinery, the land which does not change with change in production level.
Variable cost: Depend upon the level of output or activity, this includes the electricity bill, labor cost, raw materials, shipping fees which changes as the production level changes. These are generally linear (y equals a constant times x); however, the variable cost can also be nonlinear. For example, employees are often paid at 150% of their hourly rate for overtime hours.
Total cost: It is the summation of fixed cost and variable cost.

Marginal Cost: It is the addition to total cost for one additional unit of output production. This explains by how much the cost increases if the output production increases from 55 units to 56 units. It is the ratio of change in total cost to change in output. This is used to decide whether it is rational to produce the additional unit of output.
Average cost: It is the total cost divided by the number of units of output produced. If the total cost of producing 100 pens is Rs. 400 then the average cost of producing the pen is Rs. 400/100 = Rs. 4.

Cost Estimating (Continued)

Sunk Cost: Money spent as a result of past decision. Example: dollars spent last year to purchase new production machinery is money that is sunk because it has already be spent and there is nothing that can be at present to change that action. Engineering economists deal with present and future opportunities so this sunk cost should be disregarded in engineering economics as current decision cannot change the past.
Opportunity Cost: An opportunity cost is the benefit that is forgone by engaging a business resource in a chosen activity instead of engaging the same resource in the forgone activity. When a resource is used in one activity instead of another an opportunity cost is incurred. Every time a business resource (equipment, money, man-power, time, etc.) is used in one activity, the opportunity to use the same resource at that time in some other activity is lost. Each resource that a firm owns can feasibly be used in several alternative ways. The benefit that would be derived by using the resource in an alternative activity is the opportunity cost for using it in the chosen activity. It is the forgone opportunity because we are forgoing the benefit that could have been realized.

Recurring and nonrecurring costs: Recurring costs are anticipated and occurs at regular intervals. It includes resurfacing a highway or re-shingling a roof, so annual expenses for maintenance and operation are examples of recurring costs.

Nonrecurring costs occurs at irregular intervals and hence, it is difficult to plan for or anticipate. It includes emergency maintenance expenses, installing new machines, disposal or close-down cost.

In engineering economic analysis recurring costs are modeled as cash flows that occur at regular intervals (such as every year or every 5 year). Their magnitude can be estimated, and they can be estimated in the overall analysis. Nonrecurring cost can be handled easily if anticipation of timing and size can be predicted.
Incremental cost: Fundamental principles in engineering economic analysis is that in making a choice among a set of competing alternatives, focus should be placed on the differences between those alternatives. It is the extra cost associated with choosing an alternative over another. Positive incremental cost means something is more costly than another, while negative incremental cost indicates there would be a savings.

Cash cost vs Book cost: A cash-cost requires the cash transaction of money “out of one person’s pocket into the pocket of someone else.” This is a cash flow from someone to someone. Life cycle costing refers to the concept of designing products, goods and services with a full and explicit recognition of the associated costs over the various phrases of their life cycles. Two key concept in life-cycle costing are that ‘the later design changes are made, the higher the cost’, and that decisions made early in the life cycle tend to “lock in” cost that are incurred later. The downstream product (products created using raw materials from earlier production stages) changes are more costly and upstream changes (focus on raw material, design, foundational development) are easier (and less costly) to make.

When planner try to save money at an early design stage, the result is often a poor design, calling for change orders during construction and prototype development. These changes, in turn, are more costly than working out a better design would have been. The life cycle effects that engineers should consider at the design time include product costs for liability, production, material, testing and quality assurance and maintenance and warranty. Other life-cycle effects include product features based on customer input and product disposal effects on the environment. The engineers should consider all life cycle cost..

Unit I: Inflation and Price Change

Every engineering project involves cash flows distributed across several years. Because the purchasing power of money degrades over time, engineering economic analysis must explicitly account for price changes and inflation to maintain rigorous, unbiased investment comparisons.

1.1 Definition & Nature of Inflation

Inflation: The decline in the purchasing power of money over time, manifested as a continuous, compounded rise in the general price level of goods and services across an economy.
Deflation: The condition where the purchasing power of currency rises and the general price level drops. Analytically, deflation uses the identical formulas as inflation with the sign of the inflation rate f reversed.

Because engineering capital expenditures frequently span 5 to 25 years, even modest annual inflation rates compound into major distortions. A rupee or dollar received in year 10 possesses substantially diminished purchasing capability compared to a base-year dollar.

1.2 Effects of Inflation on Engineering Projects

  • Purchasing power depletion: Fixed monetary sums purchase diminishing volumes of physical capital and labor over time.
  • Cost escalation: Estimates for materials, energy, and contracted labor escalate throughout the operational life.
  • Illusory profitability: Revenues reported in then-current actual currency appear artificially inflated without delivering greater real wealth.
  • Interest rate premia: Capital lenders increase nominal lending rates to compensate for the real purchasing power lost during loan durations.
  • Distorted evaluations: Mixing constant-dollar figures with actual future cash flows skews net present worth rankings.
  • Fixed-income erosion: Fixed contracts, amortized leases, or predetermined annuities undergo continuous real decay.

1.3 Root Causes of Inflation

  1. Demand-Pull Inflation: Aggregate demand for goods and services outpaces production capacity at current prices. Buyers bid prices upward.
  2. Cost-Push Inflation: Input costs (crude energy, baseline wages, raw commodities) rise abruptly, compelling producers to transmit higher costs forward.
  3. Monetary Inflation: The money supply expands at a rate exceeding the growth of real economic output, devaluing individual currency units.

1.4 Price Change Tracking with Indexes

A price index measures the relative price of a commodity, service, or composite basket relative to an arbitrary base period (normalized to 100).

Price Index (I_t) = (P_t / P_0) × 100
Average Compound Annual Inflation Rate (f) = (I_t / I_0)^(1/n) − 1
Example Problem 6: Cement Price Index & Annual Inflation

Scenario A: A bag of cement costs ₹300 in the base year (t=0) and ₹390 five years later (t=5).
Price Index: I₅ = (390 / 300) × 100 = 130 (+30% price rise).

Scenario B: A general construction index climbs from 100 to 150 over 8 years.
Compound Rate: f = (150 / 100)^(1/8) − 1 = (1.50)^0.125 − 1 ≈ 5.199% per year.

1.5 Types of Index Numbers

  • Price Index: Monitors unit price variance of a specific commodity or defined basket.
  • Quantity Index: Measures physical volume produced or consumed, holding price constant.
  • Consumer Price Index (CPI): Measures retail cost of a fixed household consumption bundle.
  • Wholesale / Producer Price Index (WPI / PPI): Evaluates commercial prices at factory or wholesale stages.

1.6 Composite vs. Commodity Indexes

Commodity Index: Tracks a singular material (e.g., copper wire, structural steel). Crucial when project expenditure is heavily concentrated in one input.
Composite Index: Blends weighted prices of diverse resources (labor, aggregates, fuel) into an aggregate benchmark (e.g., construction cost index).

1.7 Cash Flow Conversions & Fisher Relation

Actual Dollars (then-current): The nominal amount changing hands in future period t.
Constant Dollars (real): Currency normalized to base-year purchasing power.

Actual $ = Constant $ × (1 + f)^n
Constant $ = Actual $ / (1 + f)^n
Combined (Fisher) Relation: i = i' + f + (i' × f)

Where: i = nominal market interest rate, i' = real inflation-free rate, f = general inflation rate.

Example Problem 7: Fisher Combined Rate & Purchasing Power

Part 1 (Purchasing Power): With annual inflation f = 6%, constant ₹1,00,000 required in 5 years translates to:
Actual $ = 1,00,000 × (1.06)⁵ = ₹1,33,823.

Part 2 (Fisher Combined Rate): If required real rate i' = 8% and expected inflation f = 5%:
i = 0.08 + 0.05 + (0.08 × 0.05) = 0.13 + 0.004 = 13.40%.

Unit II: Present Worth Analysis

Present Worth (PW) analysis reduces all prospective cash receipts and disbursements to an equivalent single lump sum at time zero (t=0), establishing a direct, objective basis to evaluate mutually exclusive engineering alternatives.

2.1 End-of-Year Convention

End-of-Year Assumption: All operational revenues and expenditures occurring throughout year t are treated as an aggregated lump sum transacted on the final day of year t. This standardizes compound interest discounting with minimal approximation error.

2.2 Viewpoints of Analysis

Every analysis must maintain an unwavering perspective:

  • Total Investment Viewpoint (Conventional): Considers total project capital, irrespective of equity vs. debt financing. Debt service is treated separately.
  • Borrowed Money Viewpoint (Investor's Equity): Considers only direct equity contributions as initial outlay; interest payments and principal amortization are included as annual cash outflows.
Integrity Warning: Never mix viewpoints. Subtracting debt amortization while treating total borrowed capital as equity outlay double-counts financing charges and renders PW erroneous.

2.4 Inflation Discounting Consistency

Cash Flow Type Required Discount Rate Analytical Validity
Actual Dollars (Then-current) Nominal Market Rate (i) Valid: Matches inflated cash flows with market inflation premia.
Constant Dollars (Base-year) Real Rate (i') Valid: Evaluates pure purchasing power against real productivity return.
Actual Dollars Real Rate (i') Invalid Error: Under-discounts, grossly overstating project viability.
Constant Dollars Nominal Rate (i) Invalid Error: Double-discounts for inflation, heavily penalizing viable projects.

2.5 Effect of Income Taxes & Depreciation

Engineering evaluations must proceed on an after-tax cash flow (ATCF) basis:

Taxable Income = Revenues − Operating Expenses − Depreciation
Income Tax = Taxable Income × Tax Rate (t_c)
ATCF = (Revenues − Operating Expenses) × (1 − t_c) + (Depreciation × t_c)

Depreciation is non-cash but produces a vital tax shield: Depreciation × t_c.

2.6 Economic Decision Criteria

Problem Constraint Governing Economic Criterion
Fixed Input: Fixed capital / resources available Maximize Present Worth of Benefits (PW_B)
Fixed Output: Mandated service level / deliverables Minimize Present Worth of Costs (PW_C)
Neither Input nor Output Fixed Maximize Net Present Worth: NPW = PW_B − PW_C (Subject to NPW ≥ 0)

2.7 Present Worth Techniques & Machine Evaluation

Uniform Series PW Factor: (P/A, i, n) = [(1 + i)^n − 1] / [i × (1 + i)^n]
Net Present Worth: PW = −P₀ + A × (P/A, i, n) + S × (1 + i)^(−n)
Example Problem 8: Machine Purchase Present Worth

A production machine requires initial investment P₀ = ₹5,00,000, returns uniform net savings A = ₹1,50,000 annually for n = 5 years, with MARR i = 10%. Salvage value is negligible (S = 0).

Factor: (P/A, 10%, 5) = [(1.10)⁵ − 1] / [0.10 × (1.10)⁵] = 0.61051 / 0.161051 ≈ 3.79079.
PW = −5,00,000 + 1,50,000 × (3.79079) = −5,00,000 + 5,68,618 = +₹68,618.
Decision: PW > 0; project is economically justified.

2.8 Evaluating Multiple Alternatives & Capitalized Cost

Alternatives must be evaluated over identical study periods:

  • Equal Lives: Directly compute and compare PW.
  • Unequal Lives: Use Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) of asset lifetimes, or a fixed planning horizon with terminal salvage imputation.
  • Perpetual Projects: Capitalized Cost analysis for infinite service durations.
Capitalized Cost (CC) = P₀ + (A / i)
Example Problem 9: Capitalized Cost of Perpetual Infrastructure

A regional drainage culvert costs initial P₀ = ₹20,00,000 to construct and mandates annual recurring maintenance A = ₹1,00,000 in perpetuity. Nominal discount rate i = 8%.

Perpetual Endowment: A / i = 1,00,000 / 0.08 = ₹12,50,000.
Total Capitalized Cost: CC = 20,00,000 + 12,50,000 = ₹32,50,000.

Unit III: Uncertainty in Future Events

Every engineering estimate reflects projections into an unresolved future. Unit III establishes quantitative methods to describe, model, and optimize decisions under explicit uncertainty and risk.

3.1 Estimates & Sensitivity Ranges

Three-Point Sensitivity Range: When deterministic point values mask operational vulnerability, parameters are evaluated across three discrete states:
  1. Optimistic (Best-case): Favorable cost and revenue conditions.
  2. Most-Likely (Mode): Highest subjective probability under normal operations.
  3. Pessimistic (Worst-case): Unfavorable macroeconomic or operational disruptions.

Example: Annual savings projected at ₹80,000 (pessimistic), ₹1,10,000 (most-likely), and ₹1,40,000 (optimistic) bounds the present worth distribution before undertaking stochastic modeling.

3.3 Probability & Joint Distributions

Probabilities represent outcome likelihoods satisfying Kolmogorov axioms: 0 ≤ P(E_j) ≤ 1 and Σ P(E_j) = 1.0.

Independent Joint Probability: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

If high market demand has P(A) = 0.40 and an independent raw material price hike has P(B) = 0.50, their simultaneous joint occurrence is: 0.40 × 0.50 = 0.20 (20%).

3.5 Expected Value (EV)

Expected Value: E[X] = Σ [ p_j × X_j ], where Σ p_j = 1.0
Example Problem 10: Expected Profit Calculation

A manufacturing venture forecasts annual return across three states of nature:

  • High Demand: X₁ = ₹2,00,000 with p₁ = 0.30
  • Normal Demand: X₂ = ₹3,00,000 with p₂ = 0.50
  • Low Demand: X₃ = ₹1,00,000 with p₃ = 0.20
E[X] = (0.30 × 2,00,000) + (0.50 × 3,00,000) + (0.20 × 1,00,000) = 60,000 + 1,50,000 + 20,000 = ₹2,20,000.

3.7 Risk vs. Return & Monte Carlo Simulation

Risk: The dispersion (variance/standard deviation) of actual cash flow returns around expected value, and specifically the downside probability of financial loss.

Risk vs. Return: Projects exhibiting wide probability dispersions mandate higher Minimum Attractive Rates of Return (MARR) to compensate investors for capital risk.

Monte Carlo Simulation: When multiple stochastic variables (unit demand, inflation, energy tariffs) interact non-linearly, analysts sample random variates across thousands of simulated project life cycles to generate complete continuous Present Worth probability density curves.

3.10 Real Options in Capital Budgeting

Unlike passive financial models, physical projects offer managerial flexibility to alter decisions over time as uncertainty resolves:

  • Option to Defer: Postpone initial capital commitment until market demand or technological feasibility solidifies (e.g., holding land lease without building).
  • Option to Expand / Contract: Build scalable infrastructure capable of doubling capacity or down-scaling with market shifts.
  • Option to Abandon: Liquidate physical assets for salvage if revenues collapse below operating threshold, bounding downside losses.
  • Option to Switch: Design dual-fuel generation plants or flexible manufacturing tooling to alternate feedstocks as spot prices shift.

Section 9: Time Value of Money & Interest Formulas

The purchasing power of money at a particular time is designated as the Time Value of Money (TVM). Capital received at a prospective future date inherently possesses lower real present value than an identical nominal sum held today.

Foundational Illustration: If ₹200 is deposited in a bank at 10% annual interest, the interest earned after 1 year is ₹20. The principal grows to ₹220 at the conclusion of the year. Conversely, receiving ₹200 one year hence is worth only ₹181.82 today at that discount rate.

Why Money Possesses Greater Value Today

  1. Liquidity & Investment Earning Power: Capital held immediately can be deployed productively into income-generating opportunities or interest-bearing assets to earn compounded returns.
  2. Consumption Preference & Risk Aversion: Individuals generally prefer present consumption over deferred consumption because the future is inherently contingent, uncertain, and exposed to risk.
  3. Productive Employment of Capital: Physical capital and machinery produce positive physical marginal output when combined with labor and materials.
  4. Purchasing Power Inflation: During periods of general price inflation, a rupee or dollar today commands greater command over real physical commodities than the same nominal currency unit one year later.

Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

Simple Interest: Calculated exclusively on the original initial principal for every interest period. Cumulative interest is directly proportional to time:
I = P × n × i  |  F = P × (1 + n × i)
Compound Interest: Interest for any current period is computed on the total accumulated balance (initial principal plus all accumulated interest accrued up to the end of the preceding period):
F = P × (1 + i)^n

Standard Factor Notation

  • P: Present principal sum or equivalent present worth at time zero (t=0).
  • F: Future lump sum at the end of period n.
  • A: Equal, uniform payment occurring at the end of every interest period for n periods.
  • G: Uniform arithmetic gradient amount added or subtracted period-after-period to/from base payment.
  • i: Effective interest rate per interest period.
  • n: Number of compounding interest periods.

1. Single-Payment Compound Amount (F/P Factor)

Finds the future sum F resulting from initial outlay P after n periods at compounding rate i.

F = P × (1 + i)^n
F = P × (F/P, i, n)
Example Problem 11: Single-Payment Compound Maturity (F/P)

A person deposits initial sum P = ₹20,000 at interest rate i = 18% compounded annually for n = 10 years. Find maturity value F.
F = 20,000 × (1 + 0.18)¹⁰ = 20,000 × (1.18)¹⁰ = 20,000 × 5.2338356 = ₹1,04,676.71.

Finds the present worth P required now to realize future sum F after n periods at discount rate i.

3. Equal-Payment Series Compound Amount (F/A Factor)

Evaluates future value F accumulated by an annuity series of n equal end-of-period payments A.

F = A × [ ((1 + i)^n − 1) / i ]
F = A × (F/A, i, n)

Factor: (F/A, 20%, 25) = [(1.20)²⁵ − 1] / 0.20 = [95.396216 − 1] / 0.20 = 471.98108.
F = 10,000 × 471.98108 = ₹47,19,810.80.

4. Equal-Payment Series Sinking Fund (A/F Factor)

Determines periodic contribution A required to accumulate predetermined future target F over n periods.

A = F × [ i / ((1 + i)^n − 1) ]
A = F × (A/F, i, n)

5. Equal-Payment Series Present Worth (P/A Factor)

Determines lump-sum present worth P equivalent to uniform annuity receipts A across n periods at rate i.

P = A × [ ((1 + i)^n − 1) / (i × (1 + i)^n) ]
P = A × (P/A, i, n)
Example Problem 15: Employee Welfare Reserve Fund (P/A)

A corporation commits an annual welfare disbursement A = ₹10,00,000 for n = 20 years. Capital reserve fund compounds at i = 15% annually. Find single present deposit P needed now.
Factor: (P/A, 15%, 20) = [(1.15)²⁰ − 1] / [0.15 × (1.15)²⁰] = 15.366537 / 2.4549806 = 6.259331.
P = 10,00,000 × 6.259331 = ₹62,59,331.45.

6. Equal-Payment Series Capital Recovery (A/P Factor)

Computes periodic recovery installment A required to amortize initial loan or capital investment P over n periods.

A = P × [ (i × (1 + i)^n) / ((1 + i)^n − 1) ]
A = P × (A/P, i, n)

7. Uniform Gradient Series Factors (P/G & A/G)

When operational costs (e.g. maintenance, repair) increase or decrease by a constant amount G each interest period, the cash flows form an arithmetic gradient series.

Gradient Present Worth Factor:
(P/G, i, n) = [ ((1 + i)^n − i × n − 1) / (i² × (1 + i)^n) ]

Gradient Equivalent Uniform Series Factor:
(A/G, i, n) = (1 / i) − [ n / ((1 + i)^n − 1) ]

Total Equivalent Uniform Series: A_total = A₁ ± G × (A/G, i, n)
Example Problem 17: Uniform Arithmetic Gradient Series

A machine maintenance schedule mandates ₹200 at end of year 1, ₹250 at end of year 2, ₹300 at year 3, and ₹350 at year 4. Base payment A₁ = ₹200, constant gradient G = ₹50, n = 4 periods, discount rate i = 10%.

Factor (A/G, 10%, 4) = (1 / 0.10) − [ 4 / ((1.10)⁴ − 1) ] = 10 − [ 4 / 0.4641 ] = 10 − 8.6188 = 1.3812.
Equivalent Uniform Annual Cost: A_total = 200 + (50 × 1.3812) = 200 + 69.06 = ₹269.06 per year.
Present Worth: P_total = A_total × (P/A, 10%, 4) = 269.06 × 3.16987 = ₹852.89.

8. Ordinary Annuity vs. Annuity Due

Ordinary Annuity: Payments occur strictly at the end of each payment period (standard assumption in compound interest tables).

Annuity Due: Payments are made at the beginning of each period (e.g. lease rentals, advance insurance premiums).
PW(Annuity Due) = A + A × (P/A, i, n − 1) = A × [ 1 + (P/A, i, n − 1) ]
FW(Annuity Due) = A × (F/A, i, n) × (1 + i)

9. Debt Repayment & Full Loan Amortization Schedule

Debt repayment entails discharging credit obligations through contracted installments consisting of two distinct components:

  • Interest Payment (I_t): Compensation to lender for capital utilization: I_t = Beginning Balance_{t-1} × i.
  • Principal Amortization (PR_t): Actual retirement of original borrowed principal: PR_t = Annual Installment (A) − I_t.
  • Ending Balance: Balance_t = Balance_{t-1} − PR_t.

10. Nominal vs. Effective Interest Rate

Nominal Interest Rate (r): Stated annual rate ignoring compounding within the year (e.g. 12% p.a. compounded monthly).

Effective Interest Rate (EIR / i_e): Actual true economic annual yield factoring within-year compounding frequency m.
Effective Interest Rate (EIR): i_e = (1 + r / m)^m − 1
Continuous Compounding Limit: i_e = e^r − 1
Example Problem 18: Nominal vs. Effective Annual Rate (EIR)

A bank advertises a nominal loan rate r = 12% per annum, compounded monthly (m = 12 periods/year).
Period Interest Rate: i_per = 12% / 12 = 1.0% per month.
Effective Annual Rate: i_e = (1 + 0.12 / 12)¹² − 1 = (1.01)¹² − 1 = 1.126825 − 1 = 12.6825% p.a.

Question 2: Fixed Cost vs. Variable Cost (2 Marks)

Question: Explain the difference between fixed cost and variable cost, with one example of each.

  • Fixed Cost: Expenditures that remain constant, invariant, and unchanging regardless of the level of operational activity or production volume within a relevant range.
    Example: Factory building lease, tooling investments (e.g. ₹5,00,000 tooling in Problem 2), property taxes, and depreciation.
  • Variable Cost: Expenditures that fluctuate directly and proportionately with the level of output or operational throughput.
    Example: Direct manufacturing labor, raw materials consumed per unit, and electric power for production machinery.
Total Cost: TC = Fixed Cost (FC) + Variable Cost (v × Q)
Question 3: Why Sunk Cost is Disregarded (2 Marks)

Question: Explain why sunk cost is disregarded in engineering economic analysis.

Definition: A sunk cost is an expenditure that has already occurred in the past as the outcome of a prior decision and cannot be altered, recovered, or undone by any present or future choice.

Analytical Rationale:

  1. Engineering economic analysis focuses strictly on future prospective cash flows and incremental differences between competing forward-looking alternatives.
  2. Because a sunk outlay exists identically across all available future options (including doing nothing), it cancels out in differential cash flow comparisons.
  3. Factoring in sunk costs produces the irrational sunk cost fallacy, leading managers to misallocate fresh capital into unviable assets simply to "recover" historical, unalterable losses.
Question 4: Marginal Cost & Average Cost at 56 Units (2 Marks)

Problem: Total cost of producing 55 units is Rs. 2,200 and of producing 56 units is Rs. 2,250. Calculate the marginal cost of the 56th unit and the average cost at 56 units.

Marginal Cost: MC = ΔTC / ΔQ = (TC₂ − TC₁) / (Q₂ − Q₁)
Average Cost: AC = TC_Q / Q

1. Marginal Cost of the 56th unit:
MC₅₆ = (2,250 − 2,200) / (56 − 55) = 50 / 1 = Rs. 50.00.

2. Average Cost at 56 units:
AC₅₆ = 2,250 / 56 ≈ Rs. 40.18 per unit (exactly 40.17857 Rs./unit).

Economic Insight: Because MC₅₆ (Rs. 50) exceeds previous average cost AC₅₅ (2,200 / 55 = Rs. 40), producing the 56th unit exerts upward pressure on average cost.

Question 5: Learning Curve & Significance in Short Runs (2 Marks)

Question: Explain the learning curve and why it matters in short production runs.

Definition: The learning curve models the empirical phenomenon whereby input labor hours and unit costs decline at a predictable geometric rate each time cumulative production volume doubles:

Y_x = K × x^(−b)
where Y_x = labor required for x-th unit, K = labor for first unit, b = learning parameter.

Why It Matters in Short Production Runs:

  1. In short production runs, manufacturing never reaches the steady-state, plateaued region of the learning curve. The entire batch is produced along the initial, steep learning phase where workers and supervisors are actively adapting to tooling, fixtures, and tolerances.
  2. Initial units consume dramatically higher labor and overhead than long-run averages. Assuming steady-state labor standards severely understates production costs, causing catastrophic contract bidding deficits.
Question 6: Obsolete Electric Pumps Pricing Evaluation (2 Marks)

Problem: A distributor bought a lot of electric pumps three years ago for Rs. 6,30,000 and has spent Rs. 90,000 on storage so far. The pumps are now obsolete, and the lot would fetch only Rs. 2,70,000 today. The pricing manager wants to price the lot at Rs. 7,20,000 to recover the money tied up. Evaluate this reasoning using sunk cost and opportunity cost, and advise on the price.

1. Evaluation of Pricing Manager's Reasoning:

  • Sunk Cost Fallacy: The initial purchase price of Rs. 6,30,000 and accumulated storage fees of Rs. 90,000 (totaling Rs. 7,20,000) are past, unrecoverable historical outlays (sunk costs). Prospective buyers in an open market determine value based solely on present functional utility and competitive modern substitutes, not the distributor's past accounting costs. Pricing at Rs. 7,20,000 will result in zero sales while continuing to burn ongoing monthly storage fees.
  • Opportunity Cost: The true economic value of retaining the pumps is the benefit forgone by not selling them today, which is the immediate market-clearing sum of Rs. 2,70,000. Retaining the inventory incurs an opportunity cost equal to the investment return that Rs. 2,70,000 could earn if deployed elsewhere at the firm's MARR.

2. Professional Advice on Price:
Accept the prevailing market offer of Rs. 2,70,000 (or best competitive bid near it) immediately. Liquidating now stems continuous storage overhead leakage and frees up Rs. 2,70,000 of productive working capital.

Question 1: Four Categories of Cash Flow in an Engineering Project (2 Marks)

Question: State any four categories of cash flow in an engineering project.

  1. Initial Capital Expenditure (First Cost / Outlay, P₀): Capital spent at time zero on land acquisition, civil construction, machinery procurement, and commissioning.
  2. Annual Operating and Maintenance (O&M) Costs: Recurring expenditures required during operational life for direct labor, energy, consumable materials, and routine servicing.
  3. Periodic Revenues / Cost Savings: Inflows generated from product sales, user utility tariffs, or annual operational cost reductions.
  4. Terminal Salvage Value (S): Net cash proceeds recovered at the end of the project's functional life through asset liquidation, resale, or material recycling.

(Additional valid categories: Overhauls/Major Replacements, Corporate Income Taxes & Depreciation Tax Shields).

Question 2: Definition of IRR & Project Acceptance Condition (2 Marks)

Question: Define the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) and state the condition under which a project is accepted.

Definition: The Internal Rate of Return (IRR or i*) is the break-even discount rate that equates the present worth of cash inflows (benefits) to the present worth of cash outflows (costs), reducing the Net Present Worth (NPW) of the project to exactly zero:

NPW(IRR) = Σ [ (R_t − C_t) / (1 + IRR)^t ] = 0   (from t=0 to n)

Project Acceptance Criterion:

  • For an independent project, accept if and only if IRR ≥ MARR (Minimum Attractive Rate of Return). Reject if IRR < MARR.
  • (For mutually exclusive alternatives, select based on incremental rate of return: ΔIRR ≥ MARR).
Question 3: Equivalence of Cash Flows (2 Marks)

Question: Explain what is meant by equivalence of cash flows.

Concept of Economic Equivalence:

  • Cash flow equivalence means that distinct sums of money occurring at different points in time have identical economic purchasing value to an investor when evaluated at a designated compound interest rate (i).
  • Two cash flows are equivalent if an individual is entirely indifferent between receiving cash flow A today or cash flow B at a specified future date.
  • Example: Under a 10% interest rate, Rs. 100 today is economically equivalent to Rs. 110 one year from now, and Rs. 121 two years from now, because depositing Rs. 100 at 10% compound interest mathematically yields exactly those future sums.
Question 4: Break-Even Quantity Calculation (2 Marks)

Problem: A firm has fixed costs of Rs. 3,60,000, a selling price of Rs. 150 per unit, and a variable cost of Rs. 90 per unit. Calculate the break-even quantity.

Unit Contribution Margin (CM) = Price (P) − Variable Cost (v)
Break-Even Quantity: Q_BE = Fixed Costs (FC) / (P − v)

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Unit Contribution Margin = Rs. 150 − Rs. 90 = Rs. 60 per unit.
  2. Break-Even Quantity: Q_BE = 3,60,000 / 60 = 6,000 units.

Verification:
Total Revenue = 6,000 × Rs. 150 = Rs. 9,00,000.
Total Cost = Rs. 3,60,000 + (6,000 × Rs. 90) = Rs. 3,60,000 + Rs. 5,40,000 = Rs. 9,00,000.
Net Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost = Rs. 0 (Exact Break-Even Point Verified).

Question 5: Two Drawbacks of Benefit–Cost Analysis for Public Goods (2 Marks)

Question: Explain any two drawbacks of benefit–cost analysis for public goods.

  1. Difficulty in Monetizing Non-Market Intangibles: Public infrastructure delivers societal impacts (e.g. human lives saved, travel stress reduction, clean air, scenic preservation) that lack transparent market prices. Assigning shadow prices is subjective, contentious, and easily manipulated.
  2. Arbitrariness of the Social Discount Rate: Public projects span multiple decades or generations. Selecting a social discount rate is fundamentally normative: high rates penalize future generations by heavily discounting long-term environmental and safety benefits, while low rates justify capital-inefficient public overspending.

(Alternative valid drawback: Disregard for Income Distribution & Equity—B/C aggregates total monetary surplus irrespective of whether low-income citizens bear the disruption costs while affluent commuters reap the time savings).

Question 6: Public Bridge Project Benefit–Cost Evaluation (2 Marks)

Problem: A state government plans to build a bridge across a river so that vehicle users no longer take a long detour, saving travel time and fuel.
(a) Explain how the benefit–cost ratio would be used to decide whether the project is justified.
(b) Evaluate the limitations of relying on the benefit–cost ratio alone for this public project.

(a) Use of Benefit–Cost (B/C) Ratio for Justification:

  • Quantify the present worth (or equivalent uniform annual worth) of all direct public benefits (B): fuel savings + monetary value of commuter travel time saved − user disbenefits.
  • Quantify the government's total capital construction expenditure and maintenance outlays (C).
  • Compute the Benefit–Cost Ratio:
    B/C = PW(Public Benefits) / PW(Sponsor Costs)
  • Decision Rule: If B/C ≥ 1.0 (or Net B − C ≥ 0), the bridge generates net economic welfare in excess of public expenditure and is economically justified. If B/C < 1.0, it is rejected.

(b) Limitations of Relying on B/C Ratio Alone:

  1. Subjectivity of Value of Time (VOT): Travel time savings form the dominant benefit component. Valuing an hour saved requires debatable assumptions about wage rates, trip purposes, and whether small individual time increments (e.g. 5 minutes) translate into real economic productivity.
  2. Induced Traffic & Secondary Congestion: The new bridge may induce additional vehicle trips, causing bottleneck congestion on feeder roads and increased regional carbon emissions not captured in static origin-destination models.
  3. Ecological & Aesthetic Disruption: River hydrology alteration, wetland damage during piling, and construction noise pollution cannot be fully encapsulated in a financial ratio.
  4. Distributional Equity: The bridge may displace local ferry operators and adjacent residents, concentrating costs on a vulnerable subgroup while spreading benefits to motorized commuters. A single B/C ratio hides these distributional inequalities.
