Why the Cholas Matter

The Imperial Cholas were one of South India's most powerful and longest-ruling dynasties. Their legacy spans military conquest, maritime dominance across the Indian Ocean, revolutionary temple architecture, and the first democratic village assemblies in history.

Key Fact: The Brihadeeswarar Temple at Thanjavur, built by Rajaraja I, stands as the tallest temple tower in India and a UNESCO World Heritage Site — constructed entirely without mortar.

Territorial Expansion

  • Defeated Pandyas, Cheras, and Pallavas within South India
  • Made Sri Lanka a Chola province under Rajaraja I
  • Naval expeditions attacked the Srivijaya Empire in Southeast Asia under Rajendra I
  • Controlled the Bay of Bengal and major Indian Ocean trade routes
Vijayalaya Chola
c. 850 CE · Founder
Captured Thanjavur; founded the Imperial Chola dynasty by defeating the Pallavas and Pandyas.
Rajaraja Chola I
985–1014 CE
Expanded into Sri Lanka and the Maldives. Built the iconic Brihadeeswarar Temple. Organised detailed land surveys for taxation.
Rajendra Chola I
1014–1044 CE
Led naval expeditions to Southeast Asia and attacked the Srivijaya Empire. Founded Gangaikonda Cholapuram. Built the massive Cholagangam irrigation tank.
Kulothunga Chola I
1070–1122 CE
Improved trade relations with Southeast Asia and China. Merged the Eastern Chalukya line with the Cholas.

Administration

  • Centralised monarchy with organised bureaucracy — ministers and officials under the king
  • Revenue based on detailed land surveys and land tax collection
  • Important ports: Nagapattinam, Poompuhar, Kaveripoompattinam

Village Self-Government (Revolutionary!)

Sabha — Assembly of Brahmin villages
Ur — Assembly of common villages

The Uttaramerur Inscription describes the Kudavolai system — member selection by lottery — with stated qualifications, disqualifications, committees, and tenure rules. This is among the world's earliest records of democratic local governance.

Trade & Economy

ExportsTextiles, Spices, Ivory, Gems
ImportsSilk, Horses, Luxury goods
Trade PartnersSE Asia, China, Middle East
IrrigationTanks & Canals

Temple Architecture (UNESCO)

  • Brihadeeswarar Temple, Thanjavur — built by Rajaraja I; masterpiece of Dravidian architecture
  • Gangaikondacholapuram Temple — built by Rajendra I
  • Airavatesvara Temple, Darasuram — famous for intricate sculptures
  • All three are UNESCO World Heritage "Great Living Chola Temples"

Art & Literature

  • Nataraja bronze sculpture — most iconic product of Chola bronze-casting
  • Kamba Ramayanam — Tamil retelling of the Ramayana
  • Periya Puranam — lives of the 63 Shaiva saints
  • Spread Indian culture, religion and architecture to Southeast Asia

Decline

Internal conflicts, succession disputes, and the rise of the Pandyas and Hoysalas weakened the empire. Malik Kafur's invasions in the early 14th century ended Chola power.

Vesara Style

The Chalukyas pioneered the Vesara style — a creative fusion of the northern Nagara (curvilinear shikhara) and southern Dravida (pyramidal vimana) temple styles, forming a uniquely Deccan architectural tradition.

Article 368 — Power to Amend

Article 368 grants Parliament the authority to amend the Constitution. However, Parliament cannot destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati case, 1973).

Step 1
Introduction of Bill — Can be introduced in Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha (not state legislatures).
Step 2
Parliamentary Approval — Must be passed by majority of total membership AND 2/3 of members present and voting in each House separately.
Step 3
State Ratification (if required) — If the amendment affects federal structure, state representation, or distribution of powers, at least half of state legislatures must ratify it.
Step 4
Presidential Assent — The President must give assent and cannot reject or return the bill (after the 24th Amendment).
Step 5
Enactment — The bill becomes part of the Constitution.

✅ Correct! 52nd Amendment (1985) introduced the Anti-Defection Law through the Tenth Schedule.
Q3. Which amendment reduced the voting age from 21 to 18 years?
✅ Correct! 61st Amendment (1989) reduced voting age to 18, enfranchising millions of young voters.

What is a State Legislature?

Every State in India has a Legislature — the law-making body at the state level. Just as the Union has Parliament (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha), States have their own equivalent. The structure is: Governor + Legislative Assembly (SLA), and in 6 states, additionally a Legislative Council (SLC). This dual-chamber setup mirrors the bicameral Parliament at the Centre.

Formula to Remember: State Legislature = G + A (+ C)
G = Governor  |  A = Assembly (SLA)  |  C = Council (SLC — only 6 states)

Article 168 — Composition

  • Bicameral: Governor + SLA + SLC → 6 states (UP, AP, Maharashtra, Bihar, Karnataka, Telangana)
  • Unicameral: Governor + SLA only → remaining 22 States + 3 UTs = 25 legislatures
  • Total: 6 bicameral + 25 unicameral = 31 State Legislatures

6 Bicameral States (Exam Must-Know)

Uttar PradeshSLA 403 / SLC 100
MaharashtraSLA 288 / SLC 78
BiharSLA 243 / SLC 75
KarnatakaSLA 224 / SLC 75
Andhra PradeshSLA 175 / SLC 90
TelanganaSLA 119 / SLC 40

Article 170 — Vidhan Sabha (SLA)

  • Maximum strength: 500 members
  • Minimum strength: 60 members (general rule)
  • Election: Direct election via Universal Adult Franchise
  • Constituency: Territorial, with uniform population-to-seat ratio
Exceptions to the 60-minimum rule:
Goa → 40  |  Mizoram → 40  |  Sikkim → 32  |  Puducherry → 30
(Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Goa minimum is 30; Nagaland → 46)

Article 169 — Creating / Abolishing SLC

  • Only Parliament can create or abolish a Legislative Council
  • Condition: SLA must pass a resolution by 2/3rd majority
  • Then Parliament passes the law by Simple Majority
  • Example: West Bengal SLC abolished → 1 Aug 1969
  • AP Assembly passed resolution to abolish SLC → 27 Jan 2020

Article 171 — Vidhan Parishad (SLC) Composition

  • Minimum: 40 members
  • Maximum: 1/3rd of SLA strength
  • It is a Permanent Body — cannot be dissolved; 1/3rd members retire every 2 years
SLC Composition Formula (5/6 elected + 1/6 nominated):
1/3 → elected by MLAs
1/3 → elected by Local Bodies
1/12 → elected by Teachers (≥3 yrs, secondary level)
1/12 → elected by Graduates (degree ≥3 yrs old)
1/6 → Nominated by Governor from fields: Art, Literature, Science, Social Service
(Mnemonic: CLASS — Cooperative/Art/Literature/Science/Social Service)

Election system for SLC: Proportional Representation via Single Transferable Vote (STV)

Vidhan Sabha vs Lok Sabha (Quick Parallel)

The Vidhan Sabha mirrors Lok Sabha at the state level — directly elected, 5-year term, First Past The Post system, minimum age 25. The Vidhan Parishad mirrors Rajya Sabha — permanent body, 6-year member term, age 30, STV election. Key difference: Rajya Sabha has real power over ordinary bills; the Vidhan Parishad is weaker and can only delay, not reject.

Sessions of State Legislature

  • Summoned by the Governor
  • Max gap between two sessions: 6 months
  • Minimum: 2 sessions per year; traditionally 3
  • Traditional 3 sessions: Budget (Feb–May) · Monsoon (Jul–Sep) · Winter (Nov–Dec)
  • Quorum: 10% of House strength
  • No Joint Sitting provision in state legislature (unlike Parliament)

Types of Bills

  • Ordinary Bill (Art 196) — Can start in either House; SLC can delay 3 months first time, 1 month second time
  • Money Bill (Art 198–199) — Only in SLA; SLC can delay max 14 days
  • Financial Bill (Art 207) — Governor's prior recommendation required

Governor Powers on Bills (Art 200–201 & 213)

  • Can return an Ordinary Bill once → must assent if passed again
  • Can reserve a Bill for Presidential consideration
  • Can keep assent pending indefinitely → Pocket Veto
  • Ordinance Power (Art 213) — issued during legislative recess; must be approved within 6 weeks of next session
  • Vacancy Rule (Art 190) — absence without permission for 60 days → seat declared vacant

Freshwater Sources

  • Precipitation (rainfall) — main source in India
  • Surface runoff (rivers, lakes, streams)
  • Groundwater (stored in aquifers)
  • Melting glaciers
  • Continuously renewed through the Hydrological Cycle — hence a renewable resource

India's River Systems

  • Himalayan Rivers — Perennial (year-round): Ganga, Indus, Brahmaputra
  • Deccan Rivers — Rain-fed (seasonal): Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery
  • Coastal Rivers — Short, small drainage
  • Inland Drainage — Do not reach the sea; e.g., Luni

Groundwater Facts — India

  • ~80% of domestic water needs met by groundwater
  • ~73% of groundwater recharge comes from monsoon
  • ~1/3 of all rainfall percolates to become groundwater
  • Over-exploited states: Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Delhi
Global Water Distribution:
Ice (glaciers) → 68.9% | Groundwater → 29.9% | Surface water → only ~0.3%

Types of Rock Formations (Groundwater Storage)

  • Soft/Porous Rock — High storage; found in Indo-Gangetic plains, coastal regions
      • Unconsolidated (sand, clay, gravel) — VERY HIGH storage
      • Semi-consolidated (sandstone, limestone) — moderate storage
  • Hard/Consolidated Rock — Low storage; water stored in cracks and fissures
      • Igneous & metamorphic rock, volcanic (Deccan Trap), carbonate rocks

What is Water Scarcity?

Water scarcity = shortage of usable, safe water. It occurs when demand exceeds supply OR when available water is unsafe due to pollution. It is NOT just about low rainfall — even high-rainfall areas face scarcity.

Exam Gold: Water scarcity ≠ lack of water. It = mismanagement + overuse + inequality.

Memory Trick (POP-UWI): Population growth · Overuse · Pollution · Urbanisation · Water mismanagement · Irrigation pressure

Causes in Detail

  • Population Growth — More people → more drinking water, more food production → overuse of water resources
  • Agriculture (BIGGEST USER) — Uses ~80% of water; expansion of irrigation causes groundwater depletion; tube wells and bore wells → falling water tables; solution: drought-resistant crops and dry farming techniques
  • Industrialisation — Industries use large volumes of water and pollute rivers; need electricity → hydroelectric projects → further water use
  • Urbanisation — High population density in cities; modern lifestyle → high water demand; groundwater pumping → depletion
  • Water Pollution — Domestic waste, industrial effluents, fertilisers and pesticides → water unsafe despite availability
  • Unequal Distribution — Inter-state disputes; some regions flush, others dry

MCQ Classification — Scarcity or Not?

High rainfall only❌ NOT scarce — adequate supply
High rainfall + large population✅ SCARCE — demand exceeds supply
High rainfall + highly polluted water✅ SCARCE — water unusable
Low rainfall + low population❌ NOT scarce — low demand balances low supply

Corrected False Statements (Exercise Type)

  • ❌ "Urban centres help proper utilisation" → ✅ Urban centres INCREASE pressure and lead to over-exploitation
  • ❌ "Damming rivers does not affect natural flow" → ✅ Damming AFFECTS natural flow and sediment movement, harming ecosystems
  • ❌ "Rainwater harvesting increased in Rajasthan due to Indira Gandhi Canal" → ✅ Rainwater harvesting DECLINED in Rajasthan due to availability of canal water

Interstate Water Disputes

Example: Krishna-Godavari Dispute — caused by unequal water sharing between states. Interstate water disputes arise because rivers cross state boundaries but water distribution is not always equitable.

What is a Multipurpose River Project?

A dam or river valley project designed to serve multiple purposes simultaneously — irrigation, power, flood control, water supply, navigation, fishing, and recreation.

Benefits of Dams

  • Irrigation — water for agriculture in dry areas
  • Hydroelectric power generation
  • Flood control — regulates river flow
  • Domestic and industrial water supply
  • Navigation and inland transport
  • Fish breeding and tourism

Problems of Dams (Critical for Exams)

  • Environmental — Disturbs natural river flow; sedimentation; blocks fish migration; submerges forests and soil
  • Agricultural — Shift to water-intensive crops; salinisation and land degradation
  • Flood Issues — Sedimentation reduces reservoir capacity; can increase floods instead of controlling them
  • Social — Displacement of communities; loss of livelihood
  • Other — Induced earthquakes; water-borne diseases; pollution

Perennial vs Rain-fed Rivers (Quick Fact)

A perennial river has water throughout the year (fed by glaciers + rain). A rain-fed river has water only during and after the monsoon. Groundwater in hard rock is found in joints, faults, fissures, and weathered zones.

Why Rainwater Harvesting?

Best low-cost, sustainable alternative to large dams. Reduces dependency on groundwater, prevents depletion, and ensures water availability during dry seasons.

Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting (Modern Method)

  • Rainwater collected via pipe from rooftop
  • Filtered through sand + bricks
  • Stored in tank/sump OR used to recharge groundwater
  • Important: First rain is NOT stored (cleanses the roof); subsequent rain is collected
  • Tamil Nadu made rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory

Tankas — Rajasthan Special

  • Underground tanks connected to rooftops via pipes
  • Store rainwater for long periods
  • Used for drinking during dry season
  • First rain discarded; later rain collected

Bamboo Drip Irrigation — Meghalaya

  • 200-year-old indigenous system
  • Uses bamboo pipes to channel water from streams
  • Controlled drip directly to plant roots
  • Highly efficient and eco-friendly

Success Stories

  • Tamil Nadu — Compulsory rooftop harvesting; first state to legislate this
  • Shillong, Meghalaya — High rainfall area but faces water shortage; rooftop harvesting provides 15–25% of water needs
  • Gendathur, Karnataka — 200 houses use rainwater harvesting; each house stores ~50,000 litres/year
Final Revision Trick: Scarcity = Demand > Supply OR Unusable water | Dams = Useful + Problematic | Rainwater Harvesting = Future Solution

Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM)

  • Piped drinking water to every rural household

Atal Bhujal Yojana

Why Conservation is Critical

Without water conservation: ❌ Water depletion → ❌ Food insecurity → ❌ Health hazards → ❌ Ecosystem collapse. The future depends on conservation, sustainable use, and smart management combining traditional knowledge with modern technology.

Why Does the Government Tax?

Taxation serves three core economic purposes. First, Resource Allocation — high taxes on certain industries push resources toward lower-taxed (often priority) sectors. Second, Income Distribution — progressive taxes reduce wealth inequality and promote social fairness. Third, Economic Stabilisation — during inflation, higher taxes reduce demand and cool prices; during recession, lower taxes stimulate spending.

Three Methods of Taxation

Progressive Tax — Rate increases as income rises. Example: India's Income Tax slabs. Pro-poor, most popular globally. Risk: discourages high earnings; promotes tax evasion.
Regressive Tax — Rate decreases as income/volume rises. Example: India once had regressive excise duty on small-scale industries to boost production. Criticised for burdening the poor more.
Proportional Tax — Fixed flat rate regardless of income level. Used as a transition mechanism — every progressive tax must eventually flatten into proportional, otherwise it would keep rising to infinity. Every regressive tax must also convert to proportional, else it hits zero. So proportional tax is the "stopper" at both ends.

What is a Direct Tax?

A Direct Tax has both its incidence (legal obligation to pay) and impact (actual burden) on the same person. Think of it as: the person who is hit is the same person who bleeds. These taxes cannot be transferred to someone else.

Simple test: If you earn more → you pay more, and you cannot pass that cost on to anyone else → it's a Direct Tax.

Types of Direct Tax

  • Income Tax — Levied on individual earnings above the minimum exemption limit. Assessment year = 1 Apr to 31 Mar. Governed by Income Tax Act, 1961. Must file ITR yearly.
  • Wealth Tax — Paid yearly on ownership of property (based on market value), regardless of whether the property generates income. Applies to individuals, HUFs, and corporates based on residential status.
  • Estate Tax (Inheritance Tax) — Paid on the value of the estate a person leaves behind after death.
  • Corporate Tax — Paid by domestic companies and foreign corporations earning income in India (from dividends, royalties, technical fees, asset sales, etc.).
  • Capital Gains Tax — Tax on income from sale of capital assets (land, bonds, shares, farms, art). Split into Short-Term (asset held ≤36 months) and Long-Term (held >36 months).

Advantages of Direct Tax

  • Economic & Social Balance — Slab system calibrated to income and age; exemptions reduce inequality
  • Productivity — Revenue grows automatically as the workforce and incomes grow
  • Curbs Inflation — Government raises taxes during inflation → reduces demand → prices stabilise
  • Certainty — Both payer and government know amounts in advance
  • Equal Distribution of Wealth — Higher earners pay more; surplus funds welfare programs

What is an Indirect Tax?

An Indirect Tax has its incidence and impact at different points. The person legally required to pay (producer/trader) passes the burden on to the final consumer. The person who is hit (producer) passes the bleeding on to someone else (consumer).

Example: Excise Duty is imposed on the manufacturer — but the manufacturer adds it to the price, so the customer ultimately pays it without realising it directly.

Types of Indirect Tax (Pre-GST Era)

  • Service Tax — Levied by service providers; collected and remitted to government
  • Excise Duty — On goods manufactured in India; paid by manufacturer, recovered from customers
  • VAT (Value Added Tax) — On movable goods sold directly to customers. Comprised Central Sales Tax (to Centre) + State VAT (to State)
  • Custom Duty — On goods imported into (and sometimes exported from) India
  • Stamp Duty — On transfer of immovable property and legal documents; collected by State Government
  • Entertainment Tax — State-level tax on movies, sports, video games, amusement parks, arcades, etc.
  • Securities Transaction Tax (STT) — Levied on trading of securities on Indian stock exchanges

Advantages of Indirect Tax

  • Convenience — Paid only at point of purchase; no separate filing burden on individuals
  • Ease of Collection — Collected at source (store/factory); simpler for authorities
  • Covers Everyone — Even those below the income tax threshold contribute through purchases
  • Equitable Structure — Necessities taxed at lower rates; luxury goods at higher rates

Three Components of GST

GST is not just one tax — it is split into three components depending on whether the transaction is within a state or between states:

CGST (Central GST) Intrastate transactions — revenue goes to Centre
SGST (State GST) Intrastate transactions — revenue goes to State
IGST (Integrated GST) Interstate transactions — collected by Centre, shared by destination state
Simple Rule: Selling goods within the same state? → CGST + SGST both apply (revenue split equally between Centre and State). Selling goods to another state? → IGST applies (Centre collects, destination state gets its share).

101st Constitutional Amendment

GST was enabled by the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016, which added Articles 246A, 269A, and 279A to the Constitution. Article 279A established the GST Council — a joint body of Centre and States that decides GST rates, exemptions, and rules.

Canons of Taxation — Adam Smith (1776)

Adam Smith's four classical canons define what a good tax system should look like. Modern economists added four more:

① Equality
Classical — Adam Smith
Tax burden must be distributed equitably — not equally. Rich should pay more because they have greater ability to pay. This is the basis for progressive taxation.
② Certainty
Classical — Adam Smith
Tax amount, time of payment, and manner must all be clear and known in advance — to both the taxpayer and the government. No room for arbitrariness.
③ Economy
Classical — Adam Smith
Cost of collecting a tax should be minimum. If more is spent collecting the tax than is collected, the tax is counterproductive. Avoid high administrative overhead.
④ Convenience
Classical — Adam Smith
Tax should be levied and collected in the most convenient manner for the taxpayer — right time, right method. "Ought to be levied at the time most convenient for the contributor."
⑤ Productivity
Modern Addition
The tax system should generate sufficient revenue — it should be productive enough to fund government expenditure without imposing excessive burden.
⑥ Elasticity
Modern Addition
Tax revenue should automatically expand or contract with economic conditions — rising in good times, falling during downturns, without requiring new legislation.
⑦ Simplicity
Modern Addition
The tax system should be simple enough for ordinary citizens to understand. Complexity breeds evasion and increases compliance cost.
⑧ Diversity
Modern Addition
Revenue should be raised from multiple sources — not relying solely on one tax. Diversified tax base reduces the risk of revenue collapse if one source dries up.

The Laffer Curve — Arthur Laffer

The Laffer Curve visualises the relationship between tax rates and total tax revenue. It is shaped like an inverted U (a hill), and its core insight is simple but powerful:

At 0% tax rate → Government collects ₹0 revenue (no tax = no revenue).
At 100% tax rate → Government also collects ≈₹0 revenue — because nobody would work if all earnings were seized.
Somewhere in between lies the optimal tax rate that maximises revenue.

The curve rises from 0% as taxes increase, reaches a peak (optimal point), then falls back as excessively high taxes kill incentives to work, invest, or do business — shrinking the very base from which taxes are collected.

India's Low Tax-to-GDP Ratio — Why It Matters

Causes of Low Tax-to-GDP:
• Low tax base — high exemption thresholds for personal income; complex corporate exemptions
• Tax evasion and corruption — undermines state legitimacy, reduces willingness to pay
• Weak administration — especially at sub-national level; lack of expertise and resources
• Structural issues — untaxed agricultural sector, large informal economy, cash-based transactions, low financial literacy
Consequences of Low Tax-to-GDP:
• Limited Fiscal Capacity — less money for government to spend on welfare and infrastructure
• Reduced Government Accountability — in high-tax societies, governments are more accountable to citizens

What Exactly is BOP? (Think of it like this)

Just like a company has a balance sheet showing what it earned and spent, a country has a BOP. It records every single rupee that comes into India and every single rupee that goes out — whether for buying/selling goods, paying for services, investing abroad, or receiving foreign aid. It is the most important economic statistic in an open economy because it shows exactly how India interacts with the rest of the world financially.

Credit side = Money COMING IN → exports, foreign investment inflows, remittances received.
Debit side = Money GOING OUT → imports, investment sent abroad, aid given.

Three Types of Items in BOP

  • Visible Items — Physical goods you can see and touch: cars, wheat, machines, phones exported or imported. This is "merchandise trade."
  • Invisible Items — Services that cross borders but have no physical form: IT services, banking, insurance, tourism, consulting. You can't see a software export at the port — but money flows in.
  • Capital Transfers — Movement of money for investment or loans: FDI, FPI, external borrowings, loan repayments, gold movement.

Why Does BOP Matter?

  • Tells the government which industries are growing in exports and deserve policy support
  • Guides import/export tax (tariff) decisions — raise taxes to discourage imports, lower to boost exports
  • If economy needs foreign help (technology, capital), BOP shows where the gaps are
  • Forms the basis for a country's entire monetary and fiscal policy in the external sector
  • Shows whether a country is living within its means internationally or accumulating debt

What is the Current Account?

The Current Account is the first and most important part of BOP. It captures all day-to-day international transactions — buying/selling goods, paying for services, receiving remittances from family abroad, getting foreign aid. Think of it as the "income and spending" account of a nation.

Formula: CA = GB + SB + NIR + IT
GB = Goods Balance  |  SB = Services Balance  |  NIR = Net Income Receipts  |  IT = International Transfers

Four Components of Current Account

① Goods Balance (Visible Trade)
Merchandise exports − imports
Physical goods crossing the border. Exports = Credit (+). Imports = Debit (−). If exports > imports → Trade Surplus. If imports > exports → Trade Deficit. India has had a consistent trade deficit (imports more goods than it exports).
② Services Balance (Invisible Trade)
Export of services − import of services
IT services, banking, insurance, legal, tourism, BPO, outsourcing — all services that earn or cost foreign exchange. India earns massively here (software exports). Exporting a service = Credit. Importing (paying for) a foreign service = Debit.
③ Net Income Receipts
Income from assets abroad − payments on foreign assets in India
If an Indian company owns assets (factories, bonds) abroad and earns income → Credit. If a foreign company owns assets in India and sends profits home → Debit. Net = receipts minus payments.
④ Unilateral / Transfer Payments
One-sided transactions — no return expected
Gifts, donations, personal remittances (NRI sending money home), foreign aid, NRI deposit withdrawals. "Unilateral" = one way — no goods or service is given in return. Inward transfers = Credit. Outward remittances = Debit.

Trade Balance = Goods + Services

The most quoted sub-component of BOP in the news is the Trade Balance (TB = GB + SB). It equals total exports of goods and services minus total imports of goods and services.

TB = (Goods exports + Services exports) − (Goods imports + Services imports)

Trade Surplus → TB is positive → We sell more to the world than we buy
Trade Deficit → TB is negative → We buy more from the world than we sell

What is the Capital Account?

The Capital Account records all international transactions involving the transfer of ownership of assets — money moving across borders for investment, loans, and financial flows. Unlike the Current Account which records income and spending, the Capital Account records changes in ownership of wealth.

Simple test: Does the transaction change who owns what (an asset, a company, a bond, land)? → Capital Account. Does the transaction involve earning or spending income? → Current Account.

What Goes Into the Capital Account?

  • Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) — Foreigners buying Indian companies, factories, machines = Credit. Indians investing in foreign companies = Debit.
  • Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI) — Foreigners buying Indian stocks, bonds, securities = Credit. Indians buying foreign securities = Debit.
  • Borrowing & Lending — India borrowing from abroad (private/government) = Credit (money comes in). India repaying foreign loans or lending abroad = Debit (money goes out).
  • Capital Transfers — Migrant transfers (ownership title changes when someone moves countries), debt forgiveness. Also includes transfer of intellectual property rights, franchises, natural resource ownership.
  • Reserve movements — Changes in RBI's foreign exchange reserves, gold movement, SDR allocations from IMF.

FDI vs FPI — The Most Important Comparison in Capital Account

Both FDI and FPI bring foreign money into India. The difference is in how they invest and what kind of relationship the investor wants with the business.

FDI in one line: A foreign company sets up a factory in India — they own it, manage it, and are here for the long term. (e.g., Samsung building a manufacturing plant in Noida)

FPI in one line: A foreign investor buys 100 shares of Infosys on BSE — they don't control anything, they just want returns and can sell tomorrow. (e.g., a US hedge fund buying Indian stocks)

Why FDI is Preferred Over FPI

Governments prefer FDI because it brings long-term commitment — factories don't leave overnight. FPI is called "Hot Money" because it can flow out suddenly during global uncertainty, crashing the stock market and currency in one go. India actively courts FDI through schemes like "Make in India" while regulating FPI limits to prevent excessive volatility.

What is Foreign Exchange Rate?

The exchange rate is the price of one country's currency expressed in terms of another country's currency. It tells you how much of one currency you need to buy one unit of another.

Example: ₹83 = $1 → The exchange rate of the Indian Rupee vs US Dollar is 83. This means you need 83 rupees to buy 1 dollar. The purpose is to compare the relative values of two currencies.

Two Types of Exchange Rate Systems

Fixed Exchange Rate
Also called: Pegged Exchange Rate
The government or central bank sets and maintains the exchange rate — it doesn't move with market forces. The weaker currency is "pegged" to a stronger one (e.g., US Dollar). Central bank intervenes: buys foreign currency when its price rises, sells when it falls, to keep the rate stable. Example: Saudi Riyal is pegged to the USD.
Flexible Exchange Rate
Also called: Floating Exchange Rate
Rate is determined entirely by market demand and supply — no government or central bank intervention. If demand for a currency rises, its value goes up. If supply exceeds demand, it falls. Most major economies including India use a managed float (partly flexible, with RBI occasionally intervening).

Both mean the same thing on the surface — the domestic currency loses value against foreign currencies. The entire difference is who causes it and which exchange rate system is in place.

What are NRI Deposits?

Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) are Indians living abroad. They are a massive source of foreign exchange for India through two channels: deposits (money kept in Indian bank accounts) and remittances (money sent home to family). India consistently ranks among the top recipients of remittances globally.

NRI deposits = Foreign currency deposited by NRIs in Indian banks. These are recorded in the Capital Account of BOP as they represent capital inflows.

Three Types of NRI Accounts

NRE Account
Non-Resident (External) Rupee Account · Since 1970
Money remitted from abroad to India through a foreign bank. Account maintained in Indian Rupees. Can be current, savings, or term deposit. Both principal and interest are fully repatriable (can be sent back abroad freely). Tax-free interest in India.
NRO Account
Non-Resident Ordinary Rupee Account
For NRIs who have income sources in India — rent, pension, dividends. Account maintained in Indian Rupees. Can be current, savings, recurring, or fixed deposit. Interest is taxable in India. Repatriation has limits (up to $1 million per year after taxes).
FCNR(B) Account
Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Bank) Account · Since 1 May 1993
Term deposits only — maintained in FOREIGN currencies: USD, Euro, GBP, JPY, AUD, CAD. The money does not convert to rupees — it stays in foreign currency. Protects NRI from rupee depreciation risk. Fully repatriable. Interest tax-free in India.

NRE = "External" = money from Outside → Rupees inside India, tax-free, fully repatriable
NRO = "Ordinary" = for India-sourced income → Rupees, taxable, limited repatriation
FCNR = "Foreign Currency" = stays in dollars/euros → no rupee risk, term deposits only

Economics
What are the 3 purposes of taxation?
1. Resource Allocation — redirect resources to priority sectors. 2. Income Distribution — reduce inequality via progressive rates. 3. Stabilisation — control inflation and unemployment through fiscal policy.
Economics
What is the key difference between Direct and Indirect Tax?
Direct Tax: incidence and impact on the SAME person — cannot be transferred (e.g., Income Tax). Indirect Tax: incidence on one person (producer), impact shifted to another (consumer) — e.g., GST, Excise Duty.
Economics
What shape is the Laffer Curve and what does it show?
Inverted U (hill-shaped). Shows relationship between tax rate and revenue. At 0% and 100% tax rates, revenue = ~zero. Optimal tax rate sits at the peak of the curve — maximising revenue without killing economic activity.
Economics
Name Adam Smith's 4 classical Canons of Taxation.
Mnemonic: ECCE — Equality (pay per ability), Certainty (amounts known in advance), Convenience (easy to pay), Economy (low collection cost). Modern additions: Productivity, Elasticity, Simplicity, Diversity.
Economics
What are the 3 components of GST and when do they apply?
CGST (Central) + SGST (State) → both apply on intrastate sales, revenue split equally. IGST (Integrated) → applies on interstate sales, collected by Centre and shared with destination state. Enabled by 101st Constitutional Amendment, 2016.
Polity
What is the composition formula for the Vidhan Parishad (SLC)?
1/3 by MLAs · 1/3 by Local Bodies · 1/12 by Teachers · 1/12 by Graduates · 1/6 nominated by Governor (from Art, Literature, Science, Social Service — mnemonic: CLASS). Total: 5/6 elected + 1/6 nominated.
Polity
What happens if an MLA is absent without permission for 60 days?
Article 190 — the seat is declared VACANT. The 60-day clock starts from the first day of absence without leave granted by the House.
Economics · BOP
What is the difference between BOP and BOT in one sentence each?
BOT = Exports − Imports of goods only. BOP = ALL international transactions — goods + services + income + transfers + capital flows. BOT is a part of BOP. BOP is broader.
Economics · BOP
What are the 4 components of the Current Account? (Formula)
CA = GB + SB + NIR + IT → Goods Balance (visible) + Services Balance (invisible) + Net Income Receipts + International Transfers (remittances, aid). Trade Balance = GB + SB.
Economics · BOP
What is the difference between FDI and FPI?
FDI = long-term, active, physical assets, high control, stable, brings technology. FPI = short-term, passive, financial assets (stocks/bonds), no control, volatile "Hot Money." Both go in Capital Account — FDI as Credit when foreigners invest in India.
Economics · BOP
Devaluation vs Depreciation — what's the ONE key difference?
Same result (currency loses value) — different cause. Devaluation = GOVERNMENT does it deliberately in a FIXED exchange rate system. Depreciation = MARKET forces do it automatically in a FLOATING system. Opposite of devaluation = Revaluation. Opposite of depreciation = Appreciation.
Economics · BOP
Name the 3 NRI accounts and their key difference.
NRE = money from abroad, in Rupees, tax-free, fully repatriable. NRO = India-sourced income, in Rupees, taxable, limited repatriation. FCNR(B) = foreign currency deposits (USD/EUR/GBP), term deposits only, no rupee risk, fully repatriable. Introduced: NRE 1970, FCNR(B) 1993.
Economics · BOP
What is "Invisible Trade" and give 3 examples?
Invisible trade = export/import of SERVICES — nothing physically crosses the border but money flows. Examples: IT/software exports (India's biggest), banking and insurance services, tourism, BPO/outsourcing, legal and consulting services. Counted in Services Balance of Current Account.
