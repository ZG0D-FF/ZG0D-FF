Information Theory (Slides 1–22) — Lucidified Notes

Core Idea of the whole chapter:
Information is simply a way of measuring how much uncertainty gets reduced when something happens.

Slide 1 — What is this chapter about?

This chapter has four connected ideas. Think of it as climbing a staircase:

  1. Uncertainty and Information: Why some events feel "more informative" than others.
  2. Self Information: Information contained in a single event.
  3. Mutual Information: How much one event tells us about another.
  4. Average Mutual Information: Average information shared between two random variables.

Slides 2–4 — Why does uncertainty become information?

The slides compare three sentences:

Statement Probability Information
Sun rises in EastAlmost 1Almost none
Phone rings next hourMediumMedium
Snow in DelhiVery lowVery high

Why? Imagine someone tells you: "The sun will rise tomorrow." Your reaction? "Obviously." Nothing new.
Now someone says, "It will snow in Delhi." Now you're surprised. That surprise is exactly what these slides call information.

Golden Rule: Less probable event → More information.
Why? Because before the event happened, there was much more uncertainty.

Slide 4 — Self Information

Now the slides convert intuition into mathematics. For an event X = xi, self-information is:

I(xi) = log( 1 / P(xi) ) = -log P(xi)

This formula measures the information contained in one specific event.

Break the formula:

  • Suppose P = 0.5. Then I = -log2(0.5) = 1 bit
  • Suppose P = 0.25. Then I = -log2(0.25) = 2 bits

Notice something beautiful:

ProbabilitySelf Information

Every time probability halves, information increases by one bit.

Why "bit"? The slide says: Base 2 → bits, Base e → nats. For exams, unless stated otherwise, use base 2.

Slides 5–7 — Fair Coin Examples

Example 1 — A fair coin

P(H) = P(T) = 0.5
Information of Head: I = -log2(0.5) = 1 bit. Same for Tail.

Why exactly one bit? Because only two possibilities exist. One binary digit is enough (Head = 1, Tail = 0).

Example 2 — Memoryless Source

The slide introduces an important word: Memoryless. Each toss does not remember previous tosses. Every toss is independent.

Suppose we observe 3 tosses. There are 23 = 8 possible sequences (HHH, HHT, etc.).
Each has probability 1/8.
Information becomes I = -log2(1/8) = 3 bits.
Notice: 3 tosses require 3 bits. Exactly matching intuition.

Example 3 — Two Independent Sources

Source A gives one bit. Source B gives one bit. Together they form Source C.
Since they're independent, P(C) = P(A) × P(B) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.
Information becomes I(C) = -log2(0.25) = 2 bits.

Big observation: Two independent 1-bit sources become 2 bits. Nothing mysterious happened. Information simply added.

Slide 8 — Why do we use logarithm?

This slide answers a famous question. Without logarithm, independent probabilities multiply (0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25). But information should add (1 + 1 = 2).

The logarithm converts multiplication into addition:

log(ab) = log a + log b
This is exactly why Shannon chose logarithms.

Memory Trick: Probability multiplies. Log converts multiplication into addition. Therefore information adds. This is one of the most important ideas in the chapter.

Slides 9–13 — Mutual Information

Until now, we measured information inside one event. Now we ask a new question:
If I observe Y, how much does it tell me about X?
This is Mutual Information.

Example intuition: Suppose X = What was transmitted, and Y = What was received. After seeing Y, can we guess X better? That improvement is mutual information.

Two extreme cases:

  • Case 1 — Independent: If X and Y are unrelated, observing Y changes nothing. Mutual information becomes I(X;Y) = 0.
  • Case 2 — Completely dependent: If Y completely reveals X, then observing Y removes all uncertainty. Mutual information becomes maximum.

Slide 11 — Formula

I(xi; yj) = log( P(xi | yj) / P(xi) )

Read it in words: Information equals "New probability after observing Y" divided by "Old probability before observing Y."
Interpretation: If observing Y makes X much more likely, the ratio becomes large. Large ratio → Large information.

Slide 12 — Symmetry

The slide proves I(xi; yj) = I(yj; xi). Meaning information shared between X and Y is identical in either direction. (If knowing your roll number identifies your exam paper, then your exam paper also identifies your roll number).

Slide 13 — Physical Meaning

  • Independent: P(x|y) = P(x). Therefore I = 0. No information gained.
  • Perfect dependence: If P(x|y) = 1, then I = log(1 / P(x)).
    Wait. That's exactly the self-information formula. Meaning: Perfect observation gives all the information that event originally contained.

Slides 14–18 — Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC)

This is the first communication example. The channel behaves like this:

  • If we send 0: Receive 0 with probability 1 - p. Receive 1 with probability p.
  • If we send 1: Receive 1 with probability 1 - p. Receive 0 with probability p.

Notice: Both directions have the same error probability. That's why it's called Symmetric.

Output probabilities: The slide calculates P(Y=0) = 0.5 and P(Y=1) = 0.5. So even after passing through the channel, outputs remain equally likely.

Mutual Information inside BSC: The slide gives I(0;0) = log2(2(1 - p)).

  • Case A — Perfect channel (p = 0): I = log2(2) = 1 bit. Everything received is correct. One received bit completely reveals one transmitted bit.
  • Case B — Completely noisy channel (p = 0.5): I = log2(1) = 0. Receiving a bit tells us nothing. (Tossing a fair coin would work equally well.)

How does the graph behave? As p increases, mutual information decreases. More errors → Less useful information.

Slides 19–20 — General Binary Channel

Now the slide removes symmetry. Instead of one error probability, there are two (p0 and p1 can be different).
The slide substitutes them into:

I(0;0) = log2( 2(1 - p0) / (1 - p0 + p1) )

Notice the pattern. It's still Conditional probability divided by ordinary probability. Only the probabilities became more complicated.

Slide 21 — Average Mutual Information

Until now, we looked at one pair of events (e.g., "0 was sent and 0 was received"). But many different pairs can occur. So we average them. The definition is:

I(X;Y) = ∑i ∑j P(xi, yj) log( P(xi, yj) / (P(xi)P(yj)) )

Understand this slowly: For every possible pair (xi, yj), find its probability, find its mutual information, multiply them, and add everything. It's simply a weighted average.

Important property: I(X;Y) ≥ 0. It becomes zero only when X and Y are independent. Meaning average shared information can never be negative.

The 5 Golden Intuitions to Remember:

  1. Less probability = More information.
  2. Self-information measures one event's surprise.
  3. Independent sources add their information because logarithms turn multiplication into addition.
  4. Mutual information measures how much observing Y reduces uncertainty about X.
  5. Average mutual information is never negative and becomes zero only when X and Y are independent.
