2.2 Aspects of MOS Transistor — Threshold Voltage \(V_T\)

1. What is happening inside the MOS gate structure?

The gate structure of a MOS transistor is not electrically "empty." There are charges stored:

  • in the dielectric/oxide layers,
  • at the Si–SiO₂ interface,
  • and in the semiconductor substrate itself.

These stored charges affect how much gate voltage is needed to make the transistor conduct.

Threshold voltage \(V_T\) is the gate voltage required to produce the necessary inversion condition so that the MOS transistor changes its state.

2. Enhancement-mode MOS transistor

An enhancement-mode transistor is normally OFF. To turn it ON:

  1. Apply a sufficiently large gate voltage.
  2. This gate voltage counteracts/neutralizes the charges already present in the gate structure.
  3. The electric field produced by the gate causes the silicon surface beneath the oxide to undergo inversion.
  4. An inversion channel is formed.
  5. The transistor can conduct.
Enhancement MOS: OFF → apply enough gate voltage → inversion occurs → ON

The required gate voltage is related to the threshold voltage \(V_T\).

3. Depletion-mode nMOS transistor

A depletion-mode nMOS transistor is normally ON because an n-type implanted region already provides the conducting path. To turn it OFF:

  1. Apply a suitable gate voltage.
  2. The applied voltage adds to the stored charge.
  3. The existing n-type implant region becomes inverted toward p-type.
  4. The conducting carriers are depleted.
  5. The transistor turns OFF.
Depletion nMOS: ON → apply sufficient gate voltage → n-region is inverted toward p → OFF

This is why the threshold voltage of a depletion-mode nMOS is negative.

4. What determines threshold voltage?

The text expresses \(V_T\) in terms of several physical quantities:

\(Q_D\)

Charge per unit area in the depletion layer beneath the oxide.

\(Q_{SS}\)

Charge density at the Si–SiO₂ interface.

\(C_O\)

Capacitance per unit area of the gate.

\(\Phi_{MS}\)

Work-function difference between the gate and silicon.

\(\Phi_{IN}\)

Fermi-level potential between the inverted surface and the bulk silicon.

So conceptually, threshold voltage is determined by a balance between the electrical charges and potentials associated with the gate, oxide/interface, depletion region, and semiconductor.

5. Important point for polysilicon gate

For a polysilicon gate and silicon substrate, the text says that:

\[ \Phi_{MS} \]

is negative but negligible. Therefore, the magnitude and sign of \(V_T\) are mainly determined by the balance involving the remaining terms.

The text specifically points out the negative term:

\[ -Q_{SS} \]

and two positive terms. Therefore, the threshold voltage depends strongly on the balance of these physical contributions.

6. Interface charge \(Q_{SS}\)

The text gives:

\[ Q_{SS}=(1.5\text{ to }8)\times10^{-5}\; \text{C/m}^2 \]

The exact value depends on the crystal orientation. This tells you that the Si–SiO₂ interface is physically important: imperfections/charges at this interface affect the threshold voltage.

7. Substrate bias and body effect

Now the text introduces another important factor: the substrate does not necessarily have to be at the same voltage as the source. This produces the body effect.

The substrate-to-source voltage is represented by:

\[ V_{SB} \]

The text defines the sign convention as:

  • for nMOS, substrate is negative with respect to source;
  • for pMOS, substrate is positive with respect to source.

8. Why does increasing \(V_{SB}\) increase \(V_T\)?

This is the central idea of the body effect section. When \(V_{SB}\) is increased:

substrate bias increases → channel is depleted of charge carriers → more gate voltage is required to achieve inversion → threshold voltage increases
Increasing \(V_{SB}\) raises the threshold voltage.

The change in threshold voltage is given in the text as proportional to:

\[ \Delta V_T \propto \sqrt{V_{SB}} \]

The proportionality constant is represented by \(\gamma\), the body-effect coefficient.

\(\gamma\) depends on substrate doping.

The text says that a more lightly doped substrate produces a smaller body effect.

9. Threshold voltage with substrate bias

Instead of only talking about the change in threshold voltage, the text gives the alternative form:

\[ V_T = V_T(0) + \text{body-effect term} \]

where \(V_T(0)\) means the threshold voltage when \(V_{SB}=0\). So \(V_T(0)\) is simply the zero-substrate-bias threshold voltage.

The key distinction is:

  • \(V_T(0)\): threshold voltage with no substrate-to-source bias.
  • \(V_T\): threshold voltage when substrate bias is present.

10. Typical threshold voltages

nMOS enhancement-mode

When \(V_{SB}=0\), the threshold voltage is approximately:

\[ V_T=0.2V_{DD} \]

For \(V_{DD}=5V\), this gives \(V_T=+1V\).

When \(V_{SB}=5V\), the threshold rises to approximately:

\[ V_T=0.3V_{DD} \]

Therefore, for \(V_{DD}=5V\): \(V_T=+1.5V\).

Meaning: Applying substrate bias increased the threshold from approximately 1 V to 1.5 V.

nMOS depletion-mode

For \(V_{SB}=0\), the text gives:

\[ V_{TD}=-0.7V_{DD} \]

For \(V_{DD}=5V\): \(V_{TD}=-3.5V\).

With \(V_{SB}=5V\), the threshold becomes approximately:

\[ V_{TD}=-0.6V_{DD} \]

giving \(V_{TD}=-3.0V\).

Important observation: The threshold becomes less negative: \(-3.5V\rightarrow-3.0V\). So the body effect is again shifting the threshold in the direction that makes the transistor harder to turn on.

2.3 MOS Transistor Transconductance \(g_m\) and Output Conductance \(g_{ds}\)

Now the text moves from threshold voltage to two important small-signal/device parameters: transconductance \(g_m\) and output conductance \(g_{ds}\). These describe how transistor current responds to voltage changes.

11. Transconductance \(g_m\)

The text defines transconductance as the relationship between output current \(I_{DS}\) and input voltage \(V_{GS}\).

\(g_m\) tells us how strongly the drain current changes when the gate-source voltage changes.

So a larger \(g_m\) means: small change in gate voltage → larger change in drain current. That makes \(g_m\) an important measure of the transistor's control capability.

12. Expression for \(g_m\)

The text eventually obtains, in saturation:

\[ g_m=\frac{2I_{DS}}{V_{GS}-V_T} \]

and alternatively:

\[ g_m=\frac{W}{L}\mu C_O(V_{GS}-V_T) \]

The important dependencies are:

Increasing \(W\)

\[ W\uparrow \Rightarrow g_m\uparrow \] — making the transistor wider increases transconductance.

Increasing effective gate voltage

The quantity \(V_{GS}-V_T\) is the gate voltage above threshold. Therefore \(V_{GS}-V_T\uparrow \Rightarrow g_m\uparrow\).

Increasing mobility

\[ \mu\uparrow \Rightarrow g_m\uparrow \]

Increasing \(C_O\)

\[ C_O\uparrow \Rightarrow g_m\uparrow \]

Increasing channel length

Because \(g_m\) contains \(\frac{W}{L}\), increasing \(L\) tends to reduce \(g_m\), assuming the other quantities remain fixed.

13. Why not simply make the transistor wider?

Increasing transistor width: \(W\uparrow \Rightarrow g_m\uparrow\), which is good for performance. But simultaneously:

\[ W\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{input capacitance}\uparrow \]

\[ W\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{area}\uparrow \]

So increasing width gives better transconductance, but costs more capacitance and chip area. This is an important design trade-off.

14. What happens when channel length is reduced?

The text says that reducing channel length results in an increase in \(g_m\), owing to the relevant dependence on transistor dimensions.

\[ L\downarrow \Rightarrow g_m\uparrow \]

This sounds entirely beneficial, but there is a major disadvantage. The gain of the MOS device decreases because its output resistance becomes degraded. The text relates output resistance to:

\[ r_o=\frac{1}{g_{ds}} \]

Therefore \(g_{ds}\uparrow \Rightarrow r_o\downarrow\), and lower output resistance means lower voltage gain. So short channel length gives a useful increase in \(g_m\), but causes poorer output resistance and hence poorer gain.

15. Output conductance \(g_{ds}\)

Output conductance describes how the drain current changes with drain-source voltage:

\[ g_{ds}=\frac{\partial I_{DS}}{\partial V_{DS}} \]

It also shows that \(g_{ds}\) has a strong dependence on channel length.

Shorter channel length strongly affects output conductance.

And because \(r_o=\frac{1}{g_{ds}}\), a larger \(g_{ds}\) means a smaller output resistance.

2.4 MOS Transistor Figure of Merit — \(\omega_0\)

The section introduces a parameter \(\omega_0\) as an indication of the frequency response of a MOS transistor.

The important relationship is essentially:

\[ \omega_0=\frac{g_m}{C_g} \]

and the text gives its dependence as

\[ \omega_0 \propto \frac{\mu_n(V_{GS}-V_T)}{L^2} \]

where:

  • \(g_m\) = transconductance of the transistor
  • \(C_g\) = gate capacitance
  • \(V_{GS}-V_T\) = gate voltage above threshold
  • \(\mu_n\) = carrier mobility
  • \(L\) = channel length

What this tells us

The switching speed becomes higher when:

  • \(V_{GS}-V_T\) increases → more gate voltage above threshold gives faster operation.
  • Carrier mobility increases → carriers can move more easily, improving speed.
  • Channel length \(L\) decreases → speed increases strongly because it varies approximately as \(1/L^2\).

So the central conclusion is:

A fast MOS circuit requires a high \(g_m\).

Surface mobility

The text now discusses why nMOS can be made particularly fast.

Electron mobility in an inversion layer on a (100)-oriented n-type inversion surface is larger than the electron mobility on a (111)-oriented surface.

It also says that this electron mobility is about three times the hole mobility on a (111)-oriented p-type inversion layer.

Therefore, electron mobility is especially favorable for nMOS speed.

However, surface mobility is not constant. It also depends on the effective gate voltage:

\[ V_{GS}-V_T \]

So changing the amount by which the gate voltage exceeds threshold also affects surface mobility.

Choice for faster nMOS circuits

For faster nMOS circuits, the text chooses a:

(100)-oriented p-type substrate.

Why?

Because the inversion layer formed in this structure has a surface electron mobility of approximately

\[ \mu_n \approx 650\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s} \]

at room temperature.

The text then compares this with typical bulk mobilities:

\[ \mu_n =1250\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s} \]

\[ \mu_p =480\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s} \]

The surface mobility is therefore considerably lower than the corresponding bulk mobility.

The text summarizes this approximately as:

\[ \mu_s \approx 0.5\mu \]

where:

  • \(\mu_s\) = surface mobility
  • \(\mu\) = bulk mobility

Main takeaway from 2.4

For MOS switching speed:

higher \(g_m\) → higher speed

and speed improves with:

higher excess gate voltage, higher carrier mobility, and shorter channel length.

For nMOS, a (100)-oriented p-type substrate is preferred because it provides relatively high electron surface mobility.

2.5 The Pass Transistor

Unlike a bipolar transistor, the MOS transistor has an isolated gate.

This makes it possible to use a MOS transistor as a switch in series with a line carrying a logic level.

The book compares this idea to relay contacts.

This application is called a:

Pass transistor

A pass transistor can therefore be used to control whether a logic signal is allowed to pass from one point to another.

The text says that switching logic arrays can be constructed using pass transistors. Figure 2.4 shows an example of an AND array.

The important idea from the figure is that the transistors controlled by \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) determine whether the signal can reach \(X\).

The required logical relationship is:

\[ X=A\cdot B\cdot C \]

with Logic 1 represented by the appropriate logic-high voltage range shown in the book.

Important note from the text

There must also be a mechanism that makes \(X\) assume ground potential when

\[ A+B+C=0 \]

In other words, when the required conducting path is absent, \(X\) must not simply be left floating; it needs a means of being brought to ground.

2.6 The nMOS Inverter

A complete logic system needs an inverter.

An inverter is important because it is required for:

  • restoring logic levels,
  • constructing NAND gates,
  • constructing NOR gates,
  • sequential circuits,
  • memory circuits.

The basic idea of an inverter is simple:

input LOW → output HIGH
input HIGH → output LOW

Basic nMOS inverter arrangement

The basic inverter requires:

  • a transistor whose source is connected to ground,
  • some form of load resistance connected between the drain and the positive supply \(V_{DD}\),
  • the output is taken from the drain,
  • the input is applied to the gate with respect to ground.

So the transistor acts as the pull-down element while the load provides the pull-up action.

Why not simply use a resistor?

The book points out an important IC fabrication problem:

resistors are not convenient to produce on a silicon substrate.

Even a moderately sized resistor can occupy a very large area.

Since VLSI design strongly values compactness, using an ordinary resistor as the load is undesirable.

Solution

Use a depletion-mode MOS transistor as the load.

This gives the nMOS inverter shown in Figure 2.5.

There are now two transistors:

Depletion-mode transistor

This acts as the:

pull-up (p.u.) transistor

Enhancement-mode transistor

This acts as the:

pull-down (p.d.) transistor

How the nMOS inverter works

There is an important condition:

No current is drawn from the output.

Under this condition, the current through both transistors must be equal:

\[ I_{DS(p.u.)}=I_{DS(p.d.)} \]

because they form the same current path.

Pull-up transistor

For the depletion-mode transistor, the gate is connected to its source.

Therefore,

\[ V_{GS}=0 \]

under all conditions.

Consequently, only the depletion transistor's characteristic corresponding to

\[ V_{GS}=0 \]

needs to be considered.

This is why the depletion-mode device provides a convenient load.

Obtaining the inverter transfer characteristic

To obtain the inverter's voltage-transfer characteristic, the book uses the transistor characteristic curves.

We take:

  • the \(V_{GS}=0\) characteristic of the depletion-mode pull-up transistor,
  • the family of characteristics of the enhancement-mode pull-down transistor.

These are then considered together.

The key voltage relationship is:

\[ V_{DS(enh)}=V_{DD}-V_{DS(dep)} \]

because the total voltage across the two series-connected devices must equal \(V_{DD}\).

Also,

\[ V_{GS(enh)}=V_{in} \]

because the input is applied directly to the gate of the enhancement-mode pull-down transistor.

The intersection points of the two sets of characteristics provide the operating points of the inverter.

Joining these operating points gives the voltage-transfer characteristic shown in Figure 2.7.

What happens as \(V_{in}\) increases?

This is the most important physical explanation of the inverter characteristic.

Initially: \(V_{in}\) is low

The enhancement-mode pull-down transistor has:

\[ V_{GS}

so it is essentially OFF.

Therefore, very little current flows through it, and the output remains near the positive supply:

\[ V_{out}\approx V_{DD} \]

So:

low input → high output

When \(V_{in}\) exceeds threshold

Once

\[ V_{in}=V_{GS(p.d.)}>V_T \]

the enhancement-mode pull-down transistor begins to conduct.

Current now flows.

As the pull-down transistor conducts more strongly, the output voltage \(V_{out}\) begins to decrease.

As \(V_{in}\) increases further

The pull-down transistor eventually moves out of its saturation region and becomes resistive.

At the same time, the pull-up transistor is initially resistive as the pull-down transistor turns on.

Thus the inverter moves through its transition region, where the output changes rapidly as the input changes.

Gain of the inverter

During this transition, the slope of the transfer characteristic determines the gain.

The book gives:

\[ G=-\frac{\Delta V_{out}}{\Delta V_{in}} \]

or, in differential form,

\[ G=-\frac{dV_{out}}{dV_{in}} \]

The negative sign is important because an inverter produces an opposite change:

  • input increases,
  • output decreases.

Therefore, the inverter has negative voltage gain.

The magnitude of the slope tells us how strongly the output responds to a change in input.

\(V_{inv}\)

The point where

\[ V_{out}=V_{in} \]

is called:

\[ V_{inv} \]

This is the inverter threshold point.

The book notes that both the transfer characteristic and \(V_{inv}\) can be shifted by changing the relative strength of the pull-up and pull-down devices.

This relative strength is represented by:

\[ \frac{Z_{p.u.}}{Z_{p.d.}} \]

where \(Z\) is determined by the transistor's length-to-width ratio.

So changing the transistor dimensions changes the relative pull-up/pull-down strength and consequently changes the inverter's transfer characteristic.

2.7 Determination of Pull-up to Pull-down Ratio

\[ \frac{Z_{p.u.}}{Z_{p.d.}} \]

for an nMOS inverter driven by another nMOS inverter.

Now the book considers two identical nMOS inverters cascaded together, where the output of the first inverter directly drives the second inverter.

The objective is to choose the pull-up/pull-down ratio so that cascading inverters does not degrade the logic levels.

Condition for equal noise margins

The book chooses the inverter switching point to be:

\[ V_{inv}=0.5V_{DD} \]

This corresponds to equal margins around the inverter threshold.

At this point:

  • the depletion-mode pull-up transistor is in saturation,
  • the enhancement-mode pull-down transistor is also in saturation.

Therefore, the saturation-current equations can be equated.

Depletion-mode pull-up current

For the depletion transistor, because its gate is connected to its source:

\[ V_{GS}=0 \]

Its saturation current is written in the form:

\[ I_{DS}=K\frac{W}{L}\frac{(V_{GS}-V_{td})^2}{2} \]

Here \(V_{td}\) is the threshold voltage of the depletion device.

Enhancement-mode pull-down current

For the enhancement transistor:

\[ V_{GS}=V_{inv} \]

and therefore its saturation current is:

\[ I_{DS}=K\frac{W}{L}\frac{(V_{inv}-V_t)^2}{2} \]

Since the two transistors are in series, their currents are equal.

Therefore, the two expressions can be equated.

Introducing \(Z\)

The book defines the transistor geometry parameter as:

\[ Z=\frac{W}{L} \]

So:

\[ Z_{p.d.}=\frac{W_{p.d.}}{L_{p.d.}} \]

and

\[ Z_{p.u.}=\frac{W_{p.u.}}{L_{p.u.}} \]

Thus, the ratio

\[ \frac{Z_{p.u.}}{Z_{p.d.}} \]

represents the relative geometrical strength of the pull-up and pull-down transistors.

Substituting the typical values

The book uses:

\[ V_t=0.2V_{DD} \]

\[ V_{td}=-0.6V_{DD} \]

and, for equal margins,

\[ V_{inv}=0.5V_{DD} \]

After substituting these values into the derived equation, the book obtains:

\[ 0.5=0.2+\frac{0.6}{\sqrt{Z_{p.u.}/Z_{p.d.}}} \]

Solving gives:

\[ \frac{Z_{p.u.}}{Z_{p.d.}}=\frac{4}{1} \]

So the required ratio is:

Pull-up \(Z\) : Pull-down \(Z\) = 4 : 1

for an nMOS inverter that is directly driven by another nMOS inverter, under the assumptions made in the text.

The speed of a MOS transistor is related to its frequency response. High \(g_m\), high mobility, and short channel length improve speed.

A MOS transistor's isolated gate allows it to work as a switch, passing logic signals through a controlled path.

Use a depletion transistor as pull-up and an enhancement transistor as pull-down. Increasing \(V_{in}\) turns the pull-down on, causing \(V_{out}\) to fall.

For equal inverter margins,

\[ V_{inv}=0.5V_{DD} \]

and, using the stated threshold values,

\[ Z_{p.u.}:Z_{p.d.}=4:1 \]

That ratio is chosen so that cascaded nMOS inverters can maintain appropriate logic levels without degradation.
